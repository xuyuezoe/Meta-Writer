# Sudden Cardiac Death in Young Athletes: JACC State-of-the-Art Review.

## Abstract

Athletes epitomize the healthiest segment of society. Despite this premise, sudden cardiac death may occur in apparently healthy athletes, attracting significant attention not only in the medical community but also in laypersons and media. The incidence of sudden cardiac death is variably reported, and epidemiological burden differs among cohorts. Athletes appear to be at risk of developing fatal arrhythmias when harboring a quiescent cardiac disorder. Primary cardiomyopathies, ion channelopathies, and coronary artery anomalies are prevalent causes in young individuals. Cardiac assessment of athletes can be challenging because these individuals exhibit a plethora of electrical, structural, and functional physiological changes that overlap with cardiac pathology. A diagnosis of cardiac disease in a young athlete is not necessarily an indication to terminate competition and sports participation. International guidelines, traditionally focused on disqualification of individuals with cardiac disease, have recently adopted a more liberal attitude, based on a careful assessment of the risk and on a shared-decision making approach.

Expert assessment and shared decision making should guide exercise prescription and engagement even in high-intensity sports. Sudden cardiac death (SCD) may occur in young and apparently healthy individuals, including athletes.1-3 The benefits of exercise on reducing the burden of atherosclerotic coronary artery disease are irrefutable, and athletes often represent the healthiest segment of society.4 Despite these premises, an abnormal structural or electrical cardiac substrate may promote fatal arrhythmias during adrenergic stimuli, especially in individuals who exercise vigorously. Inherited cardiac conditions are the predominant cause of SCD in young ( 35 years) athletes compared with younger counterparts. According to a Danish national study,13 the estimated incidence of SCD was 0.47 to 1.21 per 100,000 person-years in young (age ≤35 years) competitive athletes compared with 6.64 per 100,000 person-years in older (age >35 years) athletes. Athletes of African or Afro-Caribbean origin often excel in sport competitions, especially in certain disciplines such as basketball, soccer, and marathon running. A recent prospective study, based on the National Centre for Catastrophic Sports Injury Research registry in the United States, reported on 331 confirmed cases of SCA and SCD (158 survivors, 173 fatalities); the annual incidence rate among African American male NCAA Division I basketball players was 1:2,087 athlete-years and was the highest compared with other ethnic groups.14 For many years, competitive sport has been dominated by men, but the last 2 decades have witnessed an increasing number of women participating in high-level competition. A pooled analysis of 3 large databases showed that cardiac arrest during sport appears to be >10-fold lower in women compared with men, despite similar subject characteristics, including age, history of cardiac disease, and location of occurrence.15 Variables implicated in this difference may include the effect of sex on physiological cardiac adaptation to exercise and chamber remodeling, the higher prevalence of myocardial fibrosis in men, and hormonal factors.16,17 Etiology SCD in young athletes is caused by a diverse spectrum of cardiac diseases; however, inherited cardiac conditions are predominant in this age group. Cardiac autopsy may elucidate structural abnormalities, but the heart may be structurally normal in this setting, indicating sudden arrhythmic death syndrome, where a primary arrhythmia syndrome is the most likely underlying etiology18,19 (Figure 1). HCM has been reported to be the cause of SCD in 2% to 36% of athletes,1,2,25 and some studies from the United States indicate that it is the predominant cause of SCD in young athletes.2 Prevention of SCD relies on the use of implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) in primary and secondary prevention, after accurate prognostic stratification. From a pathology standpoint, the diagnosis of HCM is made by the detection of increased heart weight and/or asymmetric LVH on macroscopic exam and by the presence of widespread myocyte disarray at histology.26 Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is an inherited condition that is characterized by progressive fibrofatty replacement of the myocardium and a predilection for fatal arrhythmias. The disease is generally caused by mutations in genes encoding cardiac desmosomal proteins, with a prevalence of 1:1,000 in the general population.27 ARVC appears to be a major cause of SCD in athletes, as shown by studies from the Veneto region in Northern Italy.25 This condition appears to be a predominant etiology in athletes who die during exercise. The diagnosis of ARVC is complex and traditionally based on the Task Force Criteria, which include a series of clinical, electrocardiographic, structural, and functional traits.28 Patients at risk should be implanted with an ICD, although it is often challenging to identify individuals who would benefit from a device, especially for primary prevention. ARVC has been increasingly shown to be a biventricular disease including isolated LV involvement in some cases. These observations have resulted in calls for revising the existing diagnostic criteria as well as the nomenclature of the condition, with proposals to use the term “arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM).” At autopsy, the diagnosis is made by the presence of fibrosis in a subepicardial distribution that may be associated with fat in the absence of significant coronary artery disease.29 Dilated cardiomyopathy Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is defined by the presence of LV or biventricular dilatation and systolic dysfunction in the absence of abnormal loading conditions or coronary artery disease sufficient to cause global systolic impairment.30 DCM has a propensity to result in serious ventricular arrhythmias that may characterize the natural history of the disease, especially in some genetically mediated forms (pathogenic variants in LMNA, PLN, FLNC, DSP), which often overlap with ACM.31 DCM is a relatively rare cause of SCD in athletes (1%-3% of cases)1,2,10 because most individuals with this condition have impaired functional capacity and are unable to engage in vigorous exercise for prolonged periods. At autopsy, the heart is characterized by an increased weight with dilation and wall thinning of the left ventricle32 and replacement fibrosis of LV myocardium. Myocarditis Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease of the myocardium and demonstrates heterogeneity in its etiology and clinical presentation.33 Viral infection is the most common cause, although autoimmune mechanisms such as in giant cell and eosinophilic myocarditis are also recognized.33 Myocarditis accounts for a range of 2% to 9% of cases of SCD in athletes,1,2 and fatal arrhythmias may be the first manifestation of the disease. CMR is the gold-standard noninvasive test, and endomyocardial biopsy may provide a confirmation of the diagnosis and insights on the histological type. Postmortem diagnosis is often challenging and relies on the detection of a significant lymphocytic infiltrate and myocyte necrosis. The natural history of myocarditis is highly variable, with most patients recovering fully and others developing DCM with symptoms of heart failure.34 Some cases show a propensity to ventricular arrhythmias especially in the acute phase. Athletes with myocarditis should refrain from intense exercise for at least 3 months from the acute event.35 Idiopathic LVH Idiopathic LVH is a fairly novel entity that is increasingly reported following postmortem examination in cases of SCD.36,37 Recent studies2,3,38 reveal that in selective cohorts, idiopathic LVH may be reported in up to 30% of victims of SCD. Idiopathic LVH is characterized by unexplained LVH (heart weight >500 g in men and >400 g in women or increased heart weight as a function of body weight39 and LV wall thickness >15 mm)40 in the absence of the myocardial disarray. A recent study investigated whether idiopathic LVH and familial HCM are part of the same disease, performing comprehensive assessment of first-degree family members of 46 decedents with idiopathic LVH. Of the 125 family members investigated, none had features compatible with HCM, suggesting that idiopathic LVH is a distinct disease entity.41 Idiopathic fibrosis Myocardial fibrosis is a common feature of many cardiac conditions. In approximately 8% of cases, myocardial fibrosis is the only pathological feature detected at cardiac autopsy among athletes who are victims of SCD.1 A recent study showed that a 10% of subjects with idiopathic fibrosis at autopsy had actionable variants in genes associated with ACM, DCM, and HCM without autopsy findings of those diseases; hence, it is likely that myocardial fibrosis may precede the overt phenotypic expression of a cardiomyopathy, but still constitute a substrate of fatal arrhythmias.42 Coronary artery anomalies Coronary artery anomalies (CAAs) have been reported as the second most frequent cause of SCD in young athletes in the United States accounting for 17% of deaths.2 A study from the United Kingdom reported CAAs in 5% of cases in athletes who died suddenly,2 and another recent study attributed 9% of all SCDs in adolescent athletes to CAAs.43 Anomalous origin of the coronary artery from the wrong sinus with an inter-arterial course between the pulmonary artery and aorta are implicated in SCD.44 The inter-arterial course may also have an intramural segment running within the aortic wall.43 Imaging tests such as CMR and computed tomography coronary angiography provide detailed characterization of the origin and the course of the abnormal coronary artery. Although considerable debate remains regarding the optimal therapeutic management of these patients, surgical repair and coronary deroofing is generally preferred particularly if the left coronary artery arises from the right coronary sinus.45 Myocardial bridging (MB) is a congenital coronary anomaly characterized by the presence of a band of cardiac muscle overlying a segment of a coronary artery. The left anterior descending coronary artery is the most frequently affected vessel.46 This anomaly is relatively common in otherwise healthy individuals. In a large series of SCDs from the United Kingdom, MB was described in 0.5% of cases.47 A left anterior descending coronary artery MB was reported in 3% of young athletes who died suddenly in the United States.2 Electrical cardiac abnormalities Sudden deaths caused by electrical cardiac abnormalities are associated with a structurally normal heart at autopsy. Diagnosis can be made before death or following a cardiac arrest, typically after comprehensive investigations. Long QT syndrome Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is characterized by prolongation of the QT interval and propensity to ventricular arrhythmias. Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, and specifically torsades de pointes and ventricular fibrillation, is one of the most feared complications. Deaths can be triggered by several stimuli affecting athletes, such as adrenergic surges associated with physical and emotional stress, electrolyte disturbances, and performance enhancing agents. Inheritance follows an autosomal dominant or rarely an autosomal recessive pattern.48 LQTS has an estimated prevalence of up to 1:2,000.49 Causative genes involved in the pathogenesis of LQTS encode potassium (KCNQ1 and KCNH2), sodium (SCN5A), and calcium (CALM1-3, CACNA1C) channels.50 Although a report from an American registry attributed 3% of all SCDs to be caused by LQTS in young athletes, the true contribution of this condition to sudden fatal events is poorly understood.2 A normal heart at autopsy of young athletes who died suddenly, which is suggestive of sudden arrhythmic death syndrome, is an increasingly common finding,2,10 and LQTS is a possible cause of death in this setting. The diagnosis of LQTS relies on the assessment of the QT interval corrected for heart rate (QTc) and on a series of other ECG parameters measured at baseline and during exercise as well as features obtained by family and personal history.50 Brugada syndrome Brugada syndrome (BrS) is an inherited cardiac ion channel disorder, most commonly affecting the sodium channels, and is characterized by a propensity for ventricular arrhythmias and SCD. The diagnosis of BrS relies on the presence of the “type 1 Brugada pattern,” which consists of partial right bundle branch block, ≥0.2 mV J-point elevation, and coved ST-segment elevation followed by T-wave inversion in the right ventricular leads of the 12-lead ECG.51 This pattern can be spontaneous, or induced by sodium ion channel blocking drugs or transient conditions, such as high (>38 °C) body temperature. Inheritance follows an autosomal dominant pattern with a prevalence of 1:2,000. SCD in BrS often occurs at rest or during sleep. However, hyperthermia that may result from intense exercise and enhanced vagal tone, which is a common feature of physiological adaptation to exercise, may promote the development of fatal arrhythmias in athletes.52 Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia is an inherited arrhythmia syndrome characterized by stress-induced bidirectional ventricular tachycardia that may degenerate into ventricular fibrillation in the absence of structural cardiac abnormalities.53 Although the baseline 12-lead ECG is often normal, multifocal ventricular premature beats or VT with beat to beat 180° alternating QRS axis (bidirectional VT) can be observed at the exercise tolerance test (ETT). Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia most commonly results from mutations in 2 genes, RYR2 (cardiac ryanodine receptor RYR2—with an autosomal dominant line of inheritance) and CASQ2 (calsequestrin gene—with an autosomal recessive line of inheritance).54 Wolff-Parkinson-White The combination of ventricular pre-excitation on the ECG and cardiac symptoms (typically palpitations and syncope) and/or paroxysmal supraventricular arrhythmias defines Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome.55 The prevalence of pre-excitation (delta wave and short PR interval) is estimated between 0.1% and 0.3%.55,56 The risk of malignant arrhythmias in asymptomatic individuals is generally low, and ablation of the accessory pathway may decrease the risk of SCD in this condition.57 A refractory period of 40 mm in elite male athletes (and >34 mm in female athletes) is uncommon and is unlikely to represent the physiological consequence of exercise training.62,63 Static exercise and typically weight training may result in acute rises in blood pressure to over 300 mm Hg,64 with potential implications for aortic dilatation. Marfan syndrome, which is characterized by tall stature and may affect basketball or volleyball players, is a collagen disorder that is characterized by cardiac, ocular, and skeletal abnormalities and typically aortic dilatation. Affected individuals may present aortic dilatation and should be discouraged from engaging in intense static exercise that can increase the risk of aortic rupture or dissection.35 Commotio cordis Commotio cordis is defined as SCD triggered by a sudden blow to the precordium. It is more common in children and adolescents who have pliable chest walls.25,43 A series of experimental models indicate that if delivered at the onset of the T-wave in the cardiac cycle, precordial blows can result in ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest. In a recent study from the United Kingdom on a large cohort of adolescents who died suddenly, commotio cordis was the attributed cause of death in 1% of the cases and it was more common in athletes (5% compared with 1% in nonathletes).43 Performance enhancing drugs The use of performance enhancing drugs is increasingly reported in athletes.65 Doping substances are of many kinds and are classified according to the World Anti-Doping Agency code.65 Some of the most commonly used agents include anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS), stimulants and oxygen-carrying modulators. AAS may lead to LVH, premature atherosclerosis, hypertension, and myocardial infarction. Mortality among athletes who abuse AAS is estimated to be 6 times higher than in nonabusing athletes, and around 30% of these deaths can be attributed to cardiovascular causes.66 Stimulants such as ephedrine may cause hypertension, tremor, and palpitations.65 Recombinant human erythropoietin stimulates hemoglobin production resulting in an increase in hematocrit and better athletic performance. A recent prospective cross-sectional study of 2,488 healthy older adults found a strong association between erythropoietin and incident heart failure, coronary heart disease, stroke, mortality, and decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate.67 Molecular autopsy Postmortem genetic testing, referred to as “molecular autopsy” constitutes an important part of the diagnostic work-up of unexplained SCD and familial evaluation. The diagnostic yield of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants is reported to be between 12% and 27% across studies using sequencing or cardiac panels for electrical disorders and cardiomyopathy.68,69 When combined with family screening, the yield increased to 39%.68 Postmortem genetic testing has also been used in structural disease, with pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants found in 32% of those with cardiomyopathy identified at autopsy and 2% to 26% of those with structural disease of uncertain etiology, such as idiopathic hypertrophy and/or fibrosis.69 Diagnostic Challenges in Athletes Physiological adaptation to exercise: the “athlete’s” heart Participation in systematic vigorous exercise is associated with a modest increase in LV wall thickness, and biventricular cavity size with preserved systolic and diastolic function, which are established manifestations of the “athlete’s heart.”70,71 A proportion of male athletes reveal cardiac dimensions that overlap with the cardiomyopathies (Figure 3). The differential diagnosis between athlete’s heart and cardiomyopathy is generally confined to men with a large body surface area who engage in endurance sports such as rowing, canoeing, long distance cycling, and long-distance running.72,73 Most of these athletes reveal ventricular dimensions that resemble dilated cardiomyopathy and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, whereas few show a wall thickness which overlaps with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. In contrast, a significant proportion of athletes of African or Afro-Caribbean origin participating in explosive sports such as football, soccer, and basketball reveal an LV wall thickness, which overlaps with morphologically mild hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).74,75 Increased trabeculations and repolarization changes may overlap with LV noncompaction cardiomyopathy and ion channel diseases respectively.76,77 Figure 3 Overlap Between “Athlete’s Heart” and Predominant Causes of SCD Physiological electrical, structural, and functional changes in athletes may overlap with cardiac disease with a gray area that often needs clarification through second line cardiac investigations. The measurement of the QTc interval in young athletes may be challenging as a result of training-related ECG changes. According to previous recommendations, competitive exercise was categorically prohibited in the whole spectrum of patients with HCM. Over the past decade, there have been several observations indicating that physical activity and exercise of moderate intensity should be promoted in patients with HCM deemed at low risk. The heterogeneous morphology and pathophysiology of HCM means that some individuals are capable of participating in vigorous exercise including high-intensity competitive sports.104,105 An Italian study showed no excess of adverse events in athletes with HCM who continued to exercise, even at a high intensity level.104 Data from the first multinational sports safety ICD registry report, which included 65 individuals with HCM, showed no adverse events during a 31-month follow-up period.104 Although more individuals received appropriate and inappropriate shocks during physical activity than during resting conditions, there was no difference in shock rate between usual physical activity and competitive sport. This study suggests that even individuals deemed to be at high risk may be able to participate in vigorous exercise with an ICD in situ. The study was underpowered to assess the true impact of vigorous exercise, and although dynamic sports involving explosive sprints such as basketball and soccer were included, few athletes participated in sports with high risk of collision such as American football or ice hockey, where the risk of damaging the generator box during collision may be higher. The prospective registry, “Lifestyle and Exercise in HCM” (LIVE-HCM), which included >600 exercising individuals with HCM showed no increased risk of SCD or life-threatening arrhythmias among individuals with HCM who exercised vigorously in comparison with those exercising moderately or those who were sedentary.106 The most contemporary sports cardiology guidelines and also the recent AHA/ACC guidelines for the management of patients with HCM are largely harmonized and recommend that among asymptomatic adults with morphologically mild HCM, particularly those who are habitually active and engaging in vigorous exercise at the time of diagnosis, competitive sport involving high-intensity effort may be considered after a detailed and transparent discussion that outlines the rare occurrence of adverse cardiac events but that also emphasizes the knowledge gaps in risk stratification and the unpredictability of SCA in athletes,104,107 This precaution is especially relevant to specific high-risk populations such as adolescent and young adult men competing in sports requiring frequent bouts of explosive activity and young male athletes of African or Afro-Caribbean origin. This is because most exercise-related SCDs from HCM occur in adolescent and young adult male athletes involved in dynamic explosive sports such as basketball and soccer, with Black ethnicity conferring a higher risk. A select group of individuals with an ICD in situ may also engage in competitive sport after full discussion with an expert, although full contact/collision sport is not recommended. Exercise appears to confer a higher risk of exercise-induced fatal arrhythmias and worsening of phenotype expression in ACM,108 although available evidence is mainly from retrospective studies. Therefore, international guidelines are fairly conservative in this setting, and high-intensity exercise and competitive sports are not recommended in this condition. In athletes with LQTS, high-intensity exercise is contraindicated where there has been prior arrhythmogenic syncope or cardiac arrest and in those with QTc >500 ms, but can be considered in individuals with mild or absent phenotype who are asymptomatic and optimally treated with beta-blocker following individualized expert assessment.35,109 Genotype positive phenotype negative athletes Genotype positive phenotype negative (G+P−) individuals harbor a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant without exhibiting a phenotypic manifestation of the disease.110 The knowledge on the effects of intense exercise in G+P− individuals is limited, and the general attitude is to oversimplify clinical management based on phenotypes. The recommendation to engage in intensive exercise and competitive sports is usually contingent on annual clinical surveillance. However, when actionable variants in specific genes, such as LMNA or PKP2, are found, guidelines do not differentiate between G+P− individuals and patients with overt disease, emphasizing the arrhythmic risk and recommending avoidance of participation in high-intensity recreational exercise and competitive sports.35,111,112 Future Perspectives SCD in young athletes draws many questions regarding the epidemiology, causes, and prevention of these catastrophes (Central Illustration). Implementation of regional or national registries where autopsy is performed by a dedicated cardiac pathology laboratory with expertise in this setting is crucial to provide more robust evidence on incidence and underlying etiologies. Gaps in knowledge regarding the impact of intensive exercise in individuals with cardiovascular diseases should be addressed through randomized clinical trials that may inform the optimal safe dose of exercise in these patients. Preparticipation screening remains a means to detect early serious cardiac disease; however, theories on its beneficial role on hard endpoints (eg, prevention of SCD in large populations) remain speculative with major concerns on cost-effectiveness. Large-scale prospective longitudinal studies, perhaps using novel technologies (wearable devices or smartphone-based ECG) are necessary to corroborate the evidence in this context. Knowledge on determinants of ECG variability in athletes, including race, sex, age, and others, is limited to observational cross-sectional data, whereas longitudinal studies may provide further insight on their significance. Central Illustration Gaps in Knowledge and Future Directions in Sudden Cardiac Death in Young Athletes SCD in young athletes is a subject that remains controversial, with recent advances in the field, but still many gaps in knowledge (blue circle). Future directions (orange circles) include development of large prospective registries that may inform on epidemiology and the use of standardized autopsy protocols would increase the diagnostic accuracy. Preparticipation cardiac screening remains a matter of debate, with questions regarding its use in large cohorts. The mechanistic role of intense exercise on cardiac phenotype and its safety in individuals with cardiac disease is a territory that will need to be explored to provide tailored recommendations on exercise prescription. The precise epidemiological burden of SCD is not well established, with a high variability in terms of incidence among different studies. Young men of African and African-American origin appear to be the population at the highest risk. A diverse spectrum of diseases is implicated in SCD in young athletes. Cardiac assessment of some athletes can be challenging when the electrical and structural changes consequent to physiological adaptation to exercise overlap with cardiac disease. A holistic interpretation of clinical findings in conjunction with important elements in personal and family history is crucial for resolving the diagnostic conundrum. Prevention can be achieved through preparticipation screening and implementation of policies aimed at prompt cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Exercise prescription in young individuals with potentially serious cardiac diseases should embrace shared decision making whilst acknowledging knowledge gaps in this setting.
