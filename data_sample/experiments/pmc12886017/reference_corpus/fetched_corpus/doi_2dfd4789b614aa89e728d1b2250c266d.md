# Assessing the quality of prediction models in health care using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST): an evaluation of its use and practical application.

## Abstract

Background and objectives: Since 2019, the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST; www.probast.org) has supported methodological quality assessments of prediction model studies. Most prediction model studies are rated with a "High" risk of bias (ROB) and researchers report low interrater reliability (IRR) using PROBAST. We aimed to (1) assess the IRR of PROBAST ratings between assessors of the same study and understand reasons for discrepancies, (2) determine which items contribute most to domain-level ROB ratings, and (3) explore the impact of consensus meetings.

Study design and setting: We used PROBAST assessments from a systematic review of diagnostic and prognostic COVID-19 prediction models as a case study. Assessors included international experts in prediction model studies or their reviews. We assessed IRR using prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK) before consensus meetings, examined bias ratings per domain-level ROB judgments, and evaluated the impact of consensus meetings by identifying rating changes after discussion.

Results: We analyzed 2167 PROBAST assessments from 27 assessor pairs covering 760 prediction models: 384 developments, 242 validations, and 134 mixed assessments (including both). The IRR using PABAK was higher for overall ROB judgments (development: 0.82 [0.76; 0.89]; validation: 0.78 [0.68; 0.88]) compared to domain- and item-level judgments. Some PROBAST items frequently contributed to domain-level ROB judgments, eg, 3.5 Outcome blinding and 4.1 Sample size. Consensus discussions mainly led to item-level and never to overall ROB rating changes.

Conclusion: Within this case study, PROBAST assessments received high IRR at the overall ROB level, with some variation at item- and domain-level. To reduce variability, PROBAST assessors should standardize item- and domain-level judgments and hold well-structured consensus meetings between assessors of the same study.

Plain language summary: The Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST; www.probast.org) provides a set of items to assess the quality of medical studies on so-called prediction tools that calculate an individual's probability of having or developing a certain disease or health outcome. Previous research found low interrater reliability (IRR; ie, how consistently two assessors rate aspects of the same study) when using PROBAST. To understand why this is the case, we conducted a large study involving more than 30 experts from around the world, all of whom applied PROBAST to the same set of prediction tool studies. Based on more than 2150 PROBAST assessments, we identified which PROBAST items led to the most disagreements between raters, explored reasons for these disagreements, and examined whether the use of so-called consensus meetings (ie, different assessors of the same study discuss their ratings and decide on a finalized rating) impacted PROBAST ratings. Our study found that the IRR between different assessors of the same study was higher than previously reported. One explanation for the better agreement compared to previous research may be the preplanning on how to assess certain PROBAST aspects before starting the assessments, as well as holding well-structured consensus meetings. These improvements lead to a more effective use of PROBAST in evaluating the trustworthiness and quality of prediction tools in the health-care domain.

What is the implication and what should change now? • To reduce variability between assessors during methodological quality assessments, assessors should standardize item-level ratings and domain-level risk of bias judgments, and hold well-structured consensus meetings between different assessors of the same study. 1 Introduction The Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST; www.probast.org) was launched in 2019 and was designed to support researchers in judging the risk of bias (ROB) and applicability of diagnostic and prognostic prediction models [1,2]. Bias refers to a systematic error in the estimators of the model's predictive performance for the target population of interest and may act in either direction such that there may be over – or under estimation of the true model performance. PROBAST consists of four domains with a total of 20 signaling questions concerning ROB and applicability [1,2]. These signaling questions are rated with “No” and “Probably No” to flag the potential for bias introduced by specific methodological aspects, “Yes” and “Probably Yes” indicating no evidence for bias, and “No Information” flagging insufficient information for adequate judgment [1,2]. Responses to signaling questions guide domain-level ROB judgments (“High”, “Low”, and “Unclear”), which then inform the overall ROB rating of a study. Most prediction model studies assessed in systematic reviews and other appraisals (eg, for developing medical guidelines) receive “High” ROB ratings across different PROBAST domains [3–5]. For example, this may be due to bias introduced by participant selection (ie, PROBAST domain 1) or the analytical methods used to develop or validate the prediction model (ie, PROBAST domain 4) [1,2]. A recent systematic review on prediction models regarding COVID-19 diagnosis and prognosis illustrates this issue: 545 of the 731 assessed prediction models received a “High” overall ROB judgment using PROBAST [3]. Besides methodological concerns in prediction models and prediction model studies, other reasons for “High” ROB ratings using PROBAST have been explored. Langenhuijsen et al described “ceiling effects”, ie, the inability to distinguish between degrees of ROB ratings [5]. That is, if a single signaling question receives a “No” rating, the domain may be rated as “High” ROB; similarly, if all signaling questions receive a “No” rating, the same applies [5]. PROBAST guidance leaves whether a single “No” should lead to a “High” domain-level ROB rating up to the assessors and their specific context [1,2]. Therefore, determining if a “High” ROB judgment resulted from just a single signaling question is challenging without knowing how PROBAST assessments were operationalized [1,2,5]. Conversely, PROBAST guidance for overall study ROB judgment are clear: if one ROB domain is rated “High”, the overall study ROB should be “High”; “Low” overall study ROB should only follow if all domains are rated “Low” [1,2]. Several studies have reported poor interrater agreement and reliability among assessors using PROBAST [5,6]. Subjectivity and rater dependency are known pitfalls of any ROB assessment (eg, for randomized intervention trials, diagnostic test accuracy studies, and etiologic studies) and are not limited to PROBAST and prediction model studies [7,8]. Providing training sessions prior to using PROBAST showed improved agreement on domain- and overall-prediction model study ROB judgments [6]. As applies to all types and settings of ROB assessments, to prevent low interrater agreements and reliability, adequate guidance and trainings may be advisable before applying any ROB tool. We conducted a comprehensive study using a case study of 2167 PROBAST assessments to 1) assess IRR and agreement in PROBAST assessments by identifying signaling questions with significant variation in ratings between different assessors of the same study and understanding the reasons for these discrepancies, 2) determine which PROBAST signaling questions contribute most significantly to domain-level ROB ratings, and 3) explore whether the use of consensus meetings between different assessors of the same study impacts PROBAST ratings. 2 Methods 2.1 Case study The case study comprised 2167 PROBAST assessments from a systematic review on prediction models for diagnosis and prognosis of COVID-19 [3]. The review included the judgments of 27 pairs of assessors, who were international experts in prediction model studies or their reviews and had varying levels of experience using PROBAST [3]. Each prediction model study was judged by two assessors using PROBAST. Details of the case study, as well as the instructions provided to assessors are part of the Supplementary (section A and B). 2.2 Dataset description The case study's data extraction sheet comprised two subgroups of data: the individual assessor ratings (where available) as well as the consensus ratings (ie, the agreed ratings between the assessors after discussion). Each row in the data extraction sheet represented the assessment either of a single prediction model study (development or validation procedure), or of a single model from within a study including multiple models/procedures, or of both the model development and validation in one row if assessed together (ie, hereafter referred to as “mixed assessments”). We specifically focused on the ROB assessments using PROBAST, including responses to all 20 signaling questions, set into the following four domains: Participants, Predictors, Outcome, and Analysis. PROBAST consists of the following two parts: 20 signaling questions for model development and 17 for model validation assessments. At the end, PROBAST includes an overall judgment on the ROB of the prediction model study (hereafter “overall ROB judgment”) [1,2]. Model development refers to the actual development (or training) of the prediction model. Model validation refers to evaluating the predictive performance of a model using other data (external validation) [1,2]. Open table in a new tab 2.3 Statistical analysis Prior to all analyses and in accordance with the original guidance of PROBAST [1,2], ratings of “Probably Yes” were merged with “Yes”, and “Probably No” with “No”, as they are treated similarly in PROBAST. All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 2023.12.0 + 369) [9]. 2.3.1 Identifying variations in item- and domain-specific interrater reliability and agreement We calculated the prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK) to evaluate the IRR between different assessors of the same studies [10,11]. Though widely used, Cohen's kappa may underestimate agreement in case of prevalence imbalances [10]. Given the high proportion of “High” ROB ratings identified in the case study, we focused our IRR assessments on PABAK but calculated the Cohen's kappa for reference [3,11]. We conducted these analyses for item-level ratings, as well as domain-level and overall ROB judgments. PABAK and Cohen's kappa values were classified as less than 0.21 indicating “slight”, 0.21–0.40 indicating “fair”, 0.41–0.60 indicating “moderate”, 0.61–0.80 indicating “substantial”, and more than 0.81 indicating “almost perfect” agreement. For the absolute agreement between the assessors, we calculated counts and proportions. Incomplete sets of assessments (ie, where the rating of both or one of the assessors was missing) were excluded from the analyses. 2.3.2 Item-level ratings and domain-level ROB judgments To evaluate how item-level ratings contribute to domain-level ROB judgments, we analyzed “No” responses to the PROBAST items. We specifically examined “No” responses since these indicate the potential for bias and thus contribute to “High” domain-level ROB ratings (Supplementary, section B) [1,2]. For our analysis, we considered a single PROBAST item to be “overruled” to inform domain-level ROB judgments if an item included a “No” response despite a “Low” or “Unclear” domain-level ROB judgment. Accordingly, an item contributed less to domain-level ROB judgments if it was frequently overruled (ie, a high proportion of “No” responses despite a “Low” or “Unclear” domain-level ROB rating). Consequently, an item contributed more to domain-level ROB judgments if it had a low proportion of “No” responses in “Low” or “Unclear” domain-level ROB judgments but high proportions of “No” responses in “High” ROB domain-level judgments. Besides, we assessed how many studies received at least one “No” response per domain-level ROB rating, to explore whether receiving a “No” response contributed to domain-level ROB judgments (giving an indication whether rating instructions were followed: Did all domains rated with “High” ROB include at least one “No” response?). We calculated the counts and proportions of PROBAST items rated “No” per domain-level ROB rating. Consensus ratings were used for this analysis as they represented the most complete set and ensured a consistent interpretation of the scoring instructions. 2.3.3 Impact of consensus meetings on PROBAST ratings To explore the impact of consensus meetings between different assessors of the same study on their PROBAST assessments, we evaluated 1) patterns of rating changes in PROBAST assessments from the assessor to the consensus rating and 2) whether an item-level rating change led to a domain-level ROB judgment change (ie, domain-level shift). A rating change was present if both ratings of the assessors of the same study differed from the consensus rating. For example, if assessor 1 scored item 1.1 Data source as “No Information” and assessor 2 rated it as “No”, but after discussion during the consensus meeting, they agreed to rate it as “Yes”; this represents a rating change. Similarly, if both assessor 1 and 2 initially rated the item as “No” but the consensus rating was “Yes”, a change in rating occurred. Furthermore, a domain-level shift occurred if, eg, assessor 1 rated item 1.1 data source as “No Information” and assessor 2 rated 1.1. as “No”, but the consensus rating included a “Yes”, this may result in a change on the domain-level. In this case, the domain-level ROB judgment may change from “Unclear” (assessor 1) and “High” (assessor 2) to a consensus rating of “Low” ROB. We only considered complete assessment sets for the analysis, comprising two individual assessor ratings and one consensus rating per model. To identify patterns of rating changes, we calculated counts and proportions, with the numerator being the number of rating changes and the denominator being the number of models with complete rating sets. To evaluate domain-level shifts, we counted the number of item-level changes per domain and per domain-level changes. 3 Results Our analysis included 2167 assessments comprising 1407 individual assessor and 760 consensus ratings (for 113 consensus ratings, the individual assessments of one or both assessors were missing). Most item-level rating changes occurred in model development assessments; however, relative to the number of item-level rating changes, most domain-level shifts were observed in model validation assessments (Supplementary, section F). 4 Discussion We performed a comprehensive study on the use and practical application of the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST; www.probast.org) [1,2] to assess diagnostic and prognostic prediction models using a case study of a large systematic review on COVID-19 prediction models [3]. Our results show that the IRR using PROBAST, based on the prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK), was high for overall ROB judgments but lacks agreement on some item-level ratings and domain-level ROB judgments. Furthermore, IRR was generally higher in prediction model validation assessments compared to prediction model development assessments. We identified certain PROBAST items that frequently contributed to domain-level ROB judgments. The use of consensus meetings between different assessors of the same study impacted item-level ratings to a larger extent than domain-level ROB judgments, while not showing any impact on overall ROB judgments. 4.1 Comparison to other studies Our results partly reflect findings reported by others [5,6,12]. We observed varying IRR across PROBAST assessments but cannot confirm findings of consistently low IRR [5,6]. Use of the PABAK as opposed to the Cohen's kappa and differences in PROBAST operationalization (ie, how domains should be rated) may be possible explanations for these variations [4,10,13]. We identified varying trends in IRR between model development and validation assessments. This distinction is often overlooked in prior research, where IRR calculations frequently combine data from various systematic reviews without distinguishing between these evaluation types [5,6]. Furthermore, due to the difference in assessors and research teams, the assessments may have been conducted with a different set of instructions (ie, operationalizations), leading to greater variability in responses (ie, lower IRR) [13]. In our study, the assessors received the same set of instructions and consensus meetings were planned (per protocol) throughout the five assessment rounds to finalize ratings. Thus, subjectivity could be limited. We found higher IRR between different assessors of the same study compared to previous reports [5,6]. However, while we based our main IRR assessments on the PABAK, previous studies frequently used the Cohen's kappa. Cohen's kappa may underestimate IRR when a certain category is overrepresented (eg, as in our case, a large proportion of “High” ROB ratings for most assessments of the Analysis domain) [3,10]. For reference, we included the IRR based on Cohen's kappa in the Supplementary (section D). While the Cohen's kappa showed similar trends as by PABAK for some PROBAST items, others included lower IRR. Therefore, focusing on the PABAK to assess IRR seemed appropriate. Adequate instructions and operationalization prior to any ROB assessments are key to ensuring consistency and reliability across assessments [7,14,15]. In our study, we identified certain PROBAST items that frequently contributed to domain-level ROB judgments, while others were frequently “overruled”. However, the instructions given in our case study may differ from those of other PROBAST assessments. It is difficult to evaluate the differences in how domain-level ROB judgments were operationalized across different reports without understanding the specific criteria assessors used. To ensure comparability of methodological quality assessments, reporting checklists (eg, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analysis [PRISMA]) might consider including operationalizations as a reporting item [16]. PROBAST presents a minimal set of methodological quality criteria which merely guide the assessments of prediction model studies for diagnosis and prognosis [1,2]. In our study, some PROBAST items frequently contributed to domain-level ROB judgments. Venema et al published a short form of PROBAST in 2021 consisting of six selectively summarized items [12]. Using short forms of PROBAST should be approached with caution, as they may falsely encourage assessors to completely disregard certain aspects of methodological quality (eg, 1.1 Data source or 1.2 In/exclusion criteria [Domain 1: Participants]), which may severely impact a study's validity. This may especially be the case for inexperienced assessors or when using PROBAST to help guide the design of a prediction model study. PROBAST was developed to thoroughly assess the ROB in the entire prediction model study, addressing all relevant methodological aspects of a prediction model study. As applies to all ROB tools, consensus meetings between assessors of the same study have a critical role in improving methodological quality assessments. In our study, rating changes occurred on both item- and domain-level judgments after consensus discussion and even in case of previous agreement between the individual assessors. Therefore, holding actual consensus discussions proofed beneficial. These findings highlight the need for structured, well-facilitated consensus meetings between assessors of the same study. 4.2 Implications To ensure consistency and reliability in ROB assessments, adequate training and standardized assessment procedures are essential. Although assessors were trained experts with varying levels of experience in using PROBAST, our analyses showed that certain PROBAST items would still benefit from more concise explanations and clear operationalization by the users prior to performing PROBAST assessments. Moreover, it is crucial to hold actual consensus meetings during ROB assessments. Even when assessors initially agree on their individual ratings, discussions may still lead to changes in the domain-level ROB judgments. These improvements have been incorporated into the recent development of PROBAST + AI [17,18] (www.probast.org) which is a continuation and update of PROBAST-2019. 4.3 Limitations Our study has several limitations. First, our case study included prediction models which were developed and/or validated during the global pandemic COVID-19. Due to the pandemic's urgency, development, validation, and PROBAST assessments were performed in a short amount of time. Furthermore, specific aspects (eg, predicted outcomes) of the case study may have affected our results more than others. For example, in prediction model studies with an objective outcome (case study), such as mortality, PROBAST assessments will differ compared to those done in subjective outcomes, such as quality of life (ie, see item 3.5 Outcome blinding). Therefore, our findings might not reflect PROBAST assessments performed in other medical fields or settings. Second, for a few items, individual assessments had high percentages of missing values. Though reviewers were asked to submit their individual ratings, this was not always done. It is possible that reviewers preferred to wait for the consensus meeting or consult an expert for difficult items, which may also explain why the consensus rating set was more complete than the individual assessments, and why certain items were missing more frequently than others. Third, due to the high number of assessments, we were unable to critically evaluate any free-text comments assessors made in detail. Nevertheless, we randomly sampled 10 consensus ratings to explore any information assessors noted down. Notes primarily included reasons for specific domain-level ROB judgments. Fourth, our results may be influenced by a certain “learning effect” that occurred in the multistage review of the case study [3]. However, while some assessors were involved from the beginning, others only joined in later rounds, with varying workloads. Therefore, the “learning effect” likely only had a limited impact. 5 Conclusion For overall ROB judgments, PROBAST assessments include high interrater agreement and reliability. Some variation exists on item- and domain-level assessments. To ensure PROBAST assessments can be performed adequately and coherently, assessors involved in prediction model reviews or guideline development should therefore operationalize on how to judge PROBAST items and domains prior to conducting PROBAST assessments. We also encourage PROBAST users to hold well-structured consensus meetings between assessors of the same study. This will help to ensure that PROBAST assessments are less subjective to variability. The findings of this study have led to the recent development of PROBAST + AI as an extension and update of PROBAST-2019.
