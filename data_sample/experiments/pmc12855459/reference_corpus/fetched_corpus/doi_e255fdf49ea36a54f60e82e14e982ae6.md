# Efficacy of Nonpharmacological Interventions in Cognitive Impairment: Systematic Review And Network Meta-Analysis.

## Abstract

Introduction: There is currently no known cure for cognitive impairment, which highlights the need to explore other ways of managing this condition. This topic has recently become an area of active research. However, the availability of nonpharmacological options poses a challenge when trying to determine the best treatment for improving cognitive function.

Methods: We conducted a systematic review and a Bayesian network meta-analysis to compare the effects of nonpharmacological interventions on global cognition in patients with mild cognitive impairment and dementia. The nonpharmacological interventions were classified as aerobic exercise, strength exercise, multicomponent physical exercise, other physical exercises, tai chi, mind-body exercises, traditional cognitive rehabilitation, computer-based cognitive rehabilitation, occupational therapy, music therapy, physical-cognitive rehabilitation, and reminiscence therapy.

Results: Physical-cognitive rehabilitation emerged as the most effective nonpharmacological intervention for enhancing global cognition in patients with unspecified cognitive impairment and dementia, whereas occupational therapy focused on dual-task interventions was found to be the most effective nonpharmacological intervention for mild cognitive impairment.

Conclusion: These results underscore the importance of adopting a dual approach to managing cognitive impairment, integrating both cognitive and physical rehabilitation within the same intervention.

ABSTRACT Introduction There is currently no known cure for cognitive impairment, which highlights the need to explore other ways of managing this condition. This topic has recently become an area of active research. However, the availability of nonpharmacological options poses a challenge when trying to determine the best treatment for improving cognitive function. Methods We conducted a systematic review and a Bayesian network meta-analysis to compare the effects of nonpharmacological interventions on global cognition in patients with mild cognitive impairment and dementia. The nonpharmacological interventions were classified as aerobic exercise, strength exercise, multicomponent physical exercise, other physical exercises, tai chi, mind-body exercises, traditional cognitive rehabilitation, computer-based cognitive rehabilitation, occupational therapy, music therapy, physical-cognitive rehabilitation, and reminiscence therapy. Results Physical-cognitive rehabilitation emerged as the most effective nonpharmacological intervention for enhancing global cognition in patients with unspecified cognitive impairment and dementia, whereas occupational therapy focused on dual-task interventions was found to be the most effective nonpharmacological intervention for mild cognitive impairment. Conclusion These results underscore the importance of adopting a dual approach to managing cognitive impairment, integrating both cognitive and physical rehabilitation within the same intervention. Key Words Global cognition rehabilitation dementia mild cognitive impairment BACKGROUND The burden of noncommunicable diseases has been increasing according to the increase in life expectancy, leading to an increase in disability and an increase in the cost of aging management.1,2 Among noncommunicable diseases, dementia has a profound functional impact on individuals, along with emotional, physical, and economic costs for caregivers. The World Dementia Plan aims to mitigate these effects through risk factor education, early diagnosis, targeted treatment, caregiver assistance, and dementia-related research.3 Dementia research has increased in recent years with notable advances in terms of risk determination, prevention, assessment, and early diagnosis using neuroimaging and molecular biomarkers.4 However, the vast majority of drugs developed to modify the course of the disease have not been effective. Only aducanumab, a human monoclonal antibody against aggregated forms of amyloid beta, has obtained U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval for the treatment of early stage Alzheimer ́s disease, showing improvements in cognition and function.5 However, although the development of new treatment options may be promising, these options have been developed for Alzheimer ́s disease, excluding other dementia etiologies. Additionally, the cost of these new options could exclude patients from low-middle-income countries, increasing inequalities in regions such as Latin America.6 Considering that the efficacy of pharmacological treatment as a unique intervention for the treatment of cognitive impairment is controversial, the use of nonpharmacological interventions has been completely extended in the management of patients with dementia and mild cognitive impairment.7 There are a wide range of nonpharmacological interventions that differ in their level of patient participation, the technology requirements, the cognitive costs generated, or the level of training needed by the personnel carrying out the intervention.8 Nonpharmacological interventions are used to manage both behavioral9 and cognitive symptoms10 and reduce the burden of care on caregivers.11 Although nonpharmacological interventions are recommended as part of the management of cognitive impairment,4 no consensus exists about their effect on cognition and what kind of treatment is better. In part, this is because few head-to-head clinical trials exist. For this reason, we propose this multiple-treatment network meta-analysis to compare the efficacy of nonpharmacological interventions on global cognition in individuals with mild cognitive impairment and dementia. METHODS Protocol Registration The protocol was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under register identification CRD42023400784. In addition, this review was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses-network meta-analysis (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1).12 Information Sources and Search Strategy The search was performed in three databases, the PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases, up to July 2023. No specific filters were applied in the search. The complete PubMed search strategy is displayed in the supplemental material (Supplementary Table 2). The interventions included were as follows: aerobic exercise, strength exercise, multicomponent physical exercise, other physical exercises, tai chi, mind-body exercises (other than tai chi), traditional cognitive rehabilitation, computer-based cognitive rehabilitation, occupational therapy, music therapy, physical-cognitive rehabilitation, and reminiscence therapy (Supplementary Table 3). The outcome was global cognition at the end of the intervention or the pre and postintervention difference measured by a validated neuropsychological test. Inclusion Criteria The main objective of the search was to identify randomized controlled trials focused on assessing the effect of nonpharmacological interventions in the treatment of cognitive decline in people with a diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia, specifically the impact on global cognition. We considered studies that met the following criteria: 1) Participants: Adults with a diagnosis of MCI or dementia; 2) Intervention: At least one of the interventions mentioned above, where patient participation was needed; 3) Control: Any intervention mentioned above or usual care; 4) Outcome: Global cognition as measured using validated tests such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), or Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive (ADAS-Cog). However, other global cognition scales were also considered; and 5) Studies: Two-arm or multiarm randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English or Spanish. Studies were excluded if they 1) included only healthy people or those in which comparisons were made against healthy people, 2) included interventions such as magnetic neurostimulation or alternative medicine treatments, or 3) included drug therapy as part of the intervention. Study Identification and Data Extraction Once the search strategy was defined and performed in the databases, all records were imported to the reference management system Mendeley to identify and exclude duplicates. To continue the selection process, the systematic reviews collaborative tool Rayyan was used. Two authors independently screened the literature and selected the studies ultimately included in the review (LV, CA). A third author resolved any discrepancies. The following data were extracted for each study included in the review: author and publication year, type of population (i.e., MCI, dementia, or both), type of intervention and activity in the control group (with sample size), mean age, percentage of females in each group, data about the intervention (i.e., weeks, sessions per week, and time per session), and neuropsychological validated test to assess global cognition (i.e., MMSE, ADAS-Cog, MoCA, or other). Risk of Bias The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2·0 (RoB 2·0) was used to assess the risk of bias of each included study. This tool considers six domains in the risk of bias assessment: randomization process, derivation for intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported results, and overall results. The percentages of articles ranked as having a low, high, or some risk of bias were reported for each domain. Statistical Analysis The main outcome of this network meta-analysis was the comparison of the effects of nonpharmacological interventions on cognition in individuals with cognitive impairment. To calculate the standard mean difference (SMD) and variance for each individual study, the web-based effect size calculator Campbell Collaboration (https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/research-resources/effect-size-calculator.html) was used. The standard error was calculated considering the SMD 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The Bayesian approach was used to analyze the data of the included studies through a contrast-based model to generate the estimations of posterior distributions for each parameter.13 A random effect model approach was used considering the high probability of heterogeneity between the included studies. Network meta-analysis was conducted according to the following steps. First, we assessed the distribution and similarity of some relevant characteristics between the interventions to assess transitivity. The means for age, percentage of females, number of sessions, and time per session were calculated from the data reported in each arm where the specific intervention was included. Second, the consistency assumption was tested through a node-splitting model. In this model, the effect sizes (ESs) obtained by direct and indirect evidence were compared in each pairwise comparison. If a difference between direct and indirect comparisons was not found, we assumed that this specific pairwise comparison was consistent. Otherwise, inconsistency was assumed. Third, a network geometry graph was generated to assess the distribution of the evidence between the interventions included in the analysis. The size of each node represents the number of arms available per treatment. The line width represents the number of direct comparisons between two interventions. To meet the connectedness assumption, each treatment had to relate to at least one other.14 Fourth, to define the Bayesian model, a normal noninformative prior distribution for each parameter (i.e., each intervention included) with a mean of 0 and a variance with a scaling factor of k = 15 was assumed. Considering a random effect model, a sigma prior distribution was included, assuming a uniform distribution with a lower limit = 0 and an upper limit = 50 (the maximum likelihood).14 The Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method is a simulation-based algorithm used to assess the posterior distribution. We defined an MCMC simulation with three chains and 15000 iterations, eliminating the first 2000 iterations. The model convergence was assessed by the trade and density plot and confirmed by the Gelman Rubin diagnostic test. Fifth, the posterior distributions calculated by the MCMC simulation were used to assess the pairwise comparisons between interventions, reporting the ES and 95% CI for each intervention. These comparisons were reported in a heat plot. Additionally, the posterior distribution was used to estimate the ranking of the interventions that were reported with the surface under the cumulative (SUCRA) ranking plot. The interventions were reported from the best intervention to the worst. Finally, an intervention ranking was reported through an interval plot in which the ES of the SMD for comparison with usual care and the confidence intervals obtained were plotted. Finally, heterogeneity was assessed for both the entire network and each direct pairwise comparison. Heterogeneity was reported through I2 statistics. An I2 less than 30% was considered nonimportant heterogeneity, that between 30% and 50% was considered moderate, that between 50% and 75% was considered substantial, and that 75% or higher was considered considerable heterogeneity. The small study effect was assessed by a funnel plot. Estimations for each pair of direct comparisons were performed and plotted. Similar procedures were followed to perform the subgroup analysis considering two groups, MCI and dementia, regarding the population included in each study. All analyses were performed in R software (version 4·3·0) and STATA (version 17·0). The R package BUGSnet, which uses JAGS software, was used for the postulation of the Bayesian model and the estimation of posterior distribution.15 The gemtc package was used to construct the node-splitting model and to assess heterogeneity.16 A funnel plot was generated to evaluate the small study effect with the STATA package mvmeta. DISCUSSION The purpose of this review was to compare nonpharmacological interventions for managing cognitive decline in MCI and dementia. Our findings indicate that physical-cognitive exercise, which combines physical and cognitive training in a single protocol, is the most effective nonpharmacological intervention for improving cognition in individuals with unspecified cognitive impairment. The second most effective intervention was computerized cognitive training, although there is a notable difference between its effectiveness and that of physical-cognitive exercise. In the case of MCI, occupational therapy was the most relevant intervention, followed by multicomponent physical exercise and physical-cognitive exercise. Nevertheless, in this case, there was less of a marked difference between the interventions. The occupational therapy interventions included in the studies were dual-task interventions with motor and functional task components.76,77 Multiple studies have emphasized the importance of combining cognitive and physical exercises in healthy and cognitively impaired populations. In a multiarm clinical trial, Norouzi et al. compared motor-motor dual-task training, motor-cognitive dual-task training, and a control group in older adults without cognitive impairment. They found that motor-cognitive dual-task training had better results for working memory and balance than motor-motor dual-task training and the control conditions.138 Another study by Gallou-Guyot et al. investigated the effect of cognitive-motor dual-task training on cognition and motor outcomes in older adults with cognitive impairment. Their review revealed that the cognitive-motor dual-task intervention had a positive impact on global cognition, executive function, memory, balance, gait, strength, and cardiorespiratory fitness.139 Dual-task training is a kind of intervention in which motor and cognitive exercises are carried out simultaneously, but combined training in which the exercises are administered sequentially (i.e., the interventions are performed separately) has also been assessed. In a meta-analysis of healthy older adults, Zhu et al. compared combined cognitive and physical interventions with control conditions (passive or active) and a cognitive intervention or physical intervention alone. They included both simultaneous and separate interventions. Compared with the control group, the combined intervention positively affected overall cognition and some specific cognitive domains (memory, executive functions, and visuospatial ability). When the combined intervention was compared with physical exercise alone, an effect on overall cognition was also found, but no difference was observed compared with the cognitive intervention alone.140 Similar findings were reported in a meta-analysis performed by Gheys et al. In this review, the authors included both healthy and cognitively impaired populations. They also found differences between the combined intervention group and both the control group and the physical activity intervention alone group, but no difference between the combined intervention group and the cognitive intervention alone group was observed. The analysis of cognitive impairment revealed a positive effect of the combined intervention on cognition compared with that of the control group.141 Our findings also support these results. We found no difference between physical-cognitive rehabilitation and computerized cognitive training in individuals with unspecified cognitive impairment or between physical-cognitive rehabilitation and both computerized cognitive training and regular cognitive training in individuals with MCI. However, a significant difference for both cognitive rehabilitation techniques was found in the dementia group. As previously mentioned, physical-cognitive interventions can be introduced simultaneously or sequentially. To define the most effective strategy, Gavelin et al. proposed a network meta-analysis to compare three combinations of physical-cognitive interventions: simultaneous, sequential, or exergaming.142 They conducted their review on both cognitively healthy and cognitively impaired older adults and found that simultaneous and sequential strategies were more effective for cognition than usual care, the sham intervention, or the physical activity intervention alone. As reported in previous studies, no difference was found with the cognitive intervention alone. However, what is the effect of physical-cognitive interventions on the brain? Specifically, both physical exercise and cognitive training have been reported to cause changes in the brain and neuroplasticity. Physical exercise has been reported to change the hippocampal volume in humans, specifically when assessed in healthy older adults.143 An increase in brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)144 and a subsequent a positive effect on learning ability has been observed with physical exercise.145 Additionally, physical exercise can modulate cognitive decline-associated the inflammation, reduce proinflammatory cytokine levels, and lead to a favorable differentiation of antiinflammatory cells.146 These changes could explain the positive impact of physical exercise on global cognition, executive function, and memory in individuals with MCI and dementia.147,148 Cognitive training affects neuroplasticity and neural networks in aging and neurodegenerative disease.149 The combination of these interventions seems to enhance their effects, and cognitive training could increase the number of surviving new neurons generated in response to physical exercise.150 Additionally, the combination of physical and cognitive training could cooperate to induce neuroplasticity and increase neurogenesis and synaptogenesis.146 Our review has strengths that must be highlighted. The high number of studies included in the analysis allowed us to take a wide perspective of the evidence reported. Further, to our knowledge, this network meta-analysis includes a comparison of the greatest number of nonpharmacological interventions. Taking these points into account, this study provides clinical evidence for the use of nonpharmacological interventions in people with cognitive decline. However, this study has several limitations that must be considered. First, we analyzed data for only global cognition and did not analyze data for specific cognitive domains. Second, total heterogeneity and some of the pairwise comparison heterogeneities were high. Finally, long-term effects were not considered as outcomes. CONCLUSION Physical-cognitive interventions were the most effective nonpharmacological treatments for improving cognition in people with cognitive impairment, including unspecified cognitive impairment, MCI, and dementia. Although minor, other interventions that showed a positive effect were computerized cognitive rehabilitation, regular cognitive intervention, and multicomponent physical exercise. These results highlight the importance of multidomain interventions in treating cognitive impairment. Future studies are needed to define the most effective physical-cognitive interventions for treating cognitive impairment. DISCLOSURES We declare no competing interests.
