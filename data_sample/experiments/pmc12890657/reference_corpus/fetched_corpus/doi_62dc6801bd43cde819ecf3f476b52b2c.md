# Long-term fracture risk in patients with Crohn's disease: a population-based study in Olmsted County, Minnesota.

## Abstract

Background & aims: Osteoporosis is common in patients with Crohn's disease, but less is known about their risk of actual fractures.

Methods: The medical records of all 238 Olmsted County, Minnesota, residents diagnosed with Crohn's disease between 1940 and 1993 were reviewed for evidence of subsequent fractures compared with a control group of county residents matched by age and sex. The risk ratio of fracture in patients relative to controls was estimated using the Cox proportional hazards regression model. The cumulative incidence of fracture following diagnosis was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Results: Sixty-three patients had 117 different fractures. The cumulative incidence of any fracture from the time of diagnosis onward was 36% at 20 years versus 32% in controls (P = 0.792). Compared with controls, the overall risk ratio for any fracture was 0.9 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.6-1.4), whereas the relative risk for an osteoporotic fracture was 1.4 (95% CI, 0.7-2.7). The risk ratio for thoracolumbar vertebral fracture was 2.2 (95% CI, 0.9-5.5). Cox proportional hazards regression identified only age as a significant clinical predictor of fracture risk (hazard ratio per 10-year increase in age, 1.3; 95% CI, 1.1-1.5). Specifically, use of corticosteroids and surgical resection did not predict risk of fracture among these unselected patients with Crohn's disease from the community.

Conclusions: In this population-based inception cohort of patients with Crohn's disease, the risk of fracture was not elevated relative to age- and sex-matched controls.

GASTROENTEROLOGY 2002;123:468-475 Abbreviations CI (confidence interval) HR (hazard ratio) RR (risk ratio) SIR (standardized incidence ratio) An increasing body of evidence suggests that patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), especially Crohn's disease (CD), are at increased risk for metabolic bone disease.1–4 Depending on the definition used and the nature of the study population, the prevalence of osteopenia among patients with IBD has been reported to be as high as 70%.5–7 Bone loss has most frequently been attributed to the adverse effects of corticosteroid therapy,8–24 although some studies have not found a correlation with corticosteroid use.5,25–29 Indeed, reduced bone mass has been observed in newly diagnosed patients,27 although one study noted no significant differences in bone mineral density between recently diagnosed patients with IBD and matched controls.7 Other postulated risk factors for bone loss in IBD include reduced weight or body mass index, insufficient vitamin D levels, cytokine release from damaged bowel, small bowel involvement, low serum vitamin K levels, and the presence or absence of certain polymorphisms in the interleukin-1 receptor antagonist and interleukin-6 genes.1–4,30,31 Moreover, bone loss is reportedly increased by small bowel resection but reversed by proctocolectomy with ileoanal anastomosis.10,17,28,32 Bowel resection and/or malabsorption might also account for greater bone loss among patients with CD compared with those with chronic ulcerative colitis,15,18,19,27,33 but this difference has not been found in all studies.5,8,10,12,28,29,34 A cross-sectional study suggested that conventional risk factors for osteoporosis are more strongly predictive of bone mineral density in patients with CD than potential disease-related factors such as use of corticosteroids, duration of disease, and bowel resection.35 Mean bone density T scores reportedly as low as −1.92 at the proximal femur5 imply a 5-fold increase in the risk of hip fracture among patients with IBD compared with normal young adults.36 However, bone density is not synonymous with fracture risk, and the actual implications of osteopenia and osteoporosis with respect to fracture risk in IBD were unknown until recently, except for isolated case reports37 and notation that fractures occurred among some cases in the various clinical series.38,39 A case-control study of hip fractures in Olmsted County, Minnesota, men showed a 2-fold increase in risk associated with IBD40; however, with only 4 cases and 2 controls exposed, it was not possible to evaluate the effects of confounding factors. A questionnaire-based study of fracture incidence among members of a Danish patient support organization and randomly selected controls suggested a 2.5-fold increase in risk of fracture among women, but not men, with CD.41 More recently, a large matched cohort study using an administrative database from Manitoba detected a 41% overall increase in fractures among patients with IBD.42 In the present study, we assess the long-term risk of fracture in a population-based cohort of Olmsted County residents with CD and attempt to distinguish the influence of corticosteroid therapy and other clinical features obtained from detailed review of comprehensive community medical records. Identification of risk factors for osteoporotic fractures among patients with CD would help target therapy that might prevent bone loss43 among the estimated 600,000 U.S. residents with CD.44 Materials and methods Olmsted County, Minnesota, is well suited for studies of disease associations such as this because comprehensive medical records for the residents are available for review and because these records are accessible through a centralized index of diagnoses made by essentially all medical care providers used by the local population.45 Most gastrointestinal and trauma care, for example, is provided by the Mayo Clinic, which has maintained a common medical record with its 2 affiliated hospitals in the community (St. Mary's and Rochester Methodist) for more than 90 years. This dossier-type record contains both inpatient and outpatient data.46 Medical records of the other providers who serve the local population, most notably the Olmsted Medical Center (Olmsted Medical Group and its affiliated Olmsted Community Hospital), are also indexed into this system and are available for use in approved studies.45 This medical records linkage system (the Rochester Epidemiology Project) permitted the identification of all health care records containing a diagnosis of IBD whether resulting from inpatient or outpatient care, death certification, or autopsy. Using study criteria described in detail elsewhere,47,48 the complete medical records of each candidate case were retrieved and reviewed for the diagnosis of CD between 1940 and 1993. The Olmsted County residents with CD had been matched by age (±2 years) and sex to controls from the county who had not had IBD. The pool of controls included all Olmsted County residents who were medically attended during the calendar year of diagnosis of each case.48 Because almost all county residents have at least one contact with the medical system in any 3-year period, this pool is essentially an enumeration of the population.45 Among potential controls, the one with the closest medical registration number was chosen. Medical registration numbers are unique to each individual and are assigned at the initial visit for each patient, so this procedure matches for the duration of documented clinical history before the index date (the date of diagnosis for case patients and the date of closest clinic visit for controls). Following approval by the institutional review boards of the Mayo Clinic and Olmsted Medical Center, the 238 matched case and control pairs were followed forward in time through their linked medical records in the community (retrospective cohort study). All cases and controls provided authorization for review of their medical records for research.49 Each subject's complete inpatient and outpatient record at each local provider of medical care was searched by trained nurse abstractors for the occurrence of any fracture. Although it is not possible in a medical record study such as this to blind the reviewers to the subject's status (case or control), it was only necessary to record the occurrence of specific fractures and this objective end point is not prone to bias. Follow-up continued until death or the most recent clinical contact documented in community medical records. The records contained the clinical history and the radiologist's report of each fracture, but the original roentgenograms were not consistently available for review. Consequently, the diagnosis of vertebral fracture was accepted on the basis of a radiologist's report of compression or collapse of one or more thoracic or lumbar vertebrae.50 Fractures were also classified according to the circumstances of the injury; by convention, falls from standing height or less were considered moderate trauma, whereas motor vehicle accidents and falls from a height were deemed severe trauma. Fractures of the hip, spine, or distal forearm that resulted from minimal or moderate trauma in patients 35 years or older were considered to represent osteoporotic fractures.51 The influence of CD on fracture risk was evaluated using 4 basic methods of analysis. In the primary analysis, the frequency of fractures in the patients was compared directly with that in the controls. This analysis used a stratified proportional hazards model with the case-control pairs forming the strata.52 In such analyses, the follow-up of both members of a pair is censored at the earliest event or follow-up date of either member. Risk ratios (RR) compared case patients and controls regarding the incidence of fractures of each type. In a secondary analysis, we calculated standardized incidence ratios (SIRs), comparing the number of fractures observed at each skeletal site (based on the first fracture of a given type per person) with the number expected among case patients and controls, separately, during their follow-up in the community. This approach provides a validation of the representativeness of the control cohort as well as an independent assessment of the extent to which fractures were increased among the case patients. Expected numbers of fractures were derived by applying age- and sex-specific incidence rates of these fractures to the age- and sex-specific person-years of follow-up among the case patients and controls separately. Incidence rates from the general population of Olmsted County were available for fractures of the proximal femur,53 vertebrae,50 distal forearm,54 proximal humerus,55 and all other sites.56 Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) for the SIRs were calculated assuming that the expected rates are fixed and the observed fractures follow a Poisson distribution.57 In the third method of analysis, the cumulative incidence of a new fracture (1 minus survival free of fracture) was projected for up to 25 years following the index date using product-limit life table methods.58 Again, follow-up was censored at the earlier of the 2 last dates of follow-up for each case-control pair. A stratified log-rank test, with the case-control pairs forming the strata, was used to compare cumulative incidence of fracture. Life table methods were used to assess survival relative to controls, and the survival curves were compared using the log-rank test statistic.59 In the final approach, Cox proportional hazards regression models,52 which do not incorporate the population-expected rates, were used to assess the impact of various covariates (e.g., age, use of corticosteroids) on the subsequent risk of fracture among the case patients. Univariate relationships between the risk of specific fractures and each clinical characteristic under consideration were first assessed. Stepwise methods with forward selection and backward elimination were then used to choose independent variables for the final models. The dependent variable was time until the first new fracture, and the independent variables were the clinical characteristics. For the final multiple models and for the univariate models, the assumption of proportional hazards was examined and was not violated for the variables considered. Discussion The prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis among patients with CD is highly variable across studies, and this seems to be influenced in part by the method of bone mineral density measurement and study design itself. However, it is important to remember that bone mineral density is only an intermediate end point. The primary end point of clinical interest is fracture, and surprisingly few studies have measured the cumulative risk of fracture among cohorts of patients with CD. In this population-based study, based on an inception cohort of Olmsted County residents with CD, we were unable to detect a significantly increased overall risk of fracture relative to age- and sex-matched controls. Although the cumulative incidence of any fracture was 36% after 20 years, the absolute risk of fracture among controls was nearly as high (32%). The only clinical variable significantly associated with overall risk of fracture was age. Neither duration nor cumulative dose of corticosteroids nor a history of intestinal resection increased the risk of fracture. Our results differed from those of the other 2 large studies of fracture risk in CD.41,42 The discrepancy could possibly be explained by differences in source populations, sample size, fracture ascertainment, and analytic methods. Using questionnaires sent to members of a Colitis/Crohn's Association and randomly selected population controls, Danish investigators identified 141 fractures among 383 patients with CD and 279 fractures among 635 controls, including fractures that were reported both before and after the date of diagnosis.41 Although the analysis is obscure, these investigators reported a 2.5-fold increased risk of fracture among women with CD but no increase (RR, 0.6) among the men with CD compared with controls. When analyzed by individual fracture type, there were significant elevations in the risk of fractures of the spine, feet and toes, and “other” (ribs and pelvis) fractures.41 However, subjects were recruited through a patient support organization, and it is likely that patients with higher CD activity were overrepresented. In addition, fractures were ascertained by questionnaire so that minimally symptomatic or asymptomatic fractures may not have been reported. In the Manitoba study, a large cohort of patients with CD was matched 1:10 with age- and sex-matched controls without IBD from a large administrative database, and subsequent fractures in both groups were ascertained based on ICD-9-CM codes from hospital discharges and outpatient visits.42 Altogether, 184 fractures of the hip, spine, wrist, and ribs were observed among the cases over 21,340 person-years of follow-up, for a crude annual incidence rate of 86.2 per 10,000. This contrasts with an overall incidence of these same fractures in the present study of 155.7 per 10,000, suggesting substantial underascertainment of fractures in Manitoba. Nonetheless, the RR among patients with CD compared with controls in Manitoba was 1.36, and this increase was statistically significant given the enormous sample size. The RR for these same fractures in Olmsted County was a nearly identical 1.3 (95% CI, 0.7–2.4), but we only had 78% power (α = 0.05) to detect statistical significance at that level. In the Manitoba patients with CD, RRs were elevated for spine (1.54), hip (1.47), and wrist (1.30) but not rib (1.29) fractures.42 Vertebral fractures also accounted for the greatest excess among patients with CD in Olmsted County. Indeed, they were responsible for the entire excess of osteoporotic fractures in case patients versus controls. Although the risk of thoracolumbar vertebral fracture among case patients was twice as high as that of controls, this did not reach statistical significance, perhaps due to the small sample size. Compared with expected incidence rates for clinically evident vertebral fractures in the community, there was an apparent increase in vertebral fracture risk among controls as well as case patients. Although Bernstein et al. did not believe it to be the case,42 this raises the possibility of ascertainment bias if patients were subjected to closer surveillance following a diagnosis of CD. Although we have no evidence that this is so, the potential exists because the incidence rates used to calculate expected numbers (for both case patients and controls) were based on clinically recognized vertebral fractures, which provide an underestimate of vertebral fracture occurrence in the population.50 An alternative approach is to use incidence rates derived from prevalence data, available for women 50 years of age and older,60 but these overestimate the incidence of vertebral fracture in the general population because some vertebral deformities do not represent actual fractures. If the latter rates are substituted in the analysis, however, the RR of vertebral fracture is no longer elevated among either case patients (SIR, 0.9; 95% CI, 0.4–1.8) or controls (SIR, 0.9; 95% CI, 0.4–1.8). Although many studies have examined the association between bone density and corticosteroid use in IBD with conflicting results,5,8–29 there are virtually no data regarding fracture risk and corticosteroid therapy. In the Danish questionnaire-based study, the risk of fracture among the 308 patients with CD who had ever been treated with corticosteroids was not significantly elevated relative to that of controls.38 However, the risk seemed to increase with increasing duration of systematic corticosteroid use, such that the risk was elevated between 50% and 3-fold among those who had been treated with corticosteroids for at least 5 years.38 The influence of corticosteroid use on fracture risk was not evaluated in the Manitoba study.39 In the present study, neither high dose nor long duration of corticosteroid use was associated with a significant increase in fracture risk. We had excellent statistical power (99%) to detect a 2-fold increase in risk of fracture among patients with CD in association with use of corticosteroids but limited power (73%) to detect a 50% increase in risk. Indeed, we observed a mildly reduced risk of fracture among those patients treated with corticosteroid therapy for more than 6 months. It is important to note, however, that only 65 patients (27%) were treated with corticosteroids for more than 6 months. This may reflect a historical institutional bias at Mayo Clinic toward early surgical therapy. One of the strengths of our study was that the cohort was population based and therefore reflects the wide spectrum of clinical severity of CD and its attendant complications seen among unselected patients in the community. In addition, the controls were selected from an enumeration of the entire Olmsted County population and therefore should have been representative of the local population generally.45 This belief is supported by the observation that, with the exception of vertebral fractures, the 50 fractures observed in the controls over their follow-up in the community were almost identical to the 49.2 fractures expected. In addition, the patients with CD were followed forward from the date of their initial diagnosis for up to 51 years (mean, 15.4 years). Fractures were ascertained using the resources of the Rochester Epidemiology Project, which allowed access to all outpatient and inpatient records (including radiology reports) so that outcomes could be assessed comparably in case patients and controls. Except for vertebral fractures, some of which are never diagnosed,50 the ascertainment of clinically evident fractures is believed to be complete.56 Finally, potential risk factors could be evaluated at a level of clinical detail unavailable in administrative databases. Although the relatively small size of our cohort precluded an ability to identify modest increases in the risk of specific types of fractures, our overall results indicate that fractures do not represent a substantial problem for most patients with CD. Moreover, we were unable to identify specific clinical risk factors that might increase the likelihood of fractures, although risk factors for falls could not be fully assessed and special measurements such as bone densitometry were not routinely performed. Additional studies will be needed to define the subgroups of patients with CD in which efforts to diagnose and treat osteoporosis are cost-effective.
