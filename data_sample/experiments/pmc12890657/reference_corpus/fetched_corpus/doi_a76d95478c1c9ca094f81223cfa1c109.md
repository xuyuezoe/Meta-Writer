# High Risk of Fractures Within 7 Years of Diagnosis in Asian Patients With Inflammatory Bowel Diseases.

## Abstract

Background & aims: In this nationwide population-based study, we investigated the risk of vertebral and hip fractures in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).

Methods: Using data from the Korean National Health Insurance claims database gathered between 2007 and 2016, we calculated the incidence rate ratios (IRRs) of vertebral and hip fractures in patients with newly diagnosed IBD (n = 18,228; 64.1% male, 65.9% ulcerative colitis) compared with an age- and sex-matched control population (matching ratio, 1:10; n = 186,871).

Results: During a median follow-up period of 4.5 years, the incidence rate and IRR of vertebral and hip fractures in patients with IBD were 2.88 per 1000 person-years and 1.24 (95% CI, 1.08-1.42), respectively. The cumulative risk of vertebral and hip fractures in IBD patients was 0.6%, 1.4%, and 1.9% at 2, 5, and 7 years after diagnosis, respectively, and this risk of fracture in IBD patients was higher than that in matched controls (P = .002). The use of corticosteroids further increased the risk of fractures in IBD patients (IRR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.13-1.65) compared with matched controls. The risk of fractures was significantly higher in patients with Crohn's disease (CD) (IRR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.19-2.04) than in matched controls, and this risk remained higher in patients with CD without corticosteroid exposure (IRR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.12-2.34). The risk of fracture increased with age and was particularly high in females and in those with comorbidities.

Conclusions: The risk of fractures was significantly high in newly diagnosed IBD patients, especially in those with CD regardless of corticosteroid exposure.

Findings In this non-Western population-based study, the risk of vertebral and hip fractures was notably high in IBD patients, especially in those with Crohn’s disease who were diagnosed with IBD within the past 7 years. The use of corticosteroids further increased the risk of fractures in patients with IBD. Implications for patient care In non-Western patients with IBD, those with Crohn’s disease may particularly benefit from frequent surveillance of bone density and regular vitamin D supplementation to prevent bone fracture. The clinical significance of bone fracture in public health has been growing worldwide because of increased mortality related to this condition.1 Although fractures commonly occur as a result of aging or menopause, approximately half of the fracture cases are related to secondary osteoporosis caused by chronic diseases and/or medications.2 Given the reversibility of secondary osteoporosis, it is important to identify and correct the causes of secondary osteoporosis to maintain bone health and reduce unnecessary therapy for osteoporosis.2 Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which consists of Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic relapsing condition that leads to significant morbidity and disability.3 Chronic inflammation of the intestine and subsequent decrease in the absorption capacity might cause various nutritional problems including calcium and vitamin D deficiencies.4,5 Medications for the treatment of IBD including corticosteroids and immunomodulators also might affect bone metabolism adversely.6 In addition, the aging of the IBD population might contribute to further increased risk of comorbidities such as fractures.7 Accordingly, many clinical studies have been performed to investigate the possible link between IBD and fracture.8–10 Although the fracture risk in UC still is under debate, several studies have suggested that IBD, especially CD, may harbor increased risks of fracture, and identified various risk factors such as age, sex, comorbidities, and medications.11,12 However, there has been a paucity of data on the risk of fracture in Asian patients with IBD, whose importance is growing because of the increase in the incidence and prevalence of IBD in this region.13 We therefore investigated the risk of fractures in IBD patients and comparative groups without IBD by using data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) claims databases in South Korea. Materials and Methods Study Design This study was a nationwide, population-based, retrospective, cohort study that compared the incidence of vertebral and hip fractures in patients with IBD and in a non-IBD reference population in South Korea. The Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (Seoul, South Korea) approved this study (2019-0502). Database We used data from the HIRA claims database gathered between January 2007 and December 2016.14–16 The Korean National Health Insurance (KNHI) is mandated by the law to be provided to approximately 97% of the nation's 50 million population. The HIRA database consists of a vast amount of data on all KNHI claims, including patient demographic characteristics as well as diagnoses using the Korean Classification of Diseases, 6th edition, and the International Classification of Diseases and related health problems, 10th revision. The prescription information includes prescription drugs (according to the official code of the HIRA), prescription dates, supply days, and administration routes. The database also provides registration of rare incurable diseases (RIDs), including IBDs. The RID system is highly reliable because it requires the patient to be certified by a doctor through specified diagnostic criteria that include histopathologic examinations before registration in the system. Patients registered in this system can benefit from having more than 90% of their medical expenses covered by the Korean government. Study Population Based on our previous studies,14–16 IBD patients were defined as those satisfying all of the following criteria: (1) diagnostic codes for UC (K51.0–51.9) or CD (K50.0–50.9) in the principal or subsidiary diagnostic field, (2) RID registration codes for UC (V131) or CD (V130), and (3) a prescription for IBD-related medications. To exclude prevalent IBD cases, incident IBD cases between 2010 and 2014 were included after establishing a washout period of 3 years (2007–2009). The prescription for IBD-related medications was defined as the prescription of either oral or topical mesalamine for at least 1 month, or at least 1 prescription of topical steroids, thiopurines (azathioprine or 6-mercaptopurine), or anti–tumor necrosis factor (TNF) agents (infliximab or adalimumab). We did not include a prescription of systemic corticosteroids in our definition of IBD considering the widespread use of these medications for other indications. As a result, 18,897 patients with incident IBD were selected, of whom 18,228 were subject to matching except for patients whose diagnosis changed during the course of the study and those with a history of fractures before the index date. The sex- and 5-year age-matched non-IBD reference population was extracted during the same study period at a matching ratio of 1:10 (Figure 1). Our algorithm for identifying incident IBD cases was validated in a nationwide validation study from Korea,17 which showed good performance in terms of sensitivity (90.0%), specificity (98.0%), positive predictive value (97.8%), negative predictive value (90.7%), and error rate (6.0%). Data Collection We collected the information on the insurance type classification code, sex, age, medical diagnoses, and prescription details.18 We classified the patients into 4 groups according to age, as follows: A1 (16 y or younger), A2 (17–40 y), A3-1 (41–60 y), and A3-2 (>60 y) in consideration of the Montreal classification.19 We further divided the A3 group into A3-1 and A3-2 considering the increased risk of fractures with decreasing bone density in the elderly (age, >60 y).20 We obtained data on comorbidities including hypertension (I10-15), diabetes (E10-14), dyslipidemia (E78), stroke (I60-64), chronic kidney disease (N18, I120, E142), osteoporosis (M80-82), and anemia (D50-53).21 The information on comorbidities has been corrected based on the baseline point of time. For exposure to IBD medications, thiopurines (azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine) and anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab) are reflected as in just use, otherwise on corticosteroids (including betamethasone, deflazacort, dexamethasone, hydrocortisone, methylprednisolone, and prednisolone) is only effective for those prescribed for more than 7 days. This is because corticosteroids often are prescribed for various diseases and it is difficult to selectively check the actual use for IBD if the prescription within 7 days is fully reflected. To determine the effect of the cumulative steroid dose on the risk of fracture, the cumulative steroid dose during the study period was calculated using the sum of 5 mg prednisolone equivalent dose for all types of corticosteroids via oral and/or intravenous administrations, which then was multiplied by the duration of the prescription (Supplementary Table 1). The cumulative capacity of corticosteroids was divided into 4 groups (0 mg, 1–1500 mg, 1501–3000 mg, and >3000 mg) in each group of IBD, CD, and UC.9 Outcomes The primary outcome of the current study was the event of vertebral and hip fractures, the representative types of osteoporotic or frailty fractures resulting from low-energy trauma,22,23 as defined by the following International Classification of Diseases and related health problems, 10th revision codes: thoracic spine (S220, S221) and lumbar spine (S320) for vertebral fractures, and femur neck (S720), peritrochanter (S721), and other femur parts including the subtrochanter, shaft, lower end, and others (S72X) for hip fractures.24 Considering that corticosteroid treatment is the most common cause of secondary osteoporosis,25 we also examined the associations between corticosteroids and the risk of vertebral and hip fractures by comparing IBD patients and the reference population. Moreover, we investigated the risk factors of vertebral and hip fractures among IBD patients. All patients were followed up until they experienced vertebral or hip fractures, or until the end of the study period (December 31, 2016), whichever came first. Statistical Analysis Methods regarding statistical analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Open table in a new tab Discussion In this nationwide, population-based, cohort study in South Korea, we found that the risk for vertebral and hip fractures was significantly higher in IBD patients than in the reference population. Moreover, patients with IBD who used steroids showed further increases in the risk of vertebral and hip fractures, although there was no significant correlation between the cumulative dose of corticosteroids and the risk of vertebral and hip fractures. This was a large study to evaluate the risk of hip and spine fractures in Asian patients with IBD. There was a study from Taiwan that evaluated the risk of pathologic fractures in IBD patients at a population-based level26; in this Taiwanese study, the risks of osteoporosis and pathologic fracture were highest in patients requiring hospitalization for IBD with an IRR of 24.7 compared with a non-IBD cohort.26 However, the number of fracture cases was too small (11 cases) to evaluate the risk of fracture according to the fracture site and demographic characteristics. The most important finding of the current study was that compared with the matched reference population, patients with CD had an approximately 56% higher risk for vertebral and hip fractures, whereas patients with UC did not have a significantly higher risk. This finding seems to be comparable with previous reports from Western countries in which the risks of hip fractures and any fractures were higher in IBD patients8–10; specifically, these studies also reported higher risks of hip or any fracture in CD patients compared with those in UC patients. In a recent large-scale study from Sweden that included 83,435 patients with IBD, the relative risks of hip fracture were 1.7 in CD patients and 1.3 in UC patients, and those of any fracture were 1.2 for CD patients and 1.2 for UC patients.9 In addition, a population-based study from the United States showed that the risk of any osteoporotic fracture in UC patients was not significantly higher than that in matched controls, with a HR of 1.3 (95% CI, 0.6–2.8).27 Although the reason for this difference in the risk of fractures between UC and CD patients is unclear, it may be owing to the difference in the onset of disease, the main location of disease involvement, and inflammatory mediators.28,29 As for the type of fractures, IBD patients had higher risks for vertebral fractures rather than hip fractures, which likely is owing to the small number of events in hip fractures, which led to insufficient statistical power. The structural and metabolic differences between the 2 sites also partially may explain the discrepancy, because spines mostly have a spongy, cancellous architecture while femurs have a cortical architecture.30 Furthermore, because hip fractures frequently occur in the elderly, older 70 years of age,31 the lack of association between IBD and hip fractures could be attributed to the relatively young age of our study population. Another notable finding of the current study was that the use of corticosteroids increased the risk of vertebral and hip fractures by 37% in IBD patients compared with the reference population. This additional risk of fractures was more notable in UC patients than in CD patients because the risk of fracture in CD patients without corticosteroid exposure still was higher than that in the matched reference population; this suggests that CD itself may be a risk factor for fractures independent from corticosteroid exposure. In a recent meta-analysis that included 470,541 patients with IBD, patients with fractures were more likely to take corticosteroids compared with those without fractures, with an odds ratio of 1.47.32 However, the effect of the cumulative dose of corticosteroids on the risk of fracture in IBD patients still is debatable, likely owing to the difficulties in differentiating the effects of corticosteroids from those of the disease activity of IBD itself. One meta-analysis showed that the highest fracture risk was found in patients with high cumulative corticosteroid doses.33 However, we did not observe a dose-dependent relationship in vertebral and hip fractures in IBD patients. Also, a population-based study from Sweden reported that the correlation between the dose of corticosteroids and fracture risk in IBD patients was observed only in elderly patients (age, >60 y).9 We found that the risk factors for fractures in IBD patients included age, sex, and several comorbidities, especially osteoporosis, which is similar to the findings in previous studies.9,34 The fracture risk was especially more evident in the elderly populations (age, >60 y) than in younger populations when compared with matched controls. There may be several plausible mechanisms explaining this increased risk of fractures in such subsets of patients with IBD, including the inflammatory mechanism itself and the effect of medications for comorbidities.34 The main strength of the present study was the large number of IBD patients included and the 1:10 matching with a non-IBD reference population, which enabled us to identify a large number of fracture events, calculate precise risk estimates, and perform several subgroup analyses according to age and comorbidities. However, the possibility of underestimation of the risk of fractures should be considered because we only included the incident cases of IBD with a median follow-up period of 4.5 years, during which the patients might have had a less-severe disease in the early course of their disease. Another noteworthy strength of this study was that we used the KNHI claims database, which covers 95% of the entire Korean population and thus presents a representative and comprehensive data set. The limitations of this study were as follows. First, we could not evaluate the effect of lifestyles such as smoking on the risk of fractures, even though smoking is a well-known risk factor for osteoporosis and fractures.35 Second, we could not collect data on the disease activity or disease location/extent from this database. Third, data on other conditions such as anemia and osteoporosis were not analyzed by checking the relevant laboratory data. Fourth, we could not evaluate the effect of thiopurines and anti-TNF agents on the risk of fractures because the number of related fractures was too small to perform a statistical analysis, although some studies suggested a possible link between these medications and the risk of fractures in IBD patients.36,37 Fifth, regarding corticosteroid exposure, we could not stratify the matched control population according to the status of corticosteroid exposure along with the age- and sex-matching process, which may have led to a dilution of the effect of corticosteroid exposure on the risk of fractures. Lastly, because frequency matching was performed for the entire IBD patients, the matching ratio was not perfect in patients with UC or CD. In conclusion, by analyzing a nationwide population-based cohort in South Korea, we found that the risk for vertebral and hip fractures was high in IBD patients, especially CD patients, compared with the general population. Based on the findings of the current study, we suggest that CD patients in particular may benefit from frequent surveillance of bone density and regular vitamin D supplementation to prevent bone fractures.
