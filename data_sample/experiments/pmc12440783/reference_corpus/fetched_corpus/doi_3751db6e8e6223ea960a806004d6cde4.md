# Increased CRHR1 expression on monocytes from patients with AA enables a pro‐inflammatory response to corticotrophin‐releasing hormone

## Abstract

Stress may play a key role in alopecia areata (AA), though the exact interactions of stress with AA remain undefined. Corticotropin‐releasing hormone (CRH), the proximal regulator of the stress axis, has been recognized as an immunomodulatory factor in tissues and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). We used multicolour flow cytometry to identify receptor CRHR1 expression on PBMC subsets in AA patients ( n = 54) and controls ( n = 66). We found that CRHR1 was primarily expressed by circulating monocytes. CRHR1 expression on monocytes was enhanced in AA compared with controls (3.17% vs. 1.44%, p = 0.002, chi‐squared test). AA incidence was correlated to elevated CD14 + monocyte numbers ( R = 0.092, p = 0.036) and markedly independently correlated with increased CRHR1 expression ( R = 0.215, p = 0.027). High CRHR1 expression was significantly related to chronic AA (disease duration >1 year; p = 0.003, chi‐squared test), and large lesion area (AA area >25%; p = 0.049, chi‐squared test). We also observed enhanced percentages of active monocytes and reduced CD16 + CD3− NK cell numbers in AA patients' PBMCs ( p = 0.010; 0.025, respectively). In vitro CRH treatment of PBMCs and human monocyte cell line THP‐1 promoted CD86 upregulation. The findings imply that stress‐related factors CRH and CRHR1 contribute to AA development and progression where higher CRHR1 expression is associated with chronic AA and larger lesions.

The findings imply that stress-related factors CRH and CRHR1 contribute to AA development and progression where higher CRHR1 expression is associated with chronic AA and larger lesions. 1 INTRODUCTION Alopecia areata (AA) is a stress-associated autoimmune disease,1-5 in which CD4+ T cells assist a CD8+ T cell-mediated attack on hair follicles that leads to hair loss.6 There is a high prevalence of psychiatric comorbidity among patients with AA7 and AA-affected subjects have differential responses in emotion regulation or psychological stress coping.8 However, how stress might affect the immune disruption of hair follicles in AA remains unknown. Comprehending the underlying interactions between stress signals and immune activities could be significant for understanding this recalcitrant disease. The core stress response system consists of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system. Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and norepinephrine (NE) are the main central regulators of the HPA axis and LC-NE systems, respectively.9, 10 During stress, CRH secretion promotes the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and glucocorticoids (GCs),11 which mediate adaption to stress through ubiquitously distributed intracellular receptors, and also regulate termination of the stress response through negative feedback.12 The initiation of an escape response is preceded by an increase in the activity of CRH-positive neurons.13 In addition to the central hormone-mediated response to stress, the concept of a ‘cutaneous HPA axis’ has been proposed as many elements of the HPA axis have been identified in skin and hair follicles.14 Further, leukocytes possess functional receptors for neuroendocrine hormones and are able to synthesize biologically active neuroendocrine peptide hormones.15 As such, paracrine/autocrine signalling may exert effects on the local immune system.16, 17 Studies have established that stressful experiences alter features of an immune response.18, 19 A defective HPA axis function diminishes susceptibility to infections, but increases susceptibility to autoimmune or aseptic inflammatory diseases.20, 21 Blunted HPA responses have been found in patients with autoimmune diseases including rheumatic diseases and Sjogren's syndrome.22 Research of the HPA axis in AA-affected C3H/HeJ mice showed normal CRH gene expression in the hypothalamus and pituitary, but increased CRH, ACTH, GC and their receptors in skin and skin draining lymph nodes.23 In AA patients, plasma melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH) and cortisol production are not disturbed,24 but modified expression of CRH, ACTH and their receptors have been reported.25-27 The modulated component expression of the peripheral HPA axis suggests that an imbalanced central and/or peripheral HPA axis may be involved in immune/inflammatory activities in AA. Whether and how CRH modulates immune cells in AA remains unexplored. Equally, given the relatively low level of CRHR1 expression in control PBMCs, a lack of response to exogenous CRH in culture might be expected. 4 CONCLUSIONS The studies of stress hormone function on immune cells in AA are gradually growing. Here, we found increased expression of CRHR1 in PBMCs, primarily on monocytes, implying an enhanced perception and response to stress events in AA patients' monocytes. The elevated number of monocytes and reduced NK cells in PBMC populations highlight the important roles of these two kinds of cells which may serve as markers of an inflammatory response in AA. CRH stimulation promotes the activities of monocytes and their associated molecules, which may be one of the earliest immune responses to stress for AA patients. Further studies are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which stress hormones such as CRH and ACTH function and affect the immune system.
