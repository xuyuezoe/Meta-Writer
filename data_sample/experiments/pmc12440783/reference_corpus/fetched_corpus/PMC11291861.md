# Explore the genetic exposure to alopecia areata.

## Abstract

Background: Alopecia areata is an autoimmune hair loss disorder with an incompletely understood etiology. Although trace elements, serum metabolites, and inflammatory factors are implicated in the disease, the potential causal relationships between these factors and alopecia areata require further investigation.

Methods: This study employed Mendelian randomization (MR), utilizing data from genome-wide association studies, to explore the causal relationships between 15 trace elements, 1400 serum metabolites, and 91 inflammatory factors and alopecia areata. The analysis was conducted using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method complemented by various sensitivity analyses, including Cochran's Q test, MR-Egger regression intercept test, MR-PRESSO global test, and leave-one-out analysis, to assess the robustness of the results.

Results: MR analysis indicated a negative correlation between copper levels and the risk of developing alopecia areata (odds ratio = 0.86, 95% confidence interval: 0.75-0.99, p = 0.041). Additionally, causal relationships were identified between 15 serum metabolites and 6 inflammatory factors and the risk of alopecia areata (IVW, all p values < 0.05).

Conclusion: This study provides genetic evidence of the relationships between trace elements, serum metabolites, and alopecia areata, underscoring the potential value of targeted therapeutic strategies and preventive measures. Future research should expand to diverse populations and further explore the specific roles of these biomarkers in the disease mechanism.

## INTRODUCTION

INTRODUCTION

Alopecia areata is an immune‐mediated hair loss disorder characterized by autoimmune destruction of hair follicles, leading to rapid hair loss. 1 , 2 , 3 The precise pathogenesis of alopecia areata remains unclear, but studies indicate that genetics, environmental factors, and immune dysregulation collectively influence its development. 4 , 5 Trace elements, serum metabolites, and inflammatory cytokines play roles in the onset and progression of many diseases, yet there is a lack of research exploring their association with the risk of developing alopecia areata.

Trace elements are indispensable components of the cellular enzyme system and antioxidant defense mechanisms, involved in a variety of biological processes such as energy production, connective tissue synthesis, and neural transmission, and they are also crucial for maintaining the redox balance of immune cells. 6 , 7 , 8 Consequently, variations in the levels of trace elements may directly impact the function of immune cells, thereby influencing the occurrence of autoimmune diseases.

Serum metabolites reflect the overall state of metabolic activity within the body and can provide direct evidence of how metabolic pathways are altered in disease states. 9 , 10 For instance, certain lipid metabolites play roles in inflammation and immune regulation, and their abnormalities may indicate the presence of pathological conditions. 11 , 12 , 13 By monitoring the levels of these metabolites, a deeper understanding of the metabolic alterations in alopecia areata and their association with disease progression can be achieved.

Immune cells have been shown to play an important role in the pathogenesis of various diseases, and it has been reported that changes in immune cells, such as monocytes, may be a potential risk factor for the development of alopecia areata. 14 Immune cells secrete a large number of different inflammatory factors simultaneously during disease progression, among which a variety of cytokines and chemokines, such as interleukins and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), play a central role in maintaining immune homeostasis and regulating inflammatory responses. 15 , 16 , 17 In alopecia areata, the abnormal expression of these inflammatory factors is considered a key mechanism leading to the immune attack on hair follicles. 18 Therefore, elucidating the causal relationship between these factors and alopecia areata is crucial for understanding the disease mechanism and developing therapeutic strategies.

Mendelian randomization (MR) utilizes genetic variants as natural experiments to test the causal relationships between biomarkers and alopecia areata. 19 , 20 Compared to traditional observational studies, MR is considered a more reliable method for causal inference due to its ability to reduce confounding variables and avoid reverse causation errors. 21 This study aims to apply this approach to validate the relationship between common trace elements, serum metabolites, and inflammatory factors with the risk of developing alopecia areata from a genetic perspective, with the goal of uncovering new biological mechanisms and therapeutic targets.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This study utilizes a MR approach, examining common trace elements, serum metabolites, and inflammatory markers as exposure factors to explore their potential causal relationship with the risk of developing alopecia areata. The MR design adheres to three assumptions: first, there must be a strong association between single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and the exposure; second, SNPs should not be associated with the outcome through confounding factors; third, SNPs should not directly affect the outcome (Figure 1A,B ). This methodology has been further elaborated in previous research and is based on published studies and publicly available summary data, eliminating the need for additional ethical approval or participant consent.

Data sources of 15 trace elements and genetic instrumental variables

To avoid population overlap between exposure factors and outcome variables, this study included trace element datasets solely from the UK Biobank (ukb) and IEU databases ( https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/ ), yielding datasets for 15 common trace elements. All data are derived from the European population and include both males and females. The datasets for Copper (ieu‐a‐1073), Zinc (ieu‐a‐1079), and Selenium (ieu‐a‐1077) each encompass 2603 participants and contain 2 543 646, 2 543 617, and 2 543 610 SNPs, respectively. The datasets for Calcium (ukb‐b‐8951), Carotene (ukb‐b‐16202), Folate (ukb‐b‐11349), Iron (ukb‐b‐2044), Magnesium (ukb‐b‐7372), Potassium (ukb‐b‐17881), Vitamin B12 (ukb‐b‐19524), Vitamin B6 (ukb‐b‐7864), Vitamin C (ukb‐b‐19390), Vitamin D (ukb‐b‐18593), and Vitamin E (ukb‐b‐6888) include 64 979 participants and contain 9 851 867 SNPs. The Vitamin A dataset (ukb‐b‐9596) includes 460 351 participants with 9 851 867 SNPs (Table 1 ).

To avoid analysis bias caused by strong linkage disequilibrium among SNPs, the genetic instrumental variables (IVs) screening criteria were: (1) p 10 000 kb between every two genes; (3) r 2 threshold of LD between genes 10 was defined as reliable and valid IVs. The F ‐statistic is calculated as: F = R 2 ( N −K−1)/[K(1−R2)], K is the number of SNP‐exposure association, and N is the sample size for the SNP‐exposure association. 22

Data sources of inflammatory factors and genetic instrumental variables

The genetic predictors for 91 circulating inflammatory regulatory factors come from the most comprehensive cytokine‐related GWAS meta‐analysis, involving 11 independent cohorts with a total of 14 824 participants, all of European descent. SNPs were screened based on p 0.05 indicating the absence of horizontal pleiotropy.

Statistical methods

IVW from the TwoSampleMR package were used for the analyses. All data analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) and the R packages “TwosampleMR” (https:// mrcieu.github). Differences were considered statistically significant only when the p ‐value < 0.05. 25

## RESULT

RESULT

Genetic instrument variables

From the 15 trace element datasets, a total of 198 SNPs were identified as IVs, meeting the strong instrument assumption for MR studies (Table S1 ). Concurrently, 1523 SNPs associated with serum metabolites and 2613 SNPs linked to inflammatory markers were identified as IVs to explore the potential causal relationships with the risk of developing alopecia areata (Tables S2 and S 3 ).

Causal relationship between 15 trace elements and alopecia areata

The IVW analysis indicates a potential inverse correlation between copper levels and the risk of developing alopecia areata (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.75–0.99, p = 0.041) (Figure 2A ). The heterogeneity analysis revealed no heterogeneity ( p = 0.922), and no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected (egger_intercept = 0.01, se = 0.03, p = 0.749) (Table 2 ). Both the funnel plot and the leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis demonstrate that the results are robust (Figure 2B‐E ).

Causal relationship between serum metabolites and alopecia areata

The IVW analysis indicates that levels of Gamma‐glutamylglutamine (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.16–0.76, p = 0.007), X‐12707 (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.32–0.93, p = 0.026), and (N(1) + N(8))‐acetylspermidine (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.41–0.91, p = 0.015) may be inversely correlated with the risk of developing alopecia areata. Conversely, levels of N‐acetylarginine (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.03–1.65, p = 0.025) and 12 other serum metabolites may be positively correlated with the risk of alopecia areata (Figure 3A,B ).

Causal relationship between inflammatory factors and alopecia areata

The IVW analysis shows that levels of C‐C motif chemokine 23 (OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.39–0.81, p = 0.001), monocyte chemoattractant protein‐3 (OR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.42–0.97, p = 0.035), and Cystatin D (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.61–0.99, p = 0.042) may be inversely correlated with the risk of developing alopecia areata. Conversely, levels of transforming growth factor‐alpha (OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.01–2.94, p = 0.044), interleukin‐2 receptor subunit beta (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.01–3.19, p = 0.044), and interferon gamma (OR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.34–3.71, p = 0.001) may be positively correlated with the risk of developing alopecia areata (Figure 3C,D ).

## DISCUSSION

DISCUSSION

This study employing MR analysis reveals potential causal relationships between trace elements, serum metabolites, and inflammatory markers and the risk of developing alopecia areata. The study found that an increase in copper levels is inversely correlated with the risk of alopecia areata, aligning with the observed regulatory effects of copper on immune function in another research. Copper is considered a significant antioxidant, potentially protecting hair follicles from immune‐mediated damage by reducing the production of free radicals. 26 , 27

Our data also indicate that specific serum metabolites, such as Gamma‐glutamylglutamine and X‐12707, are inversely correlated with the risk of developing alopecia areata. These metabolites may reflect changes in specific metabolic pathways within the body, providing new insights into the pathogenesis of alopecia areata. For example, Gamma‐glutamylglutamine, a key intermediate in glutamine transport and metabolism, may influence hair follicle health by regulating amino acid metabolism and immune responses. 28 , 29

Additionally, the reduction in certain inflammatory markers such as C‐C motif chemokine 23 and Monocyte chemoattractant protein‐3 is associated with a decreased risk of developing alopecia areata. These factors typically participate in the recruitment and activation of immune cells, and their reduced levels may reflect a diminished inflammatory response in patients with alopecia areata. 30 , 31 This finding emphasizes the potential value that modulating specific inflammatory pathways may have in preventing or treating pemphigus vulgaris.

It is noteworthy that our results also show that certain factors, such as transforming growth factor‐alpha and Interleukin‐2 receptor submit beta, are positively correlated with an increased risk of alopecia areata. This suggests that these inflammatory markers may play a role in promoting inflammation and immune‐mediated destruction of hair follicles in the pathogenesis of alopecia areata.

This study has several limitations. First, the limitations of genetic tools: Although MR analysis reduces the interference of confounding factors, this method relies on the effectiveness of genetic tools. The SNPs we used as instrumental variables may not be sufficient to cover all relevant biological pathways, and there is a risk that the weak instrument assumption for these tools may not be met. Second, the representativeness of the population: The study data mainly come from European populations, so the generalizability of the results may be limited to specific ethnicities and geographical locations. The genetic background and environmental factors of different populations may affect the relationship between these biomarkers and alopecia areata. Lastly, data interpretation: Although the association between some biomarkers and the risk of alopecia areata is statistically significant, the causal mechanism remains unclear. Further experimental studies are needed to explain how these biomarkers affect hair follicle health and immune status.

Future studies should include a broader range of populations, including individuals of different ethnicities and geographic locations, to validate and expand the findings of this study and to explore how environmental factors and lifestyle interact with genetic factors to influence the risk of alopecia areata. Meanwhile, in‐depth laboratory studies of key biomarkers affecting the risk of alopecia areata, such as cell culture and animal models, should be conducted to reveal their specific roles in hair follicle immune regulation.

## CONCLUSION

CONCLUSION

This study confirms the causal links between trace elements, serum metabolites, and inflammatory factors in alopecia areata risk using MR. Elevated copper levels reduce alopecia risk, suggesting its protective role in autoimmune hair loss. Specific metabolites inversely related to alopecia highlight metabolic pathway alterations in disease development. In contrast, increased levels of certain inflammatory markers such as transforming growth factor‐alpha and interleukin‐2 receptor submit beta heighten alopecia risk by promoting inflammatory hair follicle damage.
