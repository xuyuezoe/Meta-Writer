# Alopecia areata profiling shows TH1, TH2, and IL-23 cytokine activation without parallel TH17/TH22 skewing.

## Abstract

Background: Alopecia areata (AA) is a common T cell-mediated disorder with limited therapeutics. A molecular profile of cytokine pathways in AA tissues is lacking. Although studies have focused on TH1/IFN-γ responses, several observations support a shared genetic background between AA and atopy.

Objective: We sought to define the AA scalp transcriptome and associated biomarkers with comparisons with atopic dermatitis (AD) and psoriasis.

Methods: We performed microarray and RT-PCR profiling of 27 lesional and 17 nonlesional scalp samples from patients with AA for comparison with normal scalp samples (n = 6). AA gene expression was also compared with samples from patients with lesional or nonlesional AD and those with psoriasis. A fold change of greater than 1.5 and a false discovery rate of less than 0.05 were used for differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Results: We established the AA transcriptomes (lesional vs nonlesional: 734 DEGs [297 upregulated and 437 downregulated]; lesional vs normal: 4230 DEGs [1980 upregulated and 2250 downregulated]), including many upregulated immune and downregulated hair keratin genes. Equally impressive as upregulation in TH1/interferon markers (IFNG and CXCL10/CXCL9) were those noted in TH2 (IL13, CCL18, CCL26, thymic stromal lymphopoietin, and periostin), TH9/IL-9, IL-23 (p40 and p19), and IL-16 mediators (all P 25%) was associated with greater immune and keratin dysregulation and larger abnormalities in nonlesional scalp samples (ie, CXCL10 and KRT85).

Conclusions: Our data associate the AA signature with TH2, TH1, IL-23, and IL-9/TH9 cytokine activation, suggesting consideration of anti-TH2, anti-TH1, and anti-IL-23 targeting strategies. Similar to psoriasis and AD, clinical trials with selective antagonists are required to dissect key pathogenic pathways.

inflammatory) PDE4 (Phosphodiesterase 4) POSTN (Periostin) STAT1 (Signal transducer and activator of transcription 1) TSLP (Thymic stromal lymphopoietin) Alopecia areata (AA) is a T cell–mediated, hair-specific disorder with a 1.7% lifetime risk.1-6 Although it usually presents as patchy, nonscarring hair loss, approximately 10% of patients progress to alopecia totalis (AT) or even whole-body hair loss (alopecia universalis [AU]).3,5,7-10 Treatment options for AA are limited, lack sustained efficacy,11-17 and harbor multiple side effects.12,17 The standard of care for localized AA is use of intralesional corticosteroids, which cause pain and skin atrophy and have limited efficacy.16 Although contact sensitizers (eg, diphencyprone) have some benefit, particularly in patients with localized disease, they are irritating and painful, leading to noncompliance.14,18,19 For extensive AA, broad immune antagonists, such as cyclosporine,20,21 oral steroids,22 photochemotherapy/PUVA,21,23 and recently Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors,24 are efficacious but nonspecific, often with side effects precluding long-term use.12 The lack of targeted therapeutics for AA largely derives from the unclear understanding of cytokine pathways that create the AA pathology.3,25 CD4+ and particularly CD8+ T cells have been associated with AA pathogenesis.24,26 CD8+ T cells appear first in perifollicular infiltrates, and depleting CD8+ T-cells restore hair growth in animal AA models.26-28 Although experimental and human studies suggest that cytokines play a role in mediating hair growth,24,29-33 an integrated molecular profile of activated immune pathways in tissue samples from patients with AA is lacking. Although numerous blood and skin studies focused on TH1/IFN-γ responses,34-36 recent studies also showed an increased TH2 profile in blood36,37 and scalp biopsy specimens.34 A possible involvement of the TH17 axis was also suggested based on sera from patients with AA3,36 and TH17 T-cell infiltrates surrounding hair follicles.38,39 Thus we sought to perform comprehensive molecular profiling of the lesional and nonlesional AA transcriptomes from 27 patients with AA ranging from limited to extensive (AT/AU) hair loss, with normal scalp comparisons. AA tissue samples were also compared with well-characterized lesional and nonlesional atopic dermatitis (AD), psoriasis, and healthy tissue samples. We have identified the AA genomic signature, including related immune and keratin-associated biomarkers, with a further characterization of limited versus extensive AA involvement. In addition to TH1 skewing, our data associated AA lesions with equally impressive TH2 and IL-23 (p19 and p40) cytokine activation, as well as with increases in levels of novel cytokines, such as IL-16 and IL-32. Methods Patients' characteristics Twenty-two patients with AA were enrolled in the study (13 female and 9 male patients; age range, 23-65; median age, 44 years). Four-millimeter biopsy specimens of lesional and nonlesional (>5 cm from lesions) scalp were collected under institutional review board–approved protocols. Nonlesional biopsy specimens were unavailable for 5 patients. One healthy scalp control sample was included in microarrays, and 3 control samples were included in RT-PCR. Five published (GSE45512)24 lesional and 5 healthy scalp samples were included (n = 27 lesional and 17 nonlesional samples and 6 healthy scalp samples) to increase the power of microarray analyses. Included study patients are presented in a flowchart (see Fig E1 in this article's Online Repository at www.jacionline.org). See Table E1 for patients' characteristics. See the Methods section in this article's Online Repository at www.jacionline.org for details. Quantitative RT-PCR and microarrays RNA was extracted for RT-PCR with EZ-PCR Core Reagents (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY). Expression values were normalized to human acidic ribosomal protein values. Two previously published cohorts with AD (n = 16)40 and psoriasis (n = 10)41 were used for comparisons. HGU133Plus2.0 microarrays (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, Calif) were used, as previously described.42-45 For additional details, see the Methods section in this article's Online Repository. Immunohistochemistry Immunohistochemistry was performed on frozen skin sections. See the Methods section in this article's Online Repository for antibodies. Statistical analysis Standard quality control metrics and R package microarrayQualityControl were used for microarrays. Images were scrutinized for artifacts by using Harshlight.46 Expression measures were obtained with the GCRMA algorithm.47 Batch effects were eliminated by using Combat48,49 in the R package (see Fig E2 in this article's Online Repository at www.jacionline.org). Probe sets with 1 or more samples with expression values of greater than 3 and SDs of greater than 0.1 were included. Expressions were modeled by using mixed-effect models with time and treatment as fixed factors and random effect per patient. This approach models within-patient correlation similarly to paired t tests, accounting for missing values. Fold changes (FCHs) were estimated and hypothesis testing was conducted by using contrasts in the R limma package linear model framework. P values from paired t tests were adjusted for multiple hypotheses with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. For RT-PCR, human acidic ribosomal protein–normalized expressions were log2 transformed before analysis. For each marker, mixed-effect models were fit with disease and tissue as field effects and a random intercept per patient. Heat maps used unsupervised hierarchic clustering by using Euclidean distance and McQuitty agglomeration.47 Unsupervised hierarchic clustering was performed to define inflammatory subgroups (see Fig E3 in this article's Online Repository at www.jacionline.org). For additional details, see the Methods section in this article's Online Repository. Scalp involvement ranged from 4% to AT (n = 1) or AU (n = 2; median, 15%; representative images are shown in Fig 1, A). Ten patients had concomitant AD (SCORAD score, 7.2-64.8; median score, 35.6), and 5 additional patients had some other history of atopy. Using criteria of an FCH of greater than 1.5 and a false discovery rate adjusted P value of less than .05, we established the transcriptome for the lesional versus nonlesional tissue and lesional versus healthy tissue comparisons. The lesional versus nonlesional comparison included 734 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; 297 upregulated and 437 downregulated [1145 transcripts]) and lesional versus healthy analysis had 4230 DEGs (1980 upregulated and 2250 downregulated [5980 transcripts], see Table E2 in this article's Online Repository at www.jacionline.org). A heat map of an inflammatory gene subset ordered by extent of scalp involvement shows increased inflammation in lesional scalp samples, as evidenced by stronger red colors (Fig 2, A). Even nonlesional scalp samples of patients with greater involvement showed more inflammation (Fig 2, A). Shared DEGs in lesional versus nonlesional and lesional versus healthy transcriptomes included general inflammatory (phosphodiesterase 4B [PDE4B]), T-cell activation (GZMB, CD28, and CCR7), TH1/interferon (CXCL9/CXCL10), TH2 (CCL18, CCL13, and CCL5), and dendritic cell (DC; integrin, alpha L [ITGAL]/CD11a and CD86) markers. Patients with severe AA exhibited many more DEGs (comparing lesional vs healthy scalp) and significantly increased dysregulation of lesional samples, and these were even more evident in nonlesional scalp samples, which is more abnormal (closer to lesional). This parallels AD, in which nonlesional skin of patients with higher disease activity shows greater abnormalities,40,42,80-84 possibly advocating for the systemic nature of AA. Although past studies, many focusing on mouse models, favored a TH1-mediated pathogenesis for AA,24,35,85 several human AA studies suggested a possible pathogenic involvement of the TH2 axis.33,34,36,77,86-88 The TH2 chemokine thymus and activation-regulated chemokine (TARC)/CCL17 has been suggested as a biomarker of AA at baseline and after corticosteroid treatment, regardless of atopy.86 Furthermore, higher levels of TH1 cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-12) have been observed in patients with AA with and without atopy. Recent genetic association of IL-13 with AA in 2 separate AA cohorts with and without atopy89 and several reports on IL-4 polymorphisms in patients with AA90,91 also support possible involvement of TH2 signaling in patients with AA. Although mouse AA models suggest a pathogenic role for IFN-γ,24,92 the role of IFN-γ in immune-mediated human diseases is less certain. Despite the original dichotomous view of TH1/TH2, both psoriasis and AD have established strong TH1/IFN-γ signals in chronic lesions, and clinical improvements with broad or specific antagonists are associated with TH1 suppression,40,41,53,83,93-96 but antagonism of other cytokines induces disease remission.93,97,98 Moreover, direct antagonism of the TH1/IFN-γ axis in patients with psoriasis is ineffective.99 AA might thus prove to be similar to psoriasis, in which IFN-γ was historically considered pathogenic but now has a highly uncertain role. Because JAK antagonists do not specifically target TH1/IFN-γ,100-102 hair regrowth in 3 patients with AA through JAK antagonism cannot prove a TH1-mediated pathogenesis.24,103 JAK inhibitors target common γc cytokines (IL-4, IL-2, IL-7, IL-15, IL-9, and IL-21), and in this study many of these cytokines show increased levels in patients with AA. A few studies raised a possible contribution of the TH17/IL-17 axis to the AA phenotype.36,38,39,61,104 In our large cohort TH17 and TH22 pathways were not increased in AA scalp samples compared with those in healthy scalp samples. Our data associated the AA signature with a highly inflammatory profile of TH2, TH1, IL-23, and IL-9/TH9 pathway genes. Although the AA profile showed marked upregulation of TH1/IFN-γ genes, it was also heavily TH2 polarized, including “hallmark” atopic biomarkers (POSTN, TSLP, IL13, CCL18, CCL26, and FCER1A). Several observations support a shared genetic background between AA and atopy.2,55-58,105 History of atopy (particularly AD) was the highest risk factor for AA,55 and in 2115 patients with AA, 38.2% had concomitant AD/atopy.2 AA is also often accompanied by pruritus.106 Recent genome-wide association studies associated IL-13 with susceptibility to AA regardless of atopy,89 and genetic IL-4 polymorphisms were reported in several AA cohorts.89,91 Filaggrin mutations were also associated with severe AA.58 Lastly, high serum IgE levels, eosinophilia, and infiltration of eosinophils and mast cells were demonstrated in AA lesions and correlated with higher severity.34,107-109 Our results, together with strong genetic associations of IL-13/IL-4 with AA and epidemiologic links between AA and AD/atopy, support a possible pathogenic role of the TH2 axis in patients with AA. These data provide a rationale for exploring TH2-targeting strategies, which show efficacy in AD and asthma trials,93,110,111 for patients with AA. Our data also suggest that IL-23 (p40 and p19) antagonism might also provide benefit in patients with AA. Ustekinumab was recently shown to reverse anti-TNF–induced AA in a patient with Crohn disease, perhaps suggesting a possible utility for AA.112 Only clinical trials with targeted therapeutics against the TH2, TH1, IL-23, and PDE4 pathways will help dissect the possible pathogenic contribution of these axes to disease pathogenesis. Clinical implications Our data, together with genetic links of TH2 cytokines to AA and epidemiologic associations between AA and atopy, suggest a possible pathogenic role for the TH2 axis in patients with AA.
