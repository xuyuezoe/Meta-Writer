# Association between hemoglobin glycation index and 5-year major adverse cardiovascular events: the REACTION cohort study.

## Abstract

Background: The hemoglobin glycation index (HGI) was developed to quantify glucose metabolism and individual differences and proved to be a robust measure of individual glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) bias. Here, we aimed to explore the relationship between different HGIs and the risk of 5-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) by performing a large multicenter cohort study in China.

Methods: A total of 9791 subjects from the Risk Evaluation of Cancers in Chinese Diabetic Individuals: a Longitudinal Study (the REACTION study) were divided into five subgroups (Q1-Q5) with the HGI quantiles (≤5th, >5th and ≤33.3th, >33.3th and ≤66.7th, >66.7th and ≤95th, and >95th percentile). A multivariate logistic regression model constructed by the restricted cubic spline method was used to evaluate the relationship between the HGI and the 5-year MACE risk. Subgroup analysis between the HGI and covariates were explored to detect differences among the five subgroups.

Results: The total 5-year MACE rate in the nationwide cohort was 6.87% (673/9791). Restricted cubic spline analysis suggested a U-shaped correlation between the HGI values and MACE risk after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors ( χ2 = 29.5, P 0.82 showed odds ratios (ORs) for MACE of 1.471 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.027-2.069) and 2.222 (95% CI, 1.641-3.026) compared to subjects with HGIs of >-0.75 and ≤-0.20. In the subgroup with non-coronary heart disease, the risk of MACE was significantly higher in subjects with HGIs ≤-0.75 (OR, 1.540 [1.039-2.234]; P = 0.027) and >0.82 (OR, 2.022 [1.392-2.890]; P 0.82 after adjustment for potential confounders.

Conclusions: We found a U-shaped correlation between the HGI values and the risk of 5-year MACE. Both low and high HGIs were associated with an increased risk of MACE. Therefore, the HGI may predict the 5-year MACE risk.

## Introduction

Introduction

Diabetes is one of the most common chronic diseases and can cause many complications. [ 1 ] The glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) assay is the golden criteria for the evaluation of blood glucose control in diabetes. However, the HbA1c level variation can be only explained by 60–80% of the mean blood glucose level. [ 2 ] Red blood cell turnover and the glucose gradient across the red blood cell membrane may affect the HbA1c level; however, this does not seem to depend on the average blood glucose level. [ 3 , 4 ] The residual 20–40% of HbA1c variation may reflect individual glucose metabolism, physiological differences, and genetic factors. [ 5 , 6 ] Therefore, HbA1c measurement does not fully reflect blood glucose metabolic status.

To quantify the variation, the hemoglobin glycation index (HGI) was developed by Hempe et al , [ 7 ] who proved that the HGI was a robust measure of HbA1c differences among pediatric patients with type 1 diabetes. [ 8 ] The HGI is the observed HbA1c level minus the predicted HbA1c level; the latter is derived via linear regression of the fasting blood glucose (FBG) level to the observed HbA1c level.

A high or low HGI means that the HbA1c level is higher or lower than the value expected from the FBG level. Several studies have sought correlations between the HGI and diabetic complications. For example, subjects with higher HGIs show more risk of diabetic retinopathy or nephropathy than others. [ 9 , 10 ] The HGI was strongly predictive of cardiovascular disease (CVD) outcomes. [ 11 , 12 ] Two meta-analyses reported that a high HGI (only) was associated with increased risks of composite CVDs. [ 13 , 14 ] For example, mortality from acute coronary syndrome and vascular dysfunction increased in those with high HGIs. [ 15 ] However, several studies found that low-HGI subgroups may experience more CVD events than moderate- and high-HGI subgroups. [ 16 - 18 ] Thus, the relationship between CVD outcomes and the HGI remains controversial. Here, we aimed to explore the relationship between the HGI and the risk of 5-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in a large multicenter cohort study in China. We sought to evaluate the clinical utility of the HGI further.

## Methods

Methods

Ethics approval

The Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, approved this study (No. 2011–14). All participants gave written informed consent.

Study population

In 2010, Ning and Bloomgarden [ 19 ] commenced a large, nationwide, prospective cohort study in China, termed The Risk Evaluation of Cancers in Chinese Diabetic Individuals: a Longitudinal Study (the REACTION study). This study investigated relationships between diabetes and diabetic complications, and cancer. A total of 10,216 REACTION patients were recruited for the present study. All subjects live in Laoshan, Jinding, and Gucheng communities of Beijing. Five years later, researchers went to the same communities for another investigation and notified the participants by phone for follow-up. After exclusion of 133 patients with MACE histories, or without baseline FBG or HbA1c data, 10,083 were included in 2011. Finally, in 2015, 9791 patients were included in the analysis after excluding 117 with missing data and 175 lost to follow-up at the 5-year endpoint [Figure 1 ].

A flowchart of REACTION patients included in the study. FBG: Fasting blood glucose. HbA1c: Glycosylated hemoglobin; MACE: Major adverse cardiovascular events; REACTION: The Risk Evaluation of Cancers in Chinese Diabetic Individuals.

The questionnaire and other investigations

All participants completed a standardized questionnaire, the body mass index (BMI) was measured, and biochemical tests were performed. The questionnaire covered several common vascular diseases: (1) Coronary heart disease (CHD), including asymptomatic and ischemic CHD, and angina pectoris. [ 20 ] As myocardial infarction is one of MACE, patients with a history of such infarction and those with histories of coronary revascularization were excluded [Figure 1 ]. (2) Hypertension: systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg or a self-reported history of hypertension. [ 21 ] (3) Lower extremity arterial disease (LEAD) diagnosed via vascular ultrasound and/or clinical features. [ 22 ] (4) Metabolic diseases: type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is defined as a self-reported history of T2DM, an FBG level ≥7.0 mmol/L, a 2-h postprandial blood glucose (2-hPBG) level ≥11.1 mmol/L, or an HbA1c level ≥6.5%. [ 23 ] Dyslipidemia was defined as a serum total cholesterol level >5.17 mmol/L or a serum triacylglycerol level >2.3 mmol/L. (5) Smoking was defined as one or more cigarettes daily for at least 6 months. (6) Alcohol consumption was defined as an alcohol intake ≥250 g/week. (7) Diet: daily carbohydrate intakes of grains and potatoes; the frequencies of vegetable, fruit, and legume intakes; the frequencies of red meat, poultry, milk, egg, and aquatic product intakes; and three typical dietary patterns [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B576 ] were measured. (8) Drug use: any use of hypoglycemic, lipid-lowering, or antihypertensive drugs.

Measurements

We measured height, weight, BMI, and blood pressure. Height was measured with bare feet to the nearest 0.01 m. Weight was measured in light clothes to the nearest 0.1 kg. The BMI was the weight/height 2 (kg/m 2 ). With each subject resting, blood pressure was measured in the seated position three times at 1-min intervals using an electronic device; the average was used in the analysis. Initial laboratory assays measured the levels of serum total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), creatinine, FBG, 2-hour postprandial blood glucose (2-hPBG), and HbA1c.

Outcome

The pre-specified outcome was the first MACE during 5 years of follow-up. MACE was defined as death from cardiovascular causes, a first non-fatal myocardial infarction, or a non-fatal stroke. [ 22 ] Subjects who experienced such outcomes were evaluated in terms of case history, diagnosis, and imaging examinations performed in hospital.

HGI calculation

The baseline FBG and HbA1c levels were subjected to linear correlation analysis [Figure 2 ]. The X axis represented the FBG level and the Y axis represented the HbA1c level. The regression equation was: Predicted HbA1c level = 0.49 × FBG (mmol/L) + 3.17 ( r = 0.81; P 5th and ≤33.3th, >33.3th and ≤66.7th, >66.7th and ≤95th, and >95th percentile). ## Results

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 9791 patients were finally included. The population was divided into five subgroups (Q1–Q5) by the HGI quintiles (≤5th, >5 and ≤33.3, >33.3 and ≤66.7, >66.7 and ≤95, and >95th percentile). The baseline characteristics including gender, age, BMI, HbA1c, FPG, 2-hPBG, TC, TG, LDL-C, and creatinine levels, T2DM rate, type of hypoglycemic treatment, CHD and hypertension rates, antihypertensive drug use, hyperlipidemia status, statin use, LEAD, smoking status, and systolic and diastolic blood pressures, differed significantly across the five subgroups [Table 1 ]. The fifth subgroup showed the highest BMI, HbA1c and LDL-C levels, and the most intensive antidiabetic treatment (oral drugs with insulin), compared to the other subgroups [Table 1 ]. The dietary structures including the daily intakes of rice, flour, grains, and potato differed significantly across the five subgroups [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B576 ]. The probability density function revealed that the HbA1c distributions differed significantly across the HGI subgroups [Figure 3 A]. In contrast, the distributional plots of the FBG and 2-hPBG levels were similar [Figure 3 B,C]. Figure 3 D showed that the HGI distribution revealed by the probability density function was normal.

Relationship between the HGI and MACE

Testing suggested the existence of a non-linear relationship between the HGI and MACE. We thus used a restricted cubic spline test to evaluate the relationship between the HGI and the 5-year MACE. Figure 4 A revealed a U-shaped correlation between the HGI and the MACE rate ( χ 2 = 28.8, P –0.75 and ≤–0.20). As the HGI then rose or fell, the 5-year MACE risk both increased. After adjustment for age ≥65 years, gender, BMI ≥28 kg/m 2 , hypertension, LEAD, the creatinine, LDL-C, TC levels, smoking status, and diastolic blood pressure, the U-shaped correlation between the HGI and MACE remained significant ( χ 2 = 29.5, P –0.75 and ≤–0.20 (second subgroup), >–0.20 and ≤0.13 (third subgroup), >0.13 and ≤0.82 (fourth subgroup), and >0.82 (fifth subgroup). After adjustment for age ≥65 years, gender, BMI ≥28 kg/m 2 , hypertension, LEAD, smoking status, and alcohol consumption (Model 1), the risks of MACE were significantly increased in the first (odds ratio [OR] and 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.471 [1.027–2.068], P = 0.030) and fifth (OR 95% CI, 2.164 [1.573–2.945], P <0.001) subgroups compared to the second subgroup. After additionally adjustment for diastolic blood pressure and the creatinine, TC, and LDL-C levels (Model 2), the risk of MACE was again significantly increased in the first (OR [95% CI], 1.472 [1.027–2.070], P = 0.030) and fifth (OR [95% CI], 2.218 [1.611–3.021], P = 0.003) subgroups compared to the second subgroup. After additionally adjustment for three dietary patterns (Model 3), the first and fifth subgroups remained at significantly greater risk of 5-year MACE. Supplementary Table 2 [ http://links.lww.com/CM9/B576 ] showed that the continuous NRI values of Model 2 (9.4% improvement, P = 0.019) and Model 3 (10.3% improvement, P = 0.010) were both significantly better than those of Model 1. The IDI values of Model 2 (0.2% improvement, P <0.001) and Model 3 (0.2% improvement, P <0.001) were also significantly better than those of Model 1.

## Discussion

Discussion

In this large-scale cohort study, the HGI showed a U-shaped correlation with the first 5-year MACE risk after adjustment for covariates including age, gender, BMI, hypertension, LEAD, smoking status, diastolic blood pressure, and the creatinine, TC and LDL-C levels. As the HGI decreased or increased, the 5-year MACE risk increased.

The HGI concept was first proposed by Hempe et al [ 8 ] in 2002. Mean blood glucose (MBG) and HbA1c levels were measured in 128 children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes. Over the past 20 years, many studies have explored the clinical utility of the HGI. For example, high HGI levels in glucose-intolerant populations indicate inflammation and oxidative stress. [ 25 ] The levels of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) revealed by intrinsic skin fluorescence were significantly associated with the HGI in children with type 1 diabetes. [ 26 ] Thus, various mechanisms may mediate associations between the HGI and vascular complications.

In terms of the baseline characteristics of our subjects, the highest-HGI group showed the highest BMI, HbA1c, TC, and LDL-C values of all subgroups. Metabolic status was strongly compromised in this subgroup. Also, T2DM patients with the highest HGI are more likely to be prescribed hypoglycemic drugs and intensive treatments combining insulin with oral drugs, as we noted during the study. Differences in drug use among HGI subgroups have been observed before. The Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes (ACCORD) trial of Hempe et al [ 16 ] indicated that insulin was more likely to be prescribed for individuals with persistently high HGIs, suggesting that blood glucose control for high-HGI diabetics was challenging in clinical practice.

Notably, the association between HGI values and metabolic factors does not seem to be limited to specific populations. For example, similar to what we found, non-diabetic individuals with high HGIs also evidenced significantly higher BMI, TG, and inflammatory marker values. [ 27 ] Thus, the HGI may independently predict metabolic and inflammatory changes. A recent study indicated that a high HGI was negatively associated with the telomere attrition that contributed to the pathology of aging-related diseases including metabolic syndrome, T2DM, and CVDs. Interestingly, the effect was independent of the HbA1c level. [ 28 ]

We observed a U-shaped correlation between the HGI and the risk of 5-year MACE, similar to previous studies. The HGI quantitates the biological variations in HbA1c levels; the HGI may reflect the individual ability to glycate Hb. [ 29 ] Several large clinical trials have explored the strong associations between high HGI levels, and diabetes complications or cardiovascular disease (CVD). For example, in the Trial Comparing Cardiovascular Safety of Insulin Degludec vs . Insulin Glargine in Patients With Type 2 Diabetes at High Risk of Cardiovascular Events (DEVOTE) trial, a high HGI was associated with a greater MACE risk over 33 months in T2DM patients. [ 24 ] In the large placebo-controlled Ale-Cardio trial, each percentage point increase in the HGI was associated with a 16% increase in the cardiovascular mortality risk. [ 30 ]

However, the linear relationship may not accurately reflect the correlation between the HGI and the CVD risk. We found that, as the HGI fell, the 5-year MACE risk increased. Recently, low-HGI groups have become the focus of attention. In a cohort study with 1910 T2DM patients, Østergaard et al [ 18 ] suggested that a low HGI might be a risk factor for myocardial infarction in patients with CHD. As have we, Pan et al [ 17 ] recently identified a U-shaped association between the HGI and the risk of 12-month stroke, suggesting that both low and high HGIs were associated with severe vascular outcomes. As did Pan, we speculated that both lower and higher HGIs reflected abnormal metabolic states associated with adverse outcomes. In different populations, we speculated that both low and high HGI values reflected large individual differences in glucose metabolism and Hb glycation.

The difference between HbA1c and glucose levels might predict poor cardiovascular condition. In subgroup analysis, we found an association between the highest HGI and a greater MACE risk after adjustment for several covariates including gender, BMI, and T2DM. The increased levels of inflammatory biomarkers including tumor necrosis factor- α , C-reactive protein, and white blood cell levels in high-HGI subjects might suggest the pathogenesis. [ 25 , 27 , 31 ] In an Asian cross-sectional study of subjects with impaired glucose metabolism, the OR of the highest-HGI group for composite CVD was higher in females, [ 28 ] suggesting that gender might partly explain the observed HGI differences. Furthermore, in both a non-coronary artery disease subgroup and an age <65 years subgroup, both a low and high HGI were associated with a higher risk of MACE. Therefore, it is necessary to monitor HGI regularly in such populations.

The HGI has been widely used in clinical practice. The HGI predicted the risks of retinopathy and nephropathy in type 1 diabetic children. [ 9 ] Also, as the HGI quantifies individual glycation levels, the HGI combined with the HbA1c level may diagnose both prediabetes and diabetes. [ 32 ] In China, cardiovascular-related diseases became the highest threat on mortality risk in adults and the medical resource burden of MACE was continually increasing in the past decade. [ 33 , 34 ] The HGI thus identifies subjects at high MACE risk, requiring for early therapy and optimal management. We strongly recommend that hospitals record HGIs to evaluate the risk of MACE.

This large study enrolled a representative Chinese population. However, there are certain limitations. First, HGI calculation is based on data from a specific population; it is difficult to formulate a standard equation for global studies. Each study optimally requires a new regression. Second, we might not adjust for some possible confounding factors such as race, diabetes type, or genetic differences. Finally, as the relationship between the HGI and HbA1c level is linear, we cannot exclude the possibility that the effects of the HGI are related to the HbA1c level. Although the HGI is a robust indicator, the HGI may fluctuate over time. Identification of an optimal HGI assessment time is essential.

We found a U-shaped correlation between the HGI and the risk of 5-year MACE. Compared to a moderate HGI, both low and high HGIs were associated with increased risks. The HGI may thus powerfully predict adverse vascular events in clinical practice.
