# Cell-matrix feedback controls stretch-induced cellular memory and fibroblast activation.

## Abstract

Mechanical stretch can activate long-lived changes in fibroblasts, increasing their contractility and initiating phenotypic transformations. This activation, critical to wound healing and procedures such as skin grafting, increases with mechanical stimulus for cells cultured in two-dimensional but is highly variable in cells in three-dimensional (3D) tissue. Here, we show that static mechanical stretch of cells in 3D tissues can either increase or decrease fibroblast activation depending upon recursive cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) feedback and demonstrate control of this activation through integrated in vitro and mathematical models. ECM viscoelasticity, signaling dynamics, and cell mechanics combine to yield a predictable, but nonmonotonic, relationship between mechanical stretch and long-term cell activation. Results demonstrate that feedback between cells and ECM determine how cells retain memory of mechanical stretch and have direct implications for improving outcomes in skin grafting procedures.

## Results

Results

Sustained Stretch of Tissues over a Clinically Relevant Range of Strains Leads to Dramatically Different Homeostatic States Depending upon Strain Magnitude and Duration.

To quantify the effect of sustained stretch on fibroblast activation, we developed an in vitro model system using synthetic tissues containing human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) and type I collagen ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 ). Thin (~0.1 mm thickness) ring-shaped specimens were created to match the thickness of split-thickness skin grafts, as thinner grafts are known to exhibit increased contractility ( 15 ). The fibroblasts contracted the collagen onto the central cylinder of an annular Teflon mold over 2 d ( Fig. 1 A ).

Finite element analyses of meshed skin grafts ( SI Appendix , 3 ) showed that grafts typically experience strains ranging from 0 to 30% depending on the meshing ratio and placement technique. Based on these results, we designed our experimental conditions to match physiological strain levels: tissues were stretched either with 0% strain (“control,” corresponding to widely meshed grafts), 5% strain (“low”), or 30% strain (“high,” corresponding to minimally meshed grafts) ( SI Appendix , Fig. S1 E ). We applied these strains for either 1 h or 24 h. The circumferential stretching direction in our experiments aligned with cellular orientation ( Fig. 1 B and D ), mimicking stretching of skin along Langer’s lines ( SI Appendix , Fig. S1 C ).

For each tissue, we first measured the isometric tissue contraction force F 0 p r e immediately after placing the tissue on the stretching apparatus on day 2 ( Fig. 1 C , i ) and then “prestrained” the tissue by 0% (control), 5% (low), or 30% (high) for either 24 h (“long duration”) or 1 h (“short duration”) ( Fig. 1 C , ii ). The tissues were then allowed to relax and return to the unstretched baseline length for 1 d ( Fig. 1 C , iii ), after which the isometric tissue contraction force F 0 p o s t ( Fig. 1 C , iv ) was again measured in the unstretched reference configuration. The change in contraction force associated with the strain treatment, Δ F 0 = F 0 p o s t - F 0 p r e , was recorded.

For all three specimens stretched for 1 h, Δ F 0 / F 0 p r e was positive, with the increase in contractility independent of stretch ( Fig. 1 E ). However, tissues stretched for 24 h showed a biphasic trend with respect to strain treatment: for tissues subjected to low stretch, Δ F 0 / F 0 p r e was positive and maximum, while for tissues subjected to high stretch, the mean Δ F 0 / F 0 p r e was negative and significantly lower ( P < 0.0001, Fig. 1 E ).

The Time- and Strain-Dependent Properties of the ECM Regulate Changes to Cell Homeostasis and Tissue Contractility.

To identify the sources of this unexpected, bimodal behavior, we separated the active and passive mechanical responses of the tissue specimens. After completing the 1 d of tissue recovery ( Fig. 1 C , iv ), viscoelastic stress relaxation testing was performed on each tissue, in which tissues were stretched by an amount ΔX = 0.66 mm and held at that length while the isometric force was recorded ( Fig. 2 A , v ). The force rose to a peak during the stretching, and the subsequent isometric force then relaxed to an asymptotic value over time ( Fig. 2 B ). The difference Δ F between the pretest isometric force (force measured at 0 s) and the force measured after 600 s of relaxation was recorded, and the long-term stiffness Δ F /ΔX was calculated ( SI Appendix , Fig. S3 ). Tissues were subsequently returned to their reference lengths, treated with the actomyosin contractility inhibitor cytochalasin-D ( 36 ), and then allowed to relax for 1 h prior to repetition of the viscoelastic relaxation test to obtain the passive response of the tissues ( Fig. 2 A , vi–vii ). Cytochalasin-D treatment eliminated active contraction at the start of the relaxation test and reduced the peak force but had little effect on the rate of long-term stress relaxation ( Fig. 2 B ), as observed by others ( 37 ).

## Discussion and Conclusions

Discussion and Conclusions

Using an integrated experimental and theoretical approach, we described mechanisms by which cell–ECM feedback modulates both the long-term activation of cells and tissue contractility. This occurred at strain levels known to be important for wound healing and skin grafting ( 14 ). Unlike previous observations on stem cells, fibroblasts developed mechanical memory under static tissue strain, depending on the duration and magnitude of tissue strain. Central to this was the role of the ECM, whose time- and strain-dependent physical properties modulated the development of mechanical memory in fibroblasts and their sustained long-term activation. ECMs subjected to high tensile strains for long durations underwent permanent structural changes, in contrast to ECMs exposed to either lower strain magnitudes or shorter strain durations. These permanent changes manifested as reduced ECM stiffness measured 1 d after the release of tissue strain ( Fig. 2 E ) and reorganization of the ECM structure, including the formation of microvoids and altered collagen fiber organization ( SI Appendix , Fig. S8 ). The theoretical model accurately predicted this ECM remodeling ( Fig. 2 F ) and demonstrated that these structural changes led to reduced tension experienced by cells within the tissue ( Fig. 4 ).

The model further predicted that this reduced cellular tension prevented the long-term activation of cells ( Fig. 5 D ). This prediction was validated experimentally, showing that fibroblasts within tissues subjected to high strain magnitudes and long durations exhibited significantly lower activation levels compared to fibroblasts exposed to lower strain magnitudes for the same prolonged durations ( Fig. 5 C ). Simultaneous imaging of F-actin and collagen demonstrated that cells maintained their structural relationship with the ECM even under high strain conditions ( SI Appendix , Fig. S8 ), although the spatial organization of both cellular and matrix components was altered: microvoids were observed within the ECM structure at length scales comparable to the cell diameter (∼20 μm). These voids, which would be difficult to distinguish from preparation artifacts in dehydrated samples, represent genuine structural adaptations to sustained mechanical loading and suggest that the observed reduction in cell activation was not due to loss of cell–ECM connectivity but rather to changes in ECM mechanical properties that altered force transmission to the cells.

Our integrated modeling approach captured the bidirectional relationship between mechanical and biochemical cellular responses. Tissue stretching changes cell stress σ c ( Fig. 4 C and SI Appendix , Figs. S5 and S10 ) and ECM stiffness E t a n g e n t ( Fig. 4 B and SI Appendix , Fig. S11 ), in a way that depends on both stretch magnitude and duration, as predicted by the active chemo-mechanical tissue model. Cell stress ( σ c ) was input to the signaling network model to predict how triggered mechanosensitive pathways change (normalized) actomyosin contractility A c ( Fig. 4 D ). This altered contractility state was then fed back to modify the cell’s force generation, as predicted by the active chemo-mechanical tissue model, creating a sustained feedback loop that can persist even after the initial mechanical stimulus is removed ( SI Appendix , Fig. S12 ). This two-way coupling thus predicts how transient mechanical signals can lead to long-term changes in cell behavior through both immediate mechanical responses and sustained biochemical signaling pathways.

This one-dimensional model could be extended in future work to a 3D framework, enabling the prediction of spatial variations in cellular activation and ECM stiffness within tissues. The experiments could also be repeated with varying cell densities to test the model’s predictions across a range of cell densities. A scaling measure can be used to directly relate cell density to the baseline contractility ( σ c 0 ), described in SI Appendix , 1.3 . Note that percolation-type phenomena occur in these systems ( 50 – 52 ), as established by Doha et al. ( 53 ), and the relations are thus not likely to be predicted by a simple rule-of-mixtures-type model. This represents an important direction for future work that would require additional experimental validation.

Our results demonstrate that tissue contractility ( Figs. 1 E and F and 4 I ) is regulated by the interplay between cell contractility ( Figs. 4 G and 5 A – C ) and ECM stiffness ( Figs. 2 E and F and 4 H ). This finding has direct clinical implications, especially for conditions where either insufficient or excessive tissue contraction impairs wound healing or promotes fibrosis. An important example of this is the clinical challenge of split-thickness skin grafting, where a critical issue is that the contraction of grafts cannot yet be predicted, with similar graft preparation sometimes yielding diametrically opposite outcomes in different patients or even different grafting sites on the same patient. For this case, our results suggest several potential clinical interventions. First, mechanical preconditioning of grafts could optimize fibroblast activation before transplantation. The strain magnitude and duration of preconditioning might be tailored based on anatomical location, for example using graft meshing patterns that result in lower strains when grafts are destined for mobile areas like joints, where excessive contracture is problematic. Second, the timing of mechanical loading before transplantation (preconditioning) could be optimized to either promote or limit long-term tissue contractility as needed clinically. Finally, posttransplantation strain management might help reduce complications. Limiting strain might be beneficial for areas prone to hypertrophic scarring, with postoperative dressings designed to provide site-specific mechanical support. Conversely, controlled straining might be beneficial where enhanced contractility is needed for wound closure.

## Materials and Methods

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and 3D Tissue Construction.

HDFs, derived from adult dermis, were purchased from Lonza (Basel, Switzerland, Catalog #: CC-2511). We used cells only at passages P4 to P8 in our experiments. Cells were first cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO 2 in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS). Cells were next detached with 0.05% Trypsin/Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 5 min and resuspended in fresh DMEM. The suspended cells in DMEM were mixed with rat tail-derived type I collagen in 0.1% acetic acid and 2× DMEM (Sigma). After neutralizing the pH with sodium hydroxide, 1 mL of the mixture containing 1.5 × 10 6 cells/mL HDFs and 1.2 mg/mL collagen was added into Teflon-made cylindrical casting molds with a mandrel at the center with a diameter of 9.65 mm ( Fig. 1 A ). The tissues were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO 2 for 2 d and formed compacted and thin contractile rings with cells aligned in the circumferential direction of the molds.

Tissue Force Measurement Experiments.

We measured the contractile force in each tissue before ( F 0 p r e : Fig. 1 C , i ) and after ( F 0 p o s t : Fig. 1 C , iv ) tissue stretching. The tissue force measurements were performed using a custom-made tensile tester with sensitive isometric force transducers (Harvard Apparatus) and stepper motors (Haward Industry) plugged into an Arduino for applying displacement precisely ( Fig. 1 B ). A MATLAB subscript was developed for collecting the output voltages reported by the transducers, which were calibrated to the force. To control the temperature, a water pump with a temperature control system was connected to the organ bath ( Fig. 1 B ).

The tissue rings were removed from the casting molds after 2 d of compaction ( Fig. 1 A ) and were hung onto the attachment bars, bathed with 25 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffered DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C ( Fig. 1 B ). The attachment bars were then separated at a 13.3 mm distance under the control of the Arduino ( Fig. 1 C , i ). At this length, the periphery of the tissues on the stretcher device is equal to the periphery of the tissues in the mold on day 2. The tissue force was measured by lifting the lower attachment bars away from the samples to define the zero-force point and bring it back to the 13.3 mm distance. SI Appendix , Fig. S2 shows a representative plot of tissue force measurement. The average of forces measured during the first 200 s of force measurement was reported as the tissue contractile force.

After measuring the initial tissue contractile force, the tissues were moved back to the incubator and were kept in sodium bicarbonate buffered DMEM at 37 °C with 5% CO 2 . We designed 3D-printed spacers with different lengths and placed them between the two ends of the tissues to generate 0% (tissue length = 13.3 mm), 5%, and 30% strains in the tissues ( Fig. 1 C , ii ).

The tissues were kept under the prescribed strain levels either for 1 h or 24 h ( Fig. 1 C , ii ). The spacers were then removed and the tissues were allowed to relax for 1 d ( Fig. 1 C , iii ). We then measured the contractile force in each tissue using the force measurement procedure described above ( Fig. 1 C , iv ).

Stress Relaxation Test.

After measuring the contractile force of prestrained tissues ( Fig. 1 C , iv ), tissues were allowed to relax for 10 min and then were hung onto the attachment bars of the tensile tester. We then applied 5% strain to the tissues and kept them under the strain for 10 min ( Fig. 2 A , v ) while we continuously measured and recorded the force ( Fig. 2 B ). The stiffness of the tissue was measured as Δ F /ΔX where Δ F is the difference between forces measured at t = 10 min and t = 0, respectively, and ΔX = (0.05) (13.3 mm) is tissue deformation ( SI Appendix , Fig. S3 ).

We then allowed the tissues to relax for 10 min and treated them with 2 μM cytochalasin-D for 1 h to disrupt the cell-generated contractile forces ( Fig. 2 A , vi ). We then repeated the stress relaxation test ( Fig. 2 A , vii ) and measured the stiffness as described above ( Fig. 2 E ).

Matrix Stress–Strain Relationship.

After the stress relaxation test on cytochalasin-D treated tissues, we allowed the tissues to relax for 10 min on the tensile tester. Starting from the initial tissue length of 13.3 mm, we then stretched the tissues with a loading rate of 3.11 μm/s to determine the force-strain curves in Fig. 3 D . Note that, as depicted in Fig. 2 E , tissues prestrained for 24 h showed long-term reductions in ECM stiffness, while tissues prestrained for 1 h did not. Therefore, for the determination of the matrix stress–strain relationship depicted in Fig. 3 D , we only used tissues prestrained for 1 h.

Immunofluorescence and Cell Imaging in 3D Collagen Gels.

To determine the long-term level of cell activation, we fixed the tissues 1 d after tissue unloading and stained them for F-actin ( Fig. 5 B and C ) and α-SMA ( SI Appendix , Fig. S6 A and B ). Tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Catalog #: 15714) for 2 h at 37 °C, permeabilized with 0.5% triton X-100 solution (Sigma, X100) for 30 min and blocked with 10% goat serum (Thermos Fisher, Catalog #: 50062Z) for 1 h at room temperature before incubating with anti-α-smooth-actin antibody (1:250 dilution, Abcam, Catalog #: ab124964) in 10% goat serum at 4 °C overnight. Tissues were incubated with rhodamine-phalloidin (1:400 dilution, Thermos Fisher, Catalog #: R415) with Alex Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500 dilution, Abcam, Catalog #: ab150077) in 10% goat serum for 2 h at room temperature. Each step was followed by PBS washing for a minimal 10 min at least three times. Samples were mounted to glass-bottom dishes with a prolonged gold antifade mounting solution with DAPI (Thermos Fisher, Catalog #: P36931 ). Z-stack images were obtained through a Zeiss LSM 880 laser confocal microscope with identical parameters in each experiment.

The spatial distribution of cells and ECM was analyzed using confocal microscopy, which enables simultaneous visualization of both components in their native, hydrated state ( 54 ). This approach is particularly important for observing structural changes that occur at the cellular length scale (10 to 100 μm), such as the formation of microvoids and altered ECM organization around cells ( SI Appendix , Figs. S8 and S9 ). These features, which are central to understanding how mechanical loading affects cell–ECM interactions, are difficult to preserve and observe using techniques that require sample dehydration ( 55 ).
