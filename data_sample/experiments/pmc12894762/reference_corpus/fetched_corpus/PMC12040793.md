# Implication of Gut Mycobiome and Virome in Type-2 Diabetes Mellitus: Uncovering the Hidden Players.

## Abstract

Type-2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a global epidemic with significant societal costs. The gut microbiota, including its metabolites, plays a pivotal role in maintaining health, while gut dysbiosis is implicated in several metabolic disorders, including T2DM. Although data exists on the relationship between the gut bacteriome and metabolic disorders, further attention is needed for the mycobiome and virome. Recent advancements have begun to shed light on these connections, offering potential avenues for preventive measures. However, more comprehensive investigations are required to untangle the interrelations between different microbial kingdoms and their role in T2DM development or mitigation. This review presents a simplified overview of the alterations in the gut bacteriome in T2DM and delves into the current understanding of the mycobiome and virome's role in T2DM, along with their interactions with the cohabiting bacteriome. Subsequently, it explores into the age-related dynamics of the gut microbiome and the changes observed in the microbiome composition with the onset of T2DM. Further, we explore the basic workflow utilized in gut microbiome studies. Lastly, we discuss potential therapeutic interventions in gut microbiome research, which could contribute to the amelioration of the condition, serve as preventive measures, or pave the way towards personalized medicine.

Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Gut microbiome, Bacteriome, Mycobiome, Virome, Phageome

The human gut harbors an immensely diverse, complex, dynamic yet remarkably homeostatic and resilient ecological community of microscopic life including trillions of bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses, and protists. These tiny inhabitants, comprising the bacteriome (total bacterial community in the gut), mycobiome (whole fungal components), and virome (complete viral community of the gut), interact with their host in notable ways, playing a key role in gut, metabolic, and immune health. However, when the equilibrium of this microbial ecosystem is disrupted, it can result in undesired health consequences (Manor et al. 2020 ). Abnormal shift in gut microbiome has been linked to various metabolic disorders and also a contributing factor in age related illness (Bandopadhyay and Ganguly 2022 ; Kim and Benayoun 2020 ). The composition of gut microbiome is influenced by various factors including age, method of child delivery, and type of feeding (Nagpal et al. 2018 ).

The role of gut microbiome in development of type-2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is not set in stone, with only moderate dysbiosis being observed. However, a shift in dominance transpires as the number of butyrogenic firmicutes decreases and the presence of opportunistic pathogenic bacteria rises (Wang et al. 2012b ). Conversely, some studies have reported an increase in the abundance of Firmicutes (Bhute et al. 2017 ). Nevertheless, the majority of consistent research findings indicate a relatively lower abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila , a member of the phylum Verrucomicrobia , in the gut microbiota of individuals with T2DM (Zhang et al. 2021a ). Additionally, it has been reported a decreased abundance of short chain fatty acid (SCFA) producing bacteria in the gut of T2DM patients. For instance, the abundance of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii , Roseburia and Prevotella are notably diminished in obese and T2DM patients. These bacteria produces SCFAs, which mainly includes acetate, propionate and butyrate as their by products and plays a role in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism by activating SCFA receptors present on the liver, adipose tissue, brain, and pancreas thereby improving hyperglycemia (Remely et al. 2014 ; Salamone et al. 2021 ).

It is reported that the gut microbiome, specifically the bacteriome and mycobiome, primarily targets the insulin signaling pathways either directly or through the production of various metabolites (Zhang et al. 2021a ; Zhou et al. 2024 ; Rudolph et al. 2013 ). Similarly, the role of the gut virome in T2DM pathophysiology is not fully understood, but it is shown to exacerbate the condition by generating pattern associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), which subsequently induce mucosal inflammation by activating the immune system (Norman et al. 2015 ). Despite these findings, the precise mechanism by which dysbiosis of the gut microbiome contributes to the pathophysiology of T2DM remains unclear.

Inspite of the growing body of evidence supporting the intricate connections within the human microbiome, the majority of research studies have primarily focused on the bacterial community, known as the bacteriome. However, the roles of the mycobiome and virome, integral components of the human microbiome, have recently gained attention but remain largely underexplored. Moreover, progress in this field is hampered by several bottlenecks. The high cost of comprehensive studies, particularly those incorporating the virome and mycobiome, poses a significant challenge due to the need for specialized methodologies and complex data analysis. Unlike prokaryotes and eukaryotes, viruses lack universally conserved genes, which hinders their identification through cost-effective metagenomic approaches (Carding et al. 2017 ). Additionally, the lack of comprehensive, well-curated databases of microbiome data hinders the validation and replication of findings (Zhang et al. 2021a ; Carding et al. 2017 ). While an integrated approach to studying the bacteriome, mycobiome, and virome could provide valuable insights into T2DM, these challenges pose a serious impediment to scientific research concerning the gut microbiome and its implications for human health.

Though still in its infancy, the study of the gut virome and mycobiome is gaining momentum for their potential roles in the development of T2DM. Despite limited literature, studies have revealed a link between obesity and T2DM and changes in the gut virome (Yang et al. 2021 ). Although the records of fungi in the human gut are scarce, their association with T2DM appears to be significant (Salamon et al. 2021 ).

In this review, we delve into the contemporary understanding of the virome and mycobiome’s roles in T2DM, as the spotlight has been majorly on the bacteriome, undermining the importance of other players in the development of the disorder. We scrutinize their independent significance as well as their co-regulatory interactions with the bacteriome and each other, aiming to unravel the intricate mechanisms specifically underpinning T2DM pathophysiology. Furthermore, we present a comprehensive synthesis of the current knowledge while pinpointing potential areas for future investigations.

The association between microbial dysbiosis and the development of T2DM is documented, but still having diverse views regarding its pathophysiology. When referring to specific bacterial taxa the phylum Firmicute and class Clostridia are found to be significantly reduced, while the gram-negative Betaproteobacteria were reported highly abundant in the feces of T2DM patients as compared to non-diabetic control subjects (Larsen et al. 2010 ). Also, in T2DM patients it is marked by the depletion of several butyrate-producing bacteria, namely Clostridium sp., Eubacterium rectale , Faecalibacterium prausnitzii , Roseburia intestinalis , and Roseburia inulinivorans (Wang et al. 2012 ; Karlsson et al. 2013 ; Sato et al. 2014 ), and an enrichment of opportunistic pathogens (Wang et al. 2012b ). According to a study by Zhang et al., there was a reported decrease in the levels of Akkermansia muciniphila (Zhang et al. 2013 ) and an increase in the abundance of sulphate reducing bacteria, such as Desulfovibrio along with other Lactobacillus species, specifically Lactobacillus gasseri , Lactobacillus reuteri , and Lactobacillus plantarum was observed (Karlsson et al. 2013 ; Sato et al. 2014 ).

The main mechanisms through which the gut bacteriome and their metabolites affect the progression of T2DM include targeting of different signaling pathways, and production of short chain fatty acids (SCFAs), branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), bile acid, imidazole propionate, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Zhang et al. 2021b ). SCFAs are the primary metabolites produced by gut bacteria, which eventually maintains glucose homeostasis. They do so by increasing intestinal hormone secretion and gluconeogenesis, decreasing intestinal permeability, maintaining the gut anaerobic environment, and finally assisting in metabolizing dietary fiber, propionic acid, butyrate, and acetate (Zhang et al. 2021b ). SCFAs also binds to the free fatty acid receptors (FFARs) present on the surface of intestinal cells and trigger the secretion of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) by colon cells. This hormone further contributes to the secretion of insulin, inhibition of appetite, and reduced glucagon secretion (Psichas et al. 2015 ). Butyrate improve the integrity of the intestinal barrier by stimulating the expression of tight junction protein viz. claudin-1, which further leads to the distribution of ZO-1 (Zonula Occludens-1) and occludin on the cell membrane. Additionally, butyrate also switches on peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma (PPAR-gamma) in colon cells, which helps maintain an anaerobic environment in the intestine. They also reduce the bioavailability of electron receptors in the colon cavity of Enterobacteriaceae and stop the abnormal proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, namely Escherichia coli and Salmonella , thus maintaining the microecological balance in T2DM patients. Butyrate also promotes the production of regulatory T cells, hence known for its anti-inflammatory effect (Wang et al. 2012a ). Besides, propionate actuates the gene responsible for intestinal gluconeogenesis and hence aid in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism. They also change the hematopoietic function of bone marrow, as observed in mice, and stimulate the production of macrophages and dendritic cells thereby alleviating inflammation (Vadder et al. 2014 ). Imidazole propionate and Branched chain amino acids (BCAAs) mediate glucose levels by blocking the insulin signaling pathway and activating mTORC1, which is responsible for insulin resistance (Zhang et al. 2021b ). Bile acids and LPS affect glucose metabolism by binding to Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR) and Toll like receptors (TLRs), respectively, and stimulate the release of 5-hydroxy tryptamine in enterocytes to induce insulin resistance (Shapiro et al. 2018 ).

Under dietary fiber deficiency, bacteriome utilize host-secreted mucus glycoproteins as a source of nutrients, leading to the degradation of the mucus barrier. The butyrate and propionate act on the human goblet-like LS174T cells and enhance the expression of MUC2 histones. This MUC2 synthesis reinforces mucus secretion, ultimately leading to dwindled intestinal permeability and enhanced gut barrier integrity (Desai et al. 2016 ; Paassen et al. 2009 ).

Regardless of its small proportion, evidence suggests that the gut mycobiome plays a considerable role in maintaining host homeostasis and disease development (Chin et al. 2020 ). Exploring the gut mycobiome is complicated by the lack of well-curated fungal databases. Standardized methods for mycobiome analyses are needed to minimize variations across studies (Zhang et al. 2021c ). Despite these challenges, studies have identified fungal genera including Saccharomyces , Malassezia , and Candida as the predominant fungal genera present in stool samples. As the exploration in this area is making progress, the gut mycobiome is being increasingly linked to various pathologies which seems beckoning for further exploration (Pérez 2021 ).

The role of C. albicans in exacerbating insulin resistance and other pathologies has been identified and assays for β-glucan derived from C. albicans also require attention as studies are reporting it for having deteriorating effects through dectin-1-dependent pathway (Bao et al. 2023 ). Altenusin, a fungal metabolite produced by Alternaria , is reported to attenuate hyperglycemia and insulin resistance by inhibiting the expression of certain hepatic gluconeogenesis genes namely glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) and forkhead box protein O1 (FoxO1) (Zheng et al. 2017 ; Wang et al. 2023 ). Another class of fungal metabolites lactones, known for their potential in improving diabetes and obesity by maintaining glucose and energy homeostasis, especially those produced from Alternaria and Cochliobolus . The most significant lactones from these fungi include oxacyclododecindione, curvularin, dehydrocurvularin, and galiella lactone. These lactones are of particular interest due to their ability to inhibit the transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling pathway (Zhou et al. 2024 ; Rudolph et al. 2013 ). Furthermore, specific lactones, namely galiellalactone and dehydrocurvularin, have demonstrated remarkable anti-inflammatory activity by reducing the expression of several pro-inflammatory markers such as IL-1β and TNF-α (Schreiber et al. 2017 ).

But ultimately, the precise role of the gut mycobiome in its host still remains largely unclear. More exploration is needed to fully unravel and understand its rigid nature. The link between gut fungi and diseases like T2DM remains ambiguous; nonetheless, significant differences in the abundance of certain fungal genera have been observed in T2DM cases. These include higher abundance of Candida , Aspergillus , Sarocladium , Debaryomyces , Kurtzmaniella and Monascus , and lower numbers of Fusarium and Cladosporium (Bao et al. 2023 ). In contrast, some studies have reported contradicting findings of increased Cladosporium (Jayasudha et al. 2020 ). Furthermore, a positive association has been observed between the relative abundance of Penicillium and fasting glucose levels. Additionally, the Malassezia and Kazachstania presence has been linked to higher levels of fasting glucose and larger waist circumference, respectively (Shuai et al. 2022 ).

The human gut virome colonizes the body from birth through environmental exposure and changes with the age (Gregory et al. 2020 ). These viruses interact with bacterial hosts and immune cells, shaping our microbiomes and influencing metabolic pathways (Tiamani et al. 2022 ). The gut virome is composed of prokaryotic as well as eukaryotic viruses (Bhardwaj et al. 2022 ). While it is dominated by the order Caudovirales of tailed phages (Carding et al. 2017 ), of which the crAssphage is the most abundant family (Tiamani et al. 2022 ). Also, viruses can get integrated into its host genome, as prophages, and can play a role in maintaining gut homeostasis and sequentially shaping the overall gut microbiome (Hu et al. 2021a ). We observe an interesting field to explore prophage activation in the varying conditions of the gut, specifically in T2DM as it is known to cause moderate level chronic gut inflammation (Hu et al. 2021 ; Zhang et al. 2021a ). Prophages are also involved in Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT), which can lead to the spread of beneficial or harmful genes among bacteria, eventually leading to the disruption of communal homeostasis of the gut and potentially leading to dysbiosis, further aiding to an increased intestinal permeability (Li et al. 2020 ). In individuals with T2DM, increased intestinal permeability can allow microbial molecules to enter the bloodstream, triggering an immune response and further contributing to systemic inflammation (Hu et al. 2021b ). Furthermore, HGT can allow some pathogenic gut bacteria to gain Multi-Drug Resistance (MDR) which further may impact the condition negatively (McInnes et al. 2020 ).

Considering the importance and critical nature of these interactions between prophages and bacterial host genomes within the gut environment, techniques such as Metagenomic Hi-C (metaHi-C) in combination with Shotgun Metagenomics (SGM) can be employed for gaining insights, as they can provide valuable information into the 3D organization of bacterial genomes and the potential hotspots for prophage integration and HGT (Lamy-Besnier et al. 2023 ; Marbouty and Koszul 2015 ). MetaHi-C uses SGM on proximally ligated genomic threads and allows us to understand the relationships between different parts of the DNA based on their physical proximity within the same cell, and by quantifying these contacts it can identify DNA molecules most likely to be from the same cell. But considering the resource intensiveness of the predecessor, predictive tools such as Phigaro (Starikova et al. 2020 ), VIBRANT (Kieft et al. 2020 ), Prophinder (Lima-Mendez et al. 2008 ), PropagAtE (Kieft et al. 2022 ), and PIE (Miller-Ensminger et al. 2023 ) can be employed on the data from SGM. However, viruses don’t have universally conserved genes like the 16 S rRNA in prokaryotes or the 18 S rRNA in eukaryotes. Because of this and their genetic diversity, the metataxonomic approaches such as 16 S rRNA sequencing cannot be used to identify virus-like particles (VLPs) and prophages (Carding et al. 2017 ). This presents a challenge for researchers studying the gut virome and its impact on human health.

Emerging evidence suggests that abnormal alterations in the virome (‘dysvirome’), particularly the phageome, may be associated with T2DM and could serve as a biomarker. Notably, bacteriophages and bacteria alter cohesively in cases, including an increase in phages specific to Enterobacteriaceae (Chen et al. 2021 ). A clinical study revealed a significant increase in the relative abundance of several bacteriophage including Siphovirus , Podovirus , and Myovirus among cases (Ma et al. 2018 ). Nonetheless, given the predator-prey dynamics, it is not unexpected as alterations in the bacteriome have been documented across diverse populations. A reduction in both the richness and diversity of the gut virome among obese individuals with T2DM, as compared to their lean counterparts, has also been observed (Yang et al. 2021 ). Although the mechanisms by which the phageome alter the insulin resistance and contribute to T2DM still needs to be unraveled.

Inter-phylogenetic interactions among the bacteriome, mycobiome, and virome modulate population and colonization by influencing gut homeostasis. Within this intricate network, gut virome plays a pivotal role in shaping the immune system from its establishment and development to its functionality. In a symbiotic relationship, the gut virome’s composition and functionality are also influenced and regulated by the gut bacteriome and host immunity. For instance, phages also inhabit the intestinal mucosa which regulate both the innate and adaptive immunity of the host (Popescu et al. 2021 ). A predator-prey relationship exists between phages and bacteria. The replication cycles of phages determine how they shape the bacterial community. In the lytic cycle, the injection of phage genome into the bacteriome, results in the release of viral offspring and destruction of the host bacterial cell. In contrast, temperate phages follow the lysogenic cycle under favorable conditions in the human gut (Cao et al. 2022 ). Temperate phages exist in a prophage state, until stimulating factors appear. These can include changes in temperature or pH, exposure to antibiotics, oxidative stress, and many more. Prophage promotes bacterial adhesion, invasion, colonization, transfer of antibiotic resistant genes, endotoxin genes and biofilm formation (Cao et al. 2022 ). Bacteriophages reportedly maintain mucosal inflammation in Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) with production of PAMPs and antigen through their lytic state (Norman et al. 2015 ). Residing in the intestinal lumen and on mucosal surfaces, phages interact with bacteria in a species-specific manner. However, recent studies show that phages can also interact with bacterial species in a non-specific manner, allowing them to infect a wide range of gut bacteria and alter the bacterial community (Clinton et al. 2022 ).

While, the presence of certain bacteria has also shown to inhibit the fungal hyphal growth (Noverr et al. 2004 ). The primary mechanism by which the fungi and bacteria interact in gut includes the physical interaction that involves direct binding, chemical interactions by the release of quorum sensing molecules and metabolites, environmental changes such as increase or decrease in pH and oxygen level, competition for nutrition and adhesion sites, antagonism by the production of toxic metabolic byproducts, and biofilm formation (Krüger et al. 2019 ). The production of metabolites such as alcohol and antimicrobial peptides influence the bacterial colonization (Kombrink et al. 2019 ).

A study conducted on a murine model demonstrated that post-antibiotic treatment colonization of Candida albicans negatively impacted the recolonization of Lactobacillus strains and favored the colonization of Enterococcus spp (Mason et al. 2012 ). Another study on the murine colitis model showed that antifungal administration led to severe colitis and altered bacteriome (Qiu et al. 2015 ). Also, the elimination of Enterobacteriaceae bacterial family from the gut not only nullified the negative effects of C albicans but also the beneficial effects of Saccharomyces boulardii (Sovran et al. 2018 ). Overall, the gut mycobiome and bacteriome interact with each other and have impacts on the host physiology. Notably, interactions between Saccharomycetales spp and gut bacterial diversity have been shown to influence insulin resistance (Shuai et al. 2022 ). Despite the growing body of evidence, further research is necessary to better comprehend the complex relationship. Alterations in the virome, bacteriome, and mycobiome have been observed in the gut of T2DM patients. However, specific triggers and underlying mechanisms of these disturbances remain to be elucidated.

In any case, it is universally acknowledged that the foundational composition of gut microbiota undergoes dramatic changes throughout different stages of life with significant fluctuations during early childhood, especially during the first three years (Nagpal et al. 2018 ). These changes eventually lead to the establishment of a relatively stable microbiota and sets up an equilibrium having a resemblance to that found in adults. The composition of the gut bacteriome with regard to growing age is influenced by various factors including genetics, mode of child delivery (c-section or vaginal birth), maternal diet and microbiota, exposure to antibiotics, and the infant’s feeding methods (such as breastfeeding or formula feeding) (Nagpal et al. 2018 ). Overall, the four main phyla that predominate the vast kingdom of the gut bacteriome are Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria, while Verrucomicrobia and Fusobacteria make up only a small (but important) fraction of this realm (Cunningham et al. 2021 ). Indeed, the gut microbiome is dynamic and even in healthy individuals, the core microbiome can undergo marked changes due to various factors. As the gut microbiota evolves alongside the host, its intricate interplay with a multitude of factors such as diet, lifestyle, and environmental elements becomes crucial to comprehend. Understanding the dynamics of this microbial community and its mechanisms from infancy to old age is imperative for gaining valuable insights into its potential associations with T2DM risks (Fig. 2 ). Hence, the early stages of life offer a distinct window of opportunity for regulating the gut microbiota to promote long-term sustainable health, and establishing a baseline for a healthy gut microbiome as it is very crucial to pinpoint and comprehend the microbiome alterations specific to diseases such as T2DM with greater precision.

A commonly utilized workflow in gut microbiome research (Fig. 3 ) involves the isolation of metagenomic DNA from stool samples, with kit-based methods being preferred for their reproducibility (Fiedorová et al. 2019 ). At this stage, the choice of sequencing method becomes crucial. Amplicon sequencing, due to its cost-effectiveness, is often the first choice for bacteriome and mycobiome analysis (Liu et al. 2021 ). It amplifies the conserved regions of targeted organisms, effectively eliminating host and other sequences that fall outside the study’s scope. Conversely, Shotgun Metagenomics (SGM) adopts a broader approach, sequencing nearly all DNA in a sample and possessing the ability to provide resolution up to the strain level (Madison et al. 2023 ). SGM has the added advantage of providing data on the presence or absence of genes in the identified microbes, which can further be aligned with databases such as KEGG for obtaining the functional annotations from the metagenome (Kanehisa and Goto 2000 ; Madison et al. 2023 ). This makes it a more reliable tool for delving into functional genomics compared to Amplicon Sequencing where prediction based computational tools, such as PICRUSt/PICRUSt2 and TAX4FUN/TAX4FUN2 are employed (Douglas et al. 2020 ; Aßhauer et al. 2015 ). Although they provide valuable insights into the functional potential of microbial communities, their results may not be as reliable as those obtained from the functional annotations of SGM data. Additionally, SGM has the potential to identify viruses from a single sample in single sequencing run (Bassi et al. 2022 ). In contrast, Amplicon Sequencing requires the division of the sample into two for the amplification of conserved regions (16 S and 18 S/ ITS) in bacteria and fungi using specific primers (Matsuo et al. 2021 ; Santus et al. 2021 ).

However, the virome, as it lacks the conserved regions, poses a challenge to this sequencing application. For virome analysis, preprocessing may be required to filter virus-like particles (VLPs) from the samples prior to nucleic acid isolation (Avellaneda-Franco et al. 2023 ). While for detecting RNA viruses, meta-Transcriptome Sequencing can be employed on the filtered VLPs, as this can reduce host RNA contaminations. Meta-Transcriptome Sequencing has capability in identifying live microbes and their gene expression and/or functional analysis in the gut environment. Despite its potential in providing unique information through sequencing technologies, this technique is less popular in gut microbiome research due to the complexity of sample collection, handling, labor intensity, and the costs involved. Interestingly, some studies have identified Amplicon Sequencing as more robust than SGM in identifying a greater number of phyla and families (Tessler et al. 2017 ).

Furthermore, the choice of sequencing application is majorly dependent on the DNA quantity and quality obtained from the extraction process. If the DNA quantity is low Amplicon Sequencing can be a good option as SGM usually requires a higher amount of DNA concentration. Therefore, for large-scale exploratory studies focusing more on relative data of bacteriome and/or mycobiome, Amplicon Sequencing appears to be more feasible. On the other hand, SGM may have an advantage in studies where the objective is to obtain the abundance of whole microbial community within a population having DNA as genetic material.

The next step is sequencing, performed using various platforms and opting between them can also impact the quality of results. With third generation sequencers, such as PacBio and OxfordNanopore we can achieve better read length improving the assembly of the genomes, but as of now they have higher error rates in comparison to Illumina platforms. However, second generation sequencing platforms specifically Illumina are more common for SGM because of their wide availability, high accuracy, speed and data output. After sequencing, the raw reads are downloaded and their quality is validated and improved using tools such as FastQC or MultiQC (Andrews 2010 ; Ewels et al. 2016 ). High-quality reads are then processed to generate clean reads. For Amplicon Sequencing there are generally two ways to proceed with clean reads, either we can do clustering to generate operational taxonomic units (OTUs) or we can do Denoising to get Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), which can be analyzed using reference databases for sequence identification (Table 1 ) and getting the taxonomic information. On the other hand, for SGM we can either go with assembly based profiling or read based profiling. Assembly based profiling is preferred for its capability in providing whole genomes from the data and hence presenting a detailed insight of the genetic makeup and functional analysis, also this method can aid in providing novel genes, but this comes at the cost of high computational requirements. Alternatively, read based profiling being less computationally extensive is fast and can detect low abundance species which can be a limitation to assembly based profiling, but it is more dependent on databases and can struggle in identification of novel genetic elements.

This outlined workflow, encompassing DNA isolation, amplification, preprocessing (in the case of the virome), sequencing, quality enhancement, and sequence identification, provides a basic framework for gut microbiome research. The methodological choices and sequencing strategies therein are tailored to the distinct characteristics and research goals associated with bacteriome, mycobiome, and virome components within the gut microbiota.

The links between gut health and overall well-being are being established; in line, methods to balance the gut microbiome have also come to focus, including prebiotics, probiotics, and synbiotics (Ke et al. 2021 ). Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) is another approach where fecal matter from a healthy donor is transferred to the recipient’s gut to restore the balance of the gut for therapeutic results (Vaiserman et al. 2017 ). Previous evidence from the studies on Clostridium difficile infection (CDI) have shown that FMT can restore gut microbiome balance and improve the condition (Zuo et al. 2018 ) and also, a study on mice with high-fat induced T2DM found that β-cell apoptosis was inhibited post FMT (Wang et al. 2020 ). However, the efficacy of FMT hinges on the donor’s profile; a case study revealed the onset of obesity in a female CDI patient post-procedure when the donor exhibited corpulence (Alang et al. 2015 ). As the importance of the gut microbiome became clear, the investigations turned to Fecal Filtrate Transplantation (FFT) also known as Faecal Virome Transplantation (FVT), exploring its potential to restore the balance in a safe and effective way. FFT is a refined method that removes fecal bacteria and reduces the risk of bacterial infection associated with FMT (Rasmussen et al. 2020a ). A study on murine model of mice found that transplanting caecal viral communities from lean phenotype normalized the blood glucose levels in the recipients (Rasmussen et al. 2020b ). With promising results from studies on CDI and blood glucose levels in mice, FVT appears to be a promising therapeutic option. However, more extensive studies on a larger scale are needed to fully understand it’s potential.

In addition to FMT and FFT, Phage therapy offers a targeted approach of treating diseases caused by overgrowth of invasive bacteria. It is particularly effective against pathogenic and drug-resistant bacteria (Maronek et al. 2020 ). Antibiotics, frequently used to tackle diverse microbial infections and typically consumed orally, can foster the development of MDR in bacteriome. Considering its untargeted nature it also significantly reduces the diversity and richness in the gut microbiome. Also there are several studies which have, investigated the correlation between antibiotic use and the prevalence of T2DM (Mikkelsen et al. 2015 ; Park et al. 2021 ; Zhao et al. 2024 ; Zhou et al. 2021 ; Chu et al. 2023 ). The species-specific nature of phages makes them a potential tool for targeting individual bacterial species in the gut, and molding the overall gut microbiome. Phage cocktails such as MS2 and P22 are being proposed to tailor the gut microbiome without causing dysbiosis and targeting some opportunistic pathogens such as Candidatus Saccharimonas , Aerococcus , and Oscillibacter ultimately aiding to the betterment of T2DM condition (Ye et al. 2024 ). Another study, based on Mendelian randomization found a positive association between T2DM and the abundance of certain bacteria, namely Lachanoclostridium and Streptococcus , providing potential targets for phage therapy (Li et al. 2023 ). However, there are still knowledge gaps regarding phage selection, administration, dosage, resistance, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics (Luong et al. 2020 ). The potential for lytic phages to turn temperate and lose efficiency in humans is also not clear. Besides, most studies are exploratory in nature and are on animal models, with ample research on antibiotic resistance but more of it is still needed on metabolic disorders like T2DM (Hesse et al. 2019 ). Hence, adopting Phage therapy could be a promising approach to manage the gut microbiota, especially in the context of T2DM. The species-specific nature of phage therapy will allow for targeted intervention, potentially reducing the risk of dysbiosis associated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, also as we continue to explore these therapeutic avenues, it’s crucial to promote responsible use of antibiotics to mitigate their potential adverse effects on metabolic health.

While Phage therapy tailors the gut microbiome by targeting specific bacterial strains, the addition of fungal counterparts as probiotics can also contribute in betterment of the condition. In comparison to bacterial probiotics they have an added advantage of having a resistant envelope which makes their delivery feasible with a good survival rate. Among fungi, Saccharomyces in particular have been extensively studied as fungal probiotics. S. Boulardii have been known to reduce fat mass, body weight, and inflammation, and to induce changes in the gut microbiota composition in db/db T2DM mice (Everard et al. 2014 ), also they are known to exhibit anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting NF-κB activation in monocytes and intestinal epithelial cells (Sougioultzis et al. 2006 ) and to increase the production of SCFAs, specifically butyrate which in turn improves insulin sensitivity and enhances energy expenditure (Schneider et al. 2005 ). S. cerevisiae , have also been reported to increase the glucose uptake in rat adipocytes and sequentially reduce the levels of cholesterol along with glucose in db/db mice (Tuman et al. 1978 ). Overall, while bacteria remain the most commonly studied probiotic, the potential of fungi, should not be overlooked (Czerucka et al. 2007 ). Their demonstrated benefits in reducing fat mass, body weight, inflammation, in improving gut microbiota composition, insulin sensitivity, and energy expenditure, make them a promising area for future therapeutics research.

It is crucial to expand our understanding of the impact of gut microbial diversity, including mycobiomes and viromes, as well as their associated mechanisms and interactions in relation to T2DM. Despite advanced techniques revealing the composition of the human gut microbiome and its interactions with the host, the focus has primarily been on the bacterial community. Imbalances in this community can affect metabolism and energy harvest; however, data on fungal and viral populations remain limited. Given the established presence of mycobiome and virome in the human gut and their role in shaping our immune system, it is imperative to investigate their mechanisms and interactions. Both constituents interact with the bacteriome and can act as selection pressures and/or guiding forces for each other. Hence, studies with sterile gut animal models can be employed, which will allow for a more controlled investigation of the equilibrium and interactions of specific bacteria, virus or fungi in this microenvironment. Furthermore, understanding how diet and lifestyle factors influence the gut mycobiome and virome could provide valuable insights into disease prevention and management strategies. The potential for personalized nutrition strategies based on an individual’s unique microbiome composition could also be explored. Therefore, considering all the three when scrutinizing gut microbiome, may provide more valuable and broader insights into the complex interplay and relationships. Multiomic approaches, encompassing transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and metagenomics, can be employed to scrutinize the molecular and metabolic interplay between specific microbial communities in diseased or healthy individuals. This holistic approach can aid in developing a more thorough understanding of the functions of different microbial communities within the gut of T2DM patients. Additionally, certain technological advances such as updated reference databases are required for enhanced accessibility and precision to get more reliable data on the table. The need for sophisticated instruments presents a hurdle; hence, development of more methodologies and novel approaches in this sector is required.
