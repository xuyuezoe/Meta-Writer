# The Gut Microbiota in Prediabetes and Diabetes: A Population-Based Cross-Sectional Study.

## Abstract

The link between the gut microbiota and type 2 diabetes (T2D) warrants further investigation because of known confounding effects from antidiabetic treatment. Here, we profiled the gut microbiota in a discovery (n = 1,011) and validation (n = 484) cohort comprising Swedish subjects naive for diabetes treatment and grouped by glycemic status. We observed that overall gut microbiota composition was altered in groups with impaired glucose tolerance, combined glucose intolerance and T2D, but not in those with impaired fasting glucose. In addition, the abundance of several butyrate producers and functional potential for butyrate production were decreased both in prediabetes and T2D groups. Multivariate analyses and machine learning microbiome models indicated that insulin resistance was strongly associated with microbial variations. Therefore, our study indicates that the gut microbiota represents an important modifiable factor to consider when developing precision medicine approaches for the prevention and/or delay of T2D.

However, the anti-diabetic drug metformin is known to affect the gut microbial ecology, which might have influenced earlier results. Researchers at the University of Gothenburg analyzed the microbiota in two cohorts of Swedish subjects grouped according to their glycemic status (ranging from normal glucose tolerance to diabetes-treatment-naive T2D). They showed that the gut microbiota covaried with impaired glucose tolerance, combined glucose intolerance, and T2D, independent of metformin or other treatments. The abundance of potentially beneficial butyrate-producing bacteria was reduced both in prediabetes and T2D groups. These findings suggest that the gut microbiota could potentially be targeted in novel approaches to prevent the development of T2D. Introduction The global burden of type 2 diabetes (T2D) has risen dramatically over the past 2 decades and individuals with T2D are often undiagnosed (DeFronzo et al., 2015). T2D is preceded by prediabetes, a condition characterized by intermediate hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and reduced β cell function that predisposes individuals to T2D development (Tabák et al., 2012). Roughly one in three adults in the US and one in 14 adults worldwide are estimated to have prediabetes (IDF, 2017; CDC, 2017). Progression to T2D can be prevented or delayed by lifestyle modifications, medication, or bariatric surgery (Carlsson et al., 2012; Knowler et al., 2002; Tuomilehto et al., 2001), but up to 70% of those with prediabetes will eventually develop overt T2D (Tabák et al., 2012). Current strategies for diagnosis, screening, and successful management of prediabetes are limited partly by incomplete understanding of its pathophysiology. Our gut microbiota, which bears the potential to affect adiposity and glucose metabolism (Bäckhed et al., 2004; Le Chatelier et al., 2013), has been suggested to play a role in human health and a variety of diseases (Valdes et al., 2018). Diet is one of the main factors that shapes the gut microbiota (Falony et al., 2016; Zhernakova et al., 2016), and a growing number of dietary-derived microbial metabolites have been linked to insulin secretion and sensitivity as well as incidence of T2D (Canfora et al., 2019; de Mello et al., 2017; Koh et al., 2016, 2018; Zhao et al., 2018). Butyrate, for example, is produced by specific bacteria mainly by fermentation of dietary fibers, and a loss of butyrate producers has been consistently observed in prediabetes and T2D in independent cohorts with different ethnicities (Allin et al., 2018; Forslund et al., 2015; Karlsson et al., 2013; Qin et al., 2012). Furthermore, recent studies have shown that an individual’s glucose response after a meal is influenced by a combination of their gut microbiota composition and host physiology (Mendes-Soares et al., 2019; Zeevi et al., 2015). Therefore, characterization of the gut microbiota can contribute to the understanding of T2D pathophysiology and its management. We and others have shown a link between alterations in the gut microbiota and the pathogenesis of T2D (Allin et al., 2018; Karlsson et al., 2013; Larsen et al., 2010; Qin et al., 2012; Schüssler-Fiorenza Rose et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2019). However, previously published studies suffer from relatively low sample size and/or inclusion of patients on anti-diabetic drugs, such as metformin, which have a large impact on the gut microbiota (Forslund et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2017). Furthermore, no studies have analyzed the microbial functional changes within the heterogeneous prediabetes subgroups. To investigate if the gut microbiota is altered in subjects with prediabetes and newly diagnosed (and thus diabetes-treatment-naive) T2D and whether potential alterations associate with clinical variables, we used whole-genome shotgun sequencing to profile the gut microbiota in 1,011 individuals with a range of blood glucose responses (discovery cohort) and validated our results in a second cohort (validation cohort; n = 484). Open table in a new tab Compositional Shifts of the Gut Microbiota in Prediabetes and Diabetes Principal coordinate analysis of the gut microbiota profiles in the discovery cohort showed that significant shifts separated the gut microbiota composition of the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups from that of the low-risk NGT group (p = 0.0007, 0.00010, and 0.00030, respectively; PERMANOVA test with 10,000 permutations), but no significant changes in composition were observed between the high- and low-risk NGT groups (p = 0.086), between the IFG and low-risk NGT group (p = 0.069), or between the CGI and T2D groups (p = 0.14; Figure 1A). These results were confirmed using an independent bioinformatics method (Figure S3A). A similar pattern was observed for the shifts in microbial composition in the validation cohort (Figures 1A and S3A). We also observed similar results when we reran the analyses using the combined low- and high-risk NGT group as a reference in the discovery cohort: there were significant differences in gut microbiota composition between the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups and the combined NGT group (p = 0.021, 0.00070, and 0.011, respectively), but not between the IFG group and the combined NGT group (p = 0.24) (Figure S4A). Distance-based redundancy analysis in the discovery cohort showed that the observed microbial shifts (versus the low-risk NGT group) did not associate with fasting plasma glucose after adjusting for all other nonlinear variables (STAR Methods), and an association with 2 h OGTT glucose levels was only found for the IGT versus the low-risk NGT group (Figure 1B). Eight other variables—including the triglyceride-glucose index, vitamin D, and lymphocytes, but not sex, homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), or treatment with statins or proton pump inhibitors—were independently associated with gut microbiota compositional variations in the discovery cohort (Figure 1B). Figure 1 Shifts in the Gut Microbiota Composition in Parallel with Impaired Glycemic Status Show full captionFigure viewer We next developed a machine learning classifier based on a random forest algorithm to distinguish individuals with either CGI or T2D (CGI/T2D) from those with NGT using the microbiome (Figure 2). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis showed that the model had moderate prediction power with a mean cross-validation area under the curve (AUC) (i.e., in the discovery cohort) of 0.70 (Figure 2A) and a true prediction AUC (i.e., in the validation cohort) of 0.64, similar to that obtained using FINDRISC (AUC, 0.65) (Figure 2B). Adding the microbiome-based classifier to FINDRISC improved the model performance to a true prediction AUC of 0.70 (Figure 2B). In the NGT group of the validation cohort, individuals who were misclassified by the microbiome-based model had significantly higher insulin resistance (as measured by the triglyceride-glucose index and HOMA-IR) than individuals who were correctly classified (Figure 2C). Similarly, in the combined CGI/T2D group, individuals who were misclassified had lower insulin resistance than individuals who were correctly classified (Figure 2C). When re-evaluating our microbiome-based classifier in a subgroup of the validation cohort consisting of 100 individuals from each of the NGT and combined CGI/T2D groups with the lowest and highest insulin resistance, respectively, the gut microbiome identified CGI/T2D with an AUC of 0.78 (Figure 2D). The abundance of only two of the 118 species changed significantly in the high-risk NGT group and none changed in the IFG group, whereas 20, 98, and 27 species showed significantly altered abundance (and mostly a decrease) in the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups, respectively (Figure 3). Both the validation cohort and an independent bioinformatics pipeline confirmed that the highest number of microbial features with altered abundance was in the CGI and T2D groups (Figure S3B). When we reran the analyses using the combined low- and high-risk NGT group as a reference, 11 of the 118 species showed a significant difference between the IFG and combined NGT groups; however, the overall pattern of changes between the groups with altered glycemic status versus low-risk or combined NGT groups was similar, and more changes were observed in the IGT versus combined NGT group than in the IFG versus combined NGT group (Figure S4B). When using a feature importance cutoff of 0.15 (Figure S5), we showed that 38 (8, 31, and 17 in the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups, respectively) of the 118 significantly altered metagenomic species contributed to the microbiome-based classifier. Alterations in the Potential of Butyrate Production with Glycemic Status We observed that 51 out of the 118 metagenomic species altered in the prediabetes and T2D groups compared to the low-risk NGT group were potential butyrate producers (Figure 3; the putative terminal genes for the main colonic butyrate biosynthesis pathways are indicated in Table S1), and 14 of these 51 contributed to the microbiome-based classifier. Most (2, 36, and 5 in the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups, respectively) of the potential butyrate producers decreased in abundance (cluster I in Figure 3; e.g., Faecalibacterium spp., Clostridium spp., Alistipes spp., Pseudoflavonifractor spp., and Oscillibacter spp.) but some (2, 8, and 4 in the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups, respectively) were more abundant (cluster II in Figure 3; e.g., Clostridium bolteae). Comparative genomic analysis among 12 metagenomic species selected from the two clusters of butyrate producers (Figure 3) showed an overrepresentation in cluster I of proteins involved in DNA, RNA, and protein processing and an overrepresentation in cluster II of oxidoreductases, virulence factors, and transporters (Table S2). When we reran the analyses using the combined low- and high-risk NGT group as reference, we also observed a decreased abundance of 5 metagenomic species with potential for butyrate production in the IFG group (Clostridiales bacterium, Flavonifractor plautii, Coprococcus eutactus, Alistipes obesi, and Intestinimonas butyriciproducens; Table S1; Figure S4B). We also quantified the relative abundance of five terminal genes involved in butyrate biosynthesis from both carbohydrates (i.e., but and buk) and proteins (i.e., atoA/D and 4hbt). We observed that the combined abundance of all five genes was decreased in all prediabetic groups from both cohorts and the T2D group in the validation cohort (Figure 4A); the same pattern was observed for 4hbt/but (Figure 4B). The abundance of atoA/D was decreased in the CGI and T2D groups of both cohorts (Figure 4C), while the abundance of buk was decreased only in the CGI group of the discovery cohort and the T2D group of the validation cohort (Figure 4D), the groups in which we observed the largest alterations of gut microbiota composition (Figure 1A) and metagenomic species (Figure 3). Figure 4 Relative Abundance of Gene Biomarkers Involved in Bacterial Butyrate Production Show full captionFigure viewer Microbial Functional Perturbations Associated with Glycemic Status In parallel with the shifts observed in bacterial composition across the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups, we observed shifts in potential microbial functions (Figure 5A). In the discovery cohort, we observed significant changes in the relative abundance of 3337 KEGG orthology groups (KOs) in the IGT, CGI, or T2D groups, but no significant change in KO abundance in the IFG group when compared with the low-risk NGT group (Figure S6A; Table S3); the majority (3,112) remained significant when compared to the combined NGT group (Table S3). Targeted analysis also showed significant changes in the abundance of 2,263 of these KOs also in the IGT, CGI, or T2D groups of the validation cohort (Figure S6A; Table S3). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the altered KOs in the IGT, CGI, or T2D groups in both the discovery and validation cohorts showed a significant decrease and increase of 89 and 66 pathways/modules, respectively (Figure S6B). The 46 pathways that showed the most consistent decreases (defined here as significant reductions in at least three of the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups in both cohorts) included bacterial methanogenesis, glycolysis, peptidoglycan biosynthesis, and vancomycin resistance, as well as DNA replication and transcription (Figure 5B). The 21 most consistently increased pathways included two-component systems, phosphotransferase systems, fructose and mannose metabolism, pentose phosphate pathway, bacterial biosynthesis of branched-chain amino acids, and metabolism of the B-group vitamins biotin and thiamine (Figure 5B). We observed four enriched pathways related to biotin metabolism and synthesis (Figure 5B). Targeted KO analysis showed significant enrichment of six out of seven key KOs involved in bacterial biotin biosynthesis mostly in the IGT, CGI, and T2D groups in both the discovery and validation cohorts (Figure S6C). Figure 5 Shifts in Microbial Function in Parallel with Impaired Glycemic Status Show full captionFigure viewer Discussion In this population-based cross-sectional study, we show significant alterations of the gut microbiota (at both the compositional and functional potential levels) in individuals with diabetes-treatment-naive T2D in a discovery cohort, and we confirm these findings in a validation cohort. Importantly, we also show that alterations of the gut microbiota are already present in individuals with prediabetes, but more pronounced in people with CGI and strongly linked to a state of insulin resistance. Our evidence of a link between the gut microbiota and both prediabetes and T2D is consistent with earlier studies (Allin et al., 2018; Karlsson et al., 2013; Larsen et al., 2010; Qin et al., 2012). We expand on previous knowledge by showing that this finding is independent of metformin, a major confounding factor affecting the gut microbiota (Forslund et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2017), and also independent of treatment with statins and proton pump inhibitors and other confounders such as age, sex, and BMI. Furthermore, our analyses revealed differences between the IFG and IGT groups with more pronounced changes in the microbiota of individuals with IGT, despite comparable BMI in the IFG and IGT groups. This observation is of potential importance as earlier studies have reported higher risks for both T2D (Abdul-Ghani et al., 2009) and cardiovascular disease (Tominaga et al., 1999) in those with IGT/CGI than in those with IFG. Importantly, IGT and IFG have earlier been shown to associate with peripheral and hepatic insulin resistance, respectively (Abdul-Ghani et al., 2006), and modification of the gut microbiota (by either dietary fiber supplementation or transplantation of fecal microbiota from lean insulin-sensitive individuals) has so far been shown to improve peripheral more than hepatic insulin sensitivity in individuals with the metabolic syndrome (Kootte et al., 2017; Robertson et al., 2012; Vrieze et al., 2012). However, we do not have direct measurements of hepatic and peripheral insulin resistance, and therefore cannot exclude a potential role for the gut microbiota in hepatic insulin signaling. A link between gut microbiota composition and insulin resistance is further supported by results from our distance-based redundancy analysis and microbiome-based machine learning model to distinguish between those with CGI/T2D or NGT. Specifically, we obtained an improved AUC (0.78) when applying the model to two subgroups of the validation cohort with the lowest and highest insulin resistance. Consistent with our results, two recent longitudinal studies showed a strong connection between the microbiome and insulin resistance (Schüssler-Fiorenza Rose et al., 2019) and identified specific host-microbe interactions that differed between insulin-sensitive and insulin-resistant individuals (Zhou et al., 2019). Of note, we showed that circulating levels of the microbial metabolite imidazole propionate were increased in those with CGI/T2D in the discovery cohort and that the microbial potential for biosynthesis of branched-chain amino acids was increased in the gut microbiota of those with CGI in both cohorts. Imidazole propionate and branched-chain amino acids have both been shown to affect insulin signaling through mTORC1 (Koh et al., 2018; Pedersen et al., 2016) and are therefore potential causal links between the microbiota and insulin resistance. Our results on the functional potential of the gut microbiota also suggest potential interactions between the gut microbiota and the diet that could be important for the onset of T2D. For instance, we observed enrichment in bacterial pathways for biotin biosynthesis in the gut microbiota of groups with IGT, CGI, or T2D. Because bacteria only start to produce biotin when the environmental biotin supply is insufficient (Cronan, 1988), our data suggest that individuals with IGT, CGI, or T2D may be deficient in biotin and that this deficiency can affect the gut microbiota and its function. Although biotin deficiency in T2D has been reported (Maebashi et al., 1993) and several studies indicate that supplementation with biotin may improve glucose metabolism (Albarracin et al., 2008; Fernandez-Mejia, 2005; Lazo de la Vega-Monroy et al., 2013), there is no conclusive evidence, and future studies are required to accurately determine whether biotin deficiency plays a role in the pathogenesis and development of T2D. In addition, we observed a reduction of metagenomic species classified as butyrate producers in groups with IGT, CGI, and T2D compared with the low-risk NGT and NGT groups in the discovery and validation cohorts, respectively, and also in the IFG group when compared to the combined NGT group in the discovery cohort. In parallel, we observed reduced abundance of genes required for butyrate production from both proteins and carbohydates in the prediabetes and T2D groups. Increased intestinal production of butyrate as a function of host genetics has recently been linked to an improved insulin response after an OGTT (Sanna et al., 2019). The abundance and function of butyrate producers is also strongly modulated by the diet, particularly fiber intake (Makki et al., 2018), with potential beneficial effects on glucose control. Indeed, supplementation of fermentable fibers to people with T2D enhanced butyrate production in parallel with reductions in HbA1c (Zhao et al., 2018). Loss of butyrate producers has been repeatedly observed in the fecal microbiota of people with prediabetes and T2D (Allin et al., 2018; Forslund et al., 2015; Karlsson et al., 2013; Qin et al., 2012). Our results extend earlier work by showing that loss of butyrate producers and genes for butyrate synthesis from both carbohydrates and proteins occurs already in prediabetes and to a greater extent in people with IGT and CGI. These findings indicate a potential causal role for butyrate producers and/or butyrate itself in the etiology of glucose intolerance and progression to T2D. In a long-term perspective, replenishment of the niche of butyrate production in the gut microbiota of people with prediabetes could represent a targeted approach to prevent or delay the progression to T2D. A minority of potential butyrate producers showed higher abundance in those with IGT, CGI, or T2D versus NGT in our study. These included known opportunistic pathogens in the Clostridium clostridioforme cluster such as C. clostridioforme and C. bolteae (Finegold et al., 2005); members of this cluster have been reported earlier in the gut microbiota of subjects with T2D from different ethnic backgrounds and of subjects with obesity as well as marked insulin resistance and dyslipidemia (Le Chatelier et al., 2013). Importantly, our analyses show that the genomes of the potential butyrate producers with increased abundance also contained genes related to virulence. Understanding the reasons for the consistent expansion of these opportunistic pathogens might reveal important intestinal pathophysiological targets related to the development of IGT and T2D. Strengths of the study are that we screened a large number of middle aged individuals from the Swedish general population and included both men and women with a spectrum of glucose impairments, none of whom were receiving treatment for diabetes. Furthermore, we used the same inclusion criteria when selecting the validation cohort from the larger-population-based SCAPIS cohort. Finally, we obtained similar results for the profiles of gut microbiota when using two different bioinformatics methods, indicating robust correlations between microbial features and deterioration of glycemic responses in our cohorts. In conclusion, we observed a robust covariation of the gut microbiota with IGT, CGI, and T2D, which was independent of metformin. The alterations in the gut microbiota were associated with insulin resistance but not fasting glucose. Our findings are consistent with the possibility that the gut microbiota is a modifiable determinant of T2D closely related to dietary factors. Future studies should test whether restoration of altered microbial functions, such as butyrate production, in individuals with IGT and CGI might constitute a precision medicine approach to prevent or delay the progression to T2D. Limitations of Study Our study has several limitations. Most individuals within the T2D group were diagnosed solely on fasting glucose without a 2 h OGTT for safety reasons. Given the known heterogeneity within T2D (Ahlqvist et al., 2018), this may contribute to the microbial differences observed between the T2D groups in the two cohorts. In addition, we did not include a group with IFG or divide the NGT group into low and high risk in the validation cohort. Finally, our study is cross sectional and our observations therefore require follow up in a longitudinal study and in populations with different ethnic backgrounds. Experimental Model and Subject Details Study Cohorts Two human cohorts were used: a discovery cohort and a subset of the Swedish Cardiopulmonary Bioimage Study (SCAPIS)-Gothenburg cohort (validation cohort); the SCAPIS design has been described elsewhere (Bergström et al., 2015). Both cohorts comprise men and women aged 50-64 years from the Gothenburg area, Sweden, who were recruited at random from the census register. Exclusion criteria were: known diabetes; inflammatory diseases, such as Crohn′s disease, ulcerative colitis, rheumatic diseases; treatment with steroids or immunomodulatory drugs; cancer (unless relapse free for the preceding 5 years); cognitive dysfunction; and treatment for infectious diseases and with antibiotics in the past three months. Subjects were also excluded if they did not understand Swedish and if they were born outside Sweden. All participants gave informed consent and the study was approved by the Ethics Review Board in Gothenburg. Method Details Glucose Status Participants were invited by letter to a screening examination that included a fasting capillary blood glucose measurement and a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Both measurements were performed in the morning (between 7.30 and 11 AM); fasting- and 2-hour post-load capillary blood glucose values were measured with the glucose dehydrogenase technique (Hemocue, Glucose 201 DM, Hemocue AB, Ängelholm, Sweden). The participants were asked to fast overnight and to avoid heavy physical activity both during the previous day and on the morning of the test. Furthermore, participants were encouraged to avoid smoking on the morning of the test. The examination was postponed if participants violated these restrictions or reported a current infection. Subjects were recruited to the cohorts according to their fasting and 2-hour OGTT capillary plasma glucose levels and divided into subgroups using the 1999 World Health Organization criteria (Alberti and Zimmet, 1998). Isolated impaired fasting glucose (IFG) was defined as a fasting glucose level of ≥6.1 to <7.0 mmol/L and a 2-hour OGTT glucose level of <8.9 mmol/L. Isolated impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) was defined as a fasting glucose level of <6.1 mmol/L and a 2-hour OGTT glucose level of ≥8.9 to <12.2 mmol/L. Combined glucose intolerance (CGI) was defined as a fasting glucose level of ≥6.1 to <7.0 mmol/L and a 2-hour OGTT glucose level of ≥8.9 to <12.2 mmol/L. Subjects fulfilling the criteria for type 2 diabetes (T2D), defined as a fasting capillary plasma glucose level of ≥7.0 mmol/L or a 2-hour OGTT capillary plasma glucose level of ≥12.2 mmol/L, were reexamined within 3 weeks. Subjects who met the criteria for T2D at both visits were recruited to a T2D group. If fasting glucose was ≥7.0 mmol/L at either of the examinations, a 2-hour OGTT was not performed. Participants who underwent a second examination that did not confirm a T2D diagnosis were classified as having IFG, IGT, or CGI depending on a combination of the measurements. For those with two measurements of glucose, the second measurement was used in the analyses if both measurements were within the range of the specified glucose subgroup; otherwise, the one consistent with the glucose subgroup was used. Subjects with normal glucose tolerance (NGT), defined as a fasting glucose level of <6.1 mmol/L and a 2-hour OGTT glucose level of <8.9 mmol/L, were also recruited. In the discovery cohort, approximately every fifth person with NGT and a Finnish Diabetes Risk Score (FINDRISC) of <15 was recruited to an NGT group considered to be at low risk of developing T2D. FINDRISC uses variables known to associate with T2D [e.g., body-mass index (BMI), waist circumference, history of antihypertensive drug treatment and high blood glucose, levels of physical activity, and daily consumption of fruits, berries, or vegetables] to calculate the risk of developing T2D within 10 years (Lindström and Tuomilehto, 2003). In addition, all subjects with NGT and either a FINDRISC of ≥15 or a FINDRISC of <15 but with two first-degree relatives with T2D were included in an NGT group considered to be at higher risk of developing T2D. A FINDRISC of ≥15 (Costa et al., 2012) and a first-degree relative with T2D (Lyssenko et al., 2008) have been shown to associate with a high risk for T2D. In the validation cohort, we did not separate the NGT group into low and high risk or include an IFG group on the basis of results from the discovery cohort showing that there were no significant shifts in the microbiome in the high-risk NGT and IFG groups compared with the low-risk NGT group (Figure 1A). The individuals in the NGT group in the validation cohort were selected by matching with the IGT group at the group level for sex, age, and body-mass index (BMI). Other Examinations and Biochemical Assays A venous blood sample for biochemical and other analyses was taken from all participants after an overnight fast, and all participants underwent physical examinations as described in the SCAPIS design (Bergström et al., 2015). In addition, a fecal sample was requested from all participants. Insulin resistance was estimated by: the triglyceride-glucose index, calculated as log [fasting triglyceride level (mg/dL) times fasting plasma glucose level (mg/dL) divided by 2] (Simental-Mendía et al., 2008); the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated as fasting plasma glucose level (mmol/L) times fasting insulin level (microunits/mL) divided by 22.5 (Matthews et al., 1985); and the quantitative insulin sensitivity check index, calculated as 1 divided by [log fasting plasma insulin level (microunits/mL) plus log fasting plasma glucose level (mg/dL)] (Katz et al., 2000). Plasma levels of imidazole propionate had previously been measured in a subgroup of the discovery cohort (Koh et al., 2018). Fecal Microbial Profiling and Analysis Stool samples were collected at home and stored at room temperature for a maximum of 36 h before storage at -80°C. Fecal genomic DNA was extracted as described previously (Deschasaux et al., 2018) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 instrument (150 bp; paired-end) at GATC Biotech. After removal of low-quality reads (with a quality score <20), we obtained on average 26.5 million (range 15.5 to 63.7 million) high-quality paired-end reads for each fecal sample (Table S4). A newly assembled gene catalog containing 15,186,403 non-redundant microbial genes (48.5% with complete open reading frame; minimum, average, and maximum gene lengths are 102, 674, 87999 bp, respectively) was generated using the metagenomic analysis toolkit MOCAT2 (Kultima et al., 2016) (Figure S7). The taxonomic and functional annotations of newly assembled genes were performed using BLASTN and BLASTX (Camacho et al., 2009) against the NCBI reference genome (release 84) (Pruitt et al., 2012) and KEGG (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000) databases, respectively. The MEDUSA pipeline was used to obtain gene abundance profiles across samples by mapping the reads to the newly assembled gene catalog. On average, 75.1% ± 0.05 (mean ± standard error) of the high-quality paired-end reads could be mapped to the gene catalog (Table S4). The resulting gene abundance profiles were further rarefied to 22 million reads per sample (mean abundance profile across 50 repeated rarefactions) for downstream identification of co-abundant gene groups based on the canopy clustering algorithm. In summary, we obtained 1427 co-abundant gene groups with more than 700 genes (known as metagenomic species) and those present in at least 50 samples were used for differential abundance analysis. For microbial compositional profiling, we used: (1) reference-free canopy clustering, based on co-abundance of genes across all metagenomic samples in both the discovery and validation cohorts, to identify metagenomic species and to calculate the absolute abundance (i.e., number of rarefied reads mapped) of each metagenomic species represented by median abundance profile of the gene group as indicated previously (Nielsen et al., 2014); and (2) the MEDUSA pipeline, based on read mapping to a defined reference genome catalog for calculation of the bacterial relative abundance (Karlsson et al., 2014) (Figure S7). The resulting gene catalog with KEGG orthology (KO) annotations was then utilized for microbial functional profiling based on MEDUSA (Karlsson et al., 2014). For principal coordinates analysis, distance-based redundancy analysis, and the random forest model development, an additional 966 co-abundant gene groups with more than 100 genes were also included. Taxonomic annotations were based on aggregated taxonomic assignments of all genes within each co-abundant gene group as described before (Nielsen et al., 2014) with additional inputs from two distinct phylogenetic methods (one depending on the whole genome (Xu and Hao, 2009) and the other on a conserved dnaE1 gene marker (Wu et al., 2014)). The profile hidden markov models of five key genes (4hbt, but, atoA/D, and buk) representing four different bacterial butyrate-producing pathways (Vital et al., 2014) were used to screen those genes in our gene catalog and to identify the butyrate producers among the metagenomic species by using HMMER (Finn et al., 2011) (Table S1). Relative abundance of atoA/D and buk in each sample was calculated based on reads mapping to genes annotated as K01035/K01034, and K00929, respectively. For relative abundance of 4hbt and but, which lack KEGG annotation and share substantial sequence similarity (Charrier et al., 2006), reads were mapped to those two genes screened from our gene catalog first and then combined. Comparative genomic analysis for ortholog screening among 12 phylogenetically related butyrate producers divided into two distinct clusters was performed by using OrthoMCL (Li et al., 2003). Only ortholog groups present in at least 5 out of 6 metagenomic species from one cluster but in no more than 1 out of 6 metagenomic species in the other cluster were extracted for functional analysis and additional annotations for virulence factors and oxidoreductases by using BLASTP against VFDB (Liu et al., 2019) and KEGG (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000) databases, respectively. Seven key genes involved in biotin biosynthesis pathway (Magnúsdóttir et al., 2015) were screened for relative changes in abundance in our metagenomics dataset. The principal coordinates analysis on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix was performed with significance levels determined by PERMANOVA test using the adonis2 function (permutations=10,000). Before testing for the marginal (type III) effect of each variable to the microbial variations, all variables were subjected to removal of collinearities (or redundant constraints) based on the variation inflation factor with a cutoff of 10, resulting in 25 nonlinear variables (age, sex, BMI, waist-hip ratio, height, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, 2-hour OGTT glucose, HbA1c, hemoglobin, eosinophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, neutrophils, high sensitivity C-reactive protein, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, serum vitamin D, steps per day, triglyceride-glucose index, treatment with statins, treatment with proton pump inhibitors, homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance, homeostatic model assessment of β cell function). The marginal effect resulting from the distance-based redundancy analysis was calculated using the capscale function after partialling out contributions from all other nonlinear variables with the significance level using the anova.cca function (PERMANOVA test; permutations=10,000). Additional adjusted R2 was calculated using the RsquareAdj function. All these functions are in the vegan package (Oksanen et al., 2015). Quantification and Statistical Analysis All statistical and modeling analyses were performed in the R environment (Team, 2015). All the available data were used for each analysis, with an implicit assumption that within-group data were missing completely at random. Nonparametric two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used throughout the study for statistical testing between groups. The random forest classification model was built using the caret package (Kuhn, 2008) with the following key parameters: ntree=5000, metric=‘Kappa’, and mtry chosen from a series of values ranging from 2 to 800. The optimal model was chosen based on the tree with the highest Kappa value obtained from 10-fold cross validation repeated 10 times with an additional ‘up’ sampling strategy to balance the sample sizes between groups. The odds ratio for risk of CGI/T2D for each metagenomic species (divided into two factorial groups based on their median absolute abundance level across samples) was calculated based on logistic regression adjusted for BMI, age, sex, and medication. Adjusted P values were calculated using the qvalue package based on the default settings (Storey et al., 2015). Adjusted and raw P values <0.05 were considered to be significant.
