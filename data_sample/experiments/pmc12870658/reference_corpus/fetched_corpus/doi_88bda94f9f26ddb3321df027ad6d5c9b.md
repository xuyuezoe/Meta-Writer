# Renal activation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase in rats with autosomal-dominant polycystic kidney disease.

## Abstract

Background: Abnormal proliferation of renal tubule epithelial cells is a central factor in the biogenesis and sustained expansion of cysts in autosomal-dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). Recent evidence from in vitro studies of human cyst wall epithelial cells has implicated a role for the mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinase pathway in this aberrant proliferation. To determine the extent to which this signaling pathway is involved in cyst pathogenesis in vivo, we measured the expression of select components of the MAP kinase cascade in Han:SPRD rats with ADPKD at an early stage of the disease.

Methods: Kidneys of 8-week-old normal Han:SPRD rats (+/+) or rats heterozygous (Cy/+) for ADPKD were examined by Western blot analysis and immunohistochemistry to determine the expression of extracellular-regulated kinase (ERK), phosphorylated ERK (P-ERK), Raf-1 (MAPKKK), phosphorylated Raf-1 (P-Raf-1), B-Raf, Rap-1 and phosphorylated protein kinase A (P-PKA).

Results: P-ERK was expressed to a greater extent in Cy/+ kidneys (3.74 +/- 1.07 fold) than in normal kidneys, whereas ERK abundance was not different. P-Raf-1 levels were higher in Cy/+ than in +/+ kidneys (1.53 +/- 0.08 fold) consistent with upstream stimulation of receptor tyrosine kinase. B-Raf and Raf-1 abundances were greater in Cy/+ than in +/+ (1.74 +/- 0.25 and 1.27 +/- 0.08 fold, respectively). In Cy/+, immunohistochemistry showed increased P-ERK and B-Raf expression in the abnormal mural epithelial cells within cysts. These findings, together with the detection of P-PKA and the small G protein, Rap-1, in cyst epithelial cells, implicate a potential role for cyclic adenosine monophosphate (AMP) in the activation of ERK in ADPKD cells.

Conclusions: We conclude that the MAP kinase pathway is activated to the level of ERK in the abnormal mural epithelial cells lining cysts in animals with a dominantly inherited type of polycystic kidney disease. We suggest that cAMP, acting through PKA, Rap-1 and B-Raf, may contribute to the activation of ERK in a way that complements receptor tyrosine kinase-mediated agonists in the promotion of cyst enlargement.

Keywords cyclic AMP adenylyl cyclase B-Raf MAPK tyrosine kinase ERK cell proliferation In autosomal-dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), cysts arise in renal tubules due to loss of function mutations in proteins involved in epithelial development and repair1 and 4. Evidence indicates that germ cell mutations are insufficient to cause cysts to form in renal tubules; rather a second (somatic) “hit” in the otherwise normal allele is required to initiate cyst formation2,5 and 7. In human ADPKD, each cyst that develops in widely scattered tubules appears to derive from a single aberrantly proliferative daughter cell. The pathogenetic steps in cyst biogenesis beyond the “second hit” can be examined most clearly in spontaneous animal models of renal cystic disease. In one of these, the Han:SPRD Cy rat, a mutation has been mapped to rat chromosome 58. The renal tubules in these animals appear to develop normally in heterozygous (Cy/+) animals until about 2 to 3 weeks of age, whereupon scattered pars recta segments of proximal tubules exhibit focal areas with intense epithelial cellular proliferation, morphologic de-differentiation and tubule basement membrane thickening9 and 11. Genetic evidence of a somatic or “second hit” type of mutation has not been confirmed in the rat, although such a mechanism seems likely in view of the focal nature of cyst formation and the extraordinarily rapid progression of the disease in homozygous (Cy/Cy) animals in which both alleles are abnormal. In the Han:SPRD rat it appears that the focal patches of proliferating tubule epithelial cells eventually extend the tubule wall to form a saccular cyst. The cystic portions of the tubule of origin are associated in most cases with dramatic thickening of the underlying tubule basement membrane and subjacent interstitial compartment, together with the influx of macrophages and nonspecific interstitial cells12. Focal epithelial proliferation, remodeling of tubule basement membranes, increased apoptosis and interstitial inflammation and fibrosis, forces at work in all polycystic disorders, conjointly lead to the loss of renal function13,14. Recent evidence indicates that in human ADPKD, germ-line mutations of either PKD1 or PKD2 genes together with a somatic mutation in the normal allele, cause impaired regulation of non-specific, Ca++-permeable cation channels displayed in the increased proliferation of relatively undifferentiated tubule epithelial cells15. Cells cultured from the walls of human ADPKD cysts proliferate at an accelerated rate in response to cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)16,17. On the other hand, this nucleotide ordinarily inhibits the growth of cells derived from normal human kidneys18,19, suggesting that fundamental programs in the terminal differentiation and regulation of growth in renal tubule cells are radically altered by mutations in the polycystins. Although the cause of ADPKD rests in mutated genes, the rate at which kidneys enlarge and renal function declines can be modified by extra-genetic factors. In the Han:SPRD Cy rat, for example, acidosis, potassium depletion and oral protein loading, which individually increase renal tubule cell hypertrophy in normal animals, greatly accelerated the enlargement of cysts and progression of renal dysfunction20 . Conversely, the administration of alkali diminished the rate of cyst growth and protected renal function in the Han:SPRD model of ADPKD21 and the administration of an antiproliferative 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor or an anti-inflammatory corticosteroid slowed cyst expansion and reduced the rate of functional decline22. These findings have reinforced the view that in human PKD the rate of renal cyst growth is decided by more than the mutations in polycystin. In this view, mutated polycystins are thought to establish an altered cellular phenotype within renal tubules in which ordinary regulatory hormones, growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines have prominent roles in the promotion of disease progression. Recent studies have led to the view that the adenylyl cyclase and receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) pathways may be mediators of cyst growth and disease progression in polycystic kidney disorders16,17,23 and 25. In the current study, we used immunoblotting and immunohistochemistry to explore key components of these pathways in cystic kidneys of the Han:SPRD Cy rat, a well-characterized model of ADPKD26. The results support the view that the cAMP and RTK pathways have convergent and complementary roles in promoting disease progression in this model of ADPKD. METHODS Han:SPRD Cy rats of the Sprague-Dawley strain were obtained from the original colony in Hanover, Germany26 . They were maintained with free access to water and food (Purina Rodent Chow, Bethlehem, PA, USA) at the University of Kansas Medical Center as previously described27. Heterozygotes were bred in order to increase the yield of Cy/+ in each litter. Offspring were weaned at 3 weeks of age and homozygous cystic animals (Cy/Cy) were sacrificed. A noncystic strain of normal (+/+) Sprague-Dawley animals served as controls for the Cy/Cy and Cy/+. In most experiments, animals were sacrificed at 8 weeks of age. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (Baxter Healthcare Corp., Deerfield, IL, USA) and both kidneys removed rapidly causing fatal exsanguination. Both kidneys were quickly weighed. The left kidney was homogenized in lysis buffer to extract proteins and the right kidney was sectioned and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The genotype was determined by gross inspection of the kidney sections. Whole kidneys in lysis buffer were homogenized by Polytron for 20 seconds in 1 mL/g kidney weight of ice-cold lysis buffer (TLB; 20 mmol/L Tris [pH 7.4], 137 mmol/L NaCl, 25 mmol/L beta-glycerophosphate, 2 mmol/L ethylene diamine tetra-acetic acid [EDTA], 1 mmol/L sodium orthovanadate, 2 mmol/L dithiothreitol, 1 mmol/L phenylmethlsulfonyl fluoride, 5 μg/mL aprotinin, and 5 μg/mL leupeptin with 1% Triton X-100). Insoluble material was removed by centrifugation (3000 × g, 40 minutes.). The protein content of each kidney lysate was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). Samples were stored at -80°C. Western blot Lysates (protein, 20 μg/lane, except for 60 μg/lane for detecting the P-PKA regulatory subunit) were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, 10% in a mini-protean 3 cell electrophoresis module assembly, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (Optitran; Midwest Scientific, Valley Park, MO, USA) at 4°C. After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 2% or 5% milk in TBS-T (20 mmol/L Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 137 mmol/L NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20). Blocking was carried out for 1 hour at room temperature or overnight at 4°C. Blocked membranes were incubated in primary antibody diluted 1:2000 to 5000 in 5% milk-TBS-T for 2 hours at room temperature or overnight at 4°C. Membranes were then washed three times with TBS-T and incubated with secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) diluted 1:2000 to 5000 in 2% or 5% milk in TBS-T for 1 hour. The membranes were washed three times with TBS-T and proteins visualized on x-ray film using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (ECL; Amersham Life Sciences, Arlington Heights, IL, USA). Blots were scanned using Scion-modified NIH image software for PC (Scion Corporation, Frederick, MD, USA; http://www.scioncorp.com) and images were quantified in arbitrary intensity units. Male +/+ kidneys were used as the point of reference (1.00). Absolute intensity values of Cy/+ and Cy/Cy kidneys were compared to +/+ males and expressed as fold changes. Immunohistochemistry Right kidneys were embedded in paraffin and immunohistochemistry performed on 4 μm thick sections. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. To unmask antigens, slides were heated to 100°C for 15 minutes in 10 mmol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating sections in 0.3% H2O2/methanol for 30 minutes. Sections were incubated with primary antibody at a concentration of 1:100 to 10,000 overnight at 4°C. After incubation with biotinylated antirabbit secondary antibody at a concentration of 1:50 to 500, peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin (Histofine: Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan) immunoreaction products were developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, with standardized development times. Sections were then counterstained with Omni hematoxylin. Antibodies Western blots. Primary antibodies from Santa Cruz Biotechnolgy (Santa Cruz, CA, USA) included ERK-1 (K-23, sc-94), P-ERK (E-4, sc-7383), B-Raf (C-19, sc-166), Raf-1 (C-12, sc-133), Rap-1 (sc-12905), PKA Cα (C-20, sc-903), PKA RIIα (M-20, sc-909), P-PKA Cα (Ser 96, sc-903), and P-PKA RIIα (Ser96) from Upstate Biotechnology (Waltham, MA, USA). These primary antibodies were also used for immunohistochemistry with the exceptions that P-ERK (Sigma M8159, Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) and Raf-1 (Santa Cruz E-10, sc-7267) were substituted for this purpose. Secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP were goat antirabbit immunoglobulin (IgG) (sc-2054), rabbit antimouse IgG (sc-2055), donkey antigoat IgG (sc-2056) from Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, Santa Cruz, CA, USA, and goat antirabbit IgG (PI-1000) from Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA. Secondary antibodies conjugated to biotin for immunohistochemistry were goat antirabbit IgG (111-065-003), goat antimouse IgG (115-065-003) and rabbit antimouse IgG (305-065-003) from Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA. Blocking peptide experiments utilized P-ERK (Santa Cruz sc-7383P) and B-Raf (Santa Cruz sc-166P). In these, the primary antibodies were mixed with a fivefold (by weight) excess of blocking peptide in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated overnight at 4°C. This reagent was then added to tissue sections to give an amount of antibody-antigen complex equal to that in the experimental section. Mean and SEM were calculated in the usual way. Levels of significance (P> 0.05) were determined by unpaired t test. DISCUSSION The Han:SPRD rat model of ADPKD arose within a colony of Sprague-Dawley rats in Hanover, Germany, and has served as a valuable model for studies of cyst pathogenesis and treatment. Although the chromosomal location of the rat Cy gene is not syntenic with PKD 1 or PKD 2 in humans, the renal features of the respective disorders are similar. The cysts develop focally in segments of renal tubule. In both species the development of cysts and progression to renal insufficiency are gender dependent, although the protection offered by female gender is greatest in the rat27,28. Hypertension, a feature of ADPKD in both rat and humans29,30, develops long before the disease progresses to end-stage function. Interestingly, rats homozygous for the Cy gene are born with relatively normal-appearing kidneys, but within 3 weeks die of uremia due to massively expanded cystic kidneys Figure 1. It appears that in Cy/Cy animals the threshold for cystogenesis is drastically lowered and cysts develop throughout the nephrons and collecting ducts at break-neck speed. Similar behavior is observed in homozygous mice with targeted mutations in pkd-1 and pkd-2, except that the animals die in utero of vascular complications and enlarged cystic kidneys31,32. The human counterpart of the homozygous ADPKD rat and mouse has not been described. Studies of the Han:SPRD Cy rat have revealed striking changes in the morphology of cells undergoing cystic transformation in the relatively few renal tubules destined to become cysts. In the early stages of cyst formation, patches of abnormal cells that appear poorly differentiated and hyperplastic are found within otherwise normal proximal renal tubules9,10. The abnormal cells express proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), indicative of increased mitogenesis, and mRNA for type IV collagen11. There is an abrupt transition between normal-appearing columnar cells with brush borders and cytoplasm that stains pink with hematoxylin and eosin and adjacent flattened cells with a high nucleus:cytoplasm ratio and light-staining cytoplasm Figures 3 and 4. The tubule basement membrane immediately beneath the normal-appearing cells is thin and the subjacent interstitium is narrow and compact; by contrast, the tubule basement membrane underlying the cystic transformed cells is thickened. The widened interstitium contains mononuclear cells Figures 3,4,5,6, identified in a recent study as macrophages12. It appears in this rodent model that cysts may arise as focal epithelial growths within widely scattered tubules. These patches of proliferating epithelium eventually lead to ballooning of the tubule wall and to the formation of a saccular dilatation recognized as a cyst. Were this process to proceed for several years, as it does in humans, more than likely the cysts would eventually separate from the parent tubule and expand as isolated sacs owing to fluid transported into the lumen by transepithelial secretion33,34. Clearly, the abnormal proliferation of tubule epithelial cells lies at the heart of cyst formation and expansion in ADPKD19,35 and 38. Studies in cultured cyst epithelial cells removed from the discarded kidneys of patients with ADPKD have implicated cAMP as an important mitogen that drives abnormal rates of proliferation16,17, as well as stimulating the transepithelial secretion of fluid33,34. Moreover, it appears that the mitogenic effect of cAMP is a cellular phenotypic marker of abnormal function. cAMP stimulates proliferation of human cyst epithelial cells in culture together with the increased phosphorylation of MAP kinase (ERK). Inhibition of MAPKK (MEK) with a highly specific inhibitor blocked the mitogenic response to cAMP of these cells as well as the activation of ERK. By contrast, cAMP does not stimulate proliferation or ERK activation in cells derived from normal human renal cortex16. A major objective of the current study was to determine if the MAP kinase pathway was activated in an animal model of ADPKD, and more specifically if the activated kinase was expressed within the abnormal cells that comprise renal cysts. In this, in contrast to an earlier study, we used antibodies to phosphorylated forms of ERK to assess the activity of this kinase in situ as this permitted quantitation by Western blot analysis, as well as localization by immunohistochemistry. P-ERK levels were greatly elevated in the kidneys of cystic animals relative to normal littermates of the same age, whereas levels of total ERK were not different Figure 2. This result is consistent with the selective activation of ERK within cystic kidneys. Immunohistochemistry localized the source of the increased P-ERK levels to the mural epithelial cells lining the cysts. Moreover, in cysts in which the transition from normal to cystic phenotype was in progress, the highest levels of P-ERK were found in the abnormal-appearing cells Figures 3 and 4. P-ERK levels were evinced to a much greater extent in the apparently transformed cells than in the morphologically normal adjacent cells within the cysts, or in adjacent noncystic proximal and distal tubules. This raises the interesting possibility that cells with this abnormal morphologic phenotype may be sensitive to stimulation by agonists that activate the receptor tyrosine kinase/MAP kinase pathway. In the current study we found that the levels of P-Raf-1 and Raf-1 were slightly, but significantly, increased in the kidneys of Cy/+ animals, indicating that this kinase may be up-regulated Figure 2c. Examination of the cysts did not reveal a source of the increase in P-Raf-1 expression determined by Western blot analysis as the Raf-1 expression in the mural cells lining cysts relative to adjacent normal tubules was not increased Figure 5f. B-Raf abundance was increased in Cy/+ kidneys as determined by both Western blot analysis Figure 2b and this seemed to be accounted for by an increase in the expression of B-Raf in the mural epithelial cells of cysts (Figure 5 A and D). These findings suggest that B-Raf abundance is up-regulated in cyst epithelium, although the extent to which this kinase may have been activated could not be determined since a suitable antibody to the phorphorylated form is not available. Studies of cultured ADPKD cyst cells have implicated an unusual mechanism through which cAMP may mediate a mitogenic action in cyst epithelial cells. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) was found to activate ERK in cultured ADPKD cyst cells16. Moreover, cAMP and EGF had complementary and synergistic effects on cell proliferation and ERK activation in contrast to an anticomplementary action of these agents in cells cultured from normal human kidney cortex. In preliminary studies, Yamaguchi et al (unpublished observations, 2002) have found that cAMP activates B-Raf, a serine threonine kinase that activates MEK39,40, and confirmed that EGF increased the levels of P-ERK in cultured ADPKD cyst cells. Although cAMP activated B-Raf and EGF did not, the adenylyl cyclase agonists had an additive effect on the expression of P-ERK. These findings indicate that adenylyl cyclase and receptor tyrosine kinase signaling may operate in ADPKD cyst epithelial cells through parallel pathways that converge in the complementary activation of ERK. While the current study does not prove that B-Raf is activated in Han:SPRD kidneys, the modest increase in the abundance of B-Raf is consistent with the view that this component of the MAP kinase pathway may be up-regulated and perhaps activated by cAMP. Further evidence in support of a role for cAMP in the Cy/+ rat was sought by determining the expression of activated subunits of PKA in cyst epithelial cells Figure 6d. The PKA holoenzyme is a heterotetramer composed of two N-terminally dimerized regulatory subunits combined with two catalytic subunits, which are ubiquitously expressed in rodents41. When PKA is activated by cAMP subunits of the kinase localize within the nucleus. In the current study, PKA Cα was detected in the nuclei of renal epithelial cells in both normal and cystic animals, consistent with activation of PKA by cAMP Figure 6 c and d. Western blot analysis of the catalytic subunit was not successful. On the other hand, expression of the phosphorylated regulatory subunit appeared to be increased in Western blots to a greater level in Cy/+ than in +/+Figure 6e. Taken together, these results provide indirect evidence that PKA is activated by cAMP in cystic kidneys of Han:SPRD Cy/+ rats. Rap-1 is a small G protein that activates B-Raf upon stimulation by P-PKA40,42. Rap-1 levels were greater in Cy/+ than in +/+ kidneys Figure 6e and the protein was expressed at a greater level in the cysts than in noncystic tubules Figure 6 a and b. Thus, the cyst cells harbor proteins that may enable cAMP to activate ERK through the intermediacy of B-Raf. B-Raf is distributed in relatively few organs and its activation is highly cell specific. In neuronal cells, for example, B-Raf is abundant and activated by Rap-140,42. Thus, elevated levels of cyclic AMP stimulate ERK through the successive activation of Rap-1, B-Raf and MEK. By contrast, B-Raf is not activated by cAMP in astrocytic cells; rather the cyclic nucleotide binds to Raf-1 leading to a blockade of the receptor tyrosine kinase cascade at MEK. PKA binds to B-Raf at two sites43. One of these sites leads to the activation of B-Raf by PKA and Rap-1 whereas binding to the other site blocks this stimulation. Binding of PKA to the inhibitory site appears to be controlled by 14-3-3, a ubiquitous protein within cells. It is postulated that 14-3-3 binds to the inhibitory site, shielding it from the inhibitory action of PKA leaving the stimulatory site unopposed and responsive to activation by Rap-1 and activated PKA. In the last analysis the capacity of cAMP to activate ERK may depend upon the relative abundances of B-Raf and 14-3-3 as renal tubule cells are transformed from a normal to an abnormal phenotype by polycystin mutations. Figure 7 is a hypothetical scheme based on the current work together with published information. cAMP is frequently elevated in renal cells owing to the actions of vasopressin, parathyroid hormone, prostaglandins, secretin, and vasoactive intestinal peptide; thus, this second messenger has an important role in determining the extent to which the MAP kinase pathway may be activated by extracellular agonists. Normally, cAMP is not mitogenic and may serve to dampen mitogenic signals mediated through the receptor tyrosine kinase/MAP kinase cascade. On the other hand, in cyst epithelial cells, cAMP is mitogenic and its action complements that of receptor tyrosine kinase activation of the MAP kinase pathway. Thus, cyst cells may be unusually sensitive to mitogenic stimulation by normal levels of circulating hormones and autocoids that increase the production of cAMP.
