# Kidney Energetics and Cyst Burden in Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease: A Pilot Study.

## Abstract

Rationale & objective: In this pilot study, we hypothesized that autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is characterized by impaired kidney oxidative metabolism that associates with kidney size and cyst burden.

Study design: Cross-sectional study.

Setting & participants: Twenty adults with ADPKD (age, 31±6 years; 65% women; body mass index [BMI], 26.8 [22.7-30.4] kg/m^2; estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR, 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine], 103±18mL/min/1.73m^2; height-adjusted total kidney volume [HTKV], 731±370mL/m; Mayo classifications 1B [5%], 1C [42%], 1D [21%], and 1E [32%]) and 11 controls in normal weight category (NWC) (age, 25±3 years; 45% women; BMI, 22.5 [21.7-24.2] kg/m2; eGFR, 113±15mL/min/1.73m^2; HTKV, 159±31mL/m) at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus.

Predictors: ADPKD status (yes/no) and severity (Mayo classifications).

Outcome: HTKV and cyst burden by magnetic resonance imaging, kidney oxidative metabolism, and perfusion by ^11C-acetate positron emission tomography/computed tomography, insulin sensitivity by hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamps (presented as ratio of M-value of steady state insulin concentration [M/I]).

Analytical approach: For categorical variables, χ^2/Fisher's exact tests, and for continuous variables t tests/Mann-Whitney U tests. Pearson correlation was used to estimate the relationships between variables.

Results: Compared with NWC individuals, the participants with ADPKD exhibited lower mean±SD M/I ratio (0.586±0.205 vs 0.424±0.171 [mg/kg lean/min]/(μIU/mL), P=0.04), lower median cortical perfusion (1.93 [IQR, 1.80-2.09] vs 0.68 [IQR, 0.47-1.04] mL/min/g, P<0.001) and lower median total kidney oxidative metabolism (0.17 [IQR, 0.16-0.19] vs. 0.14 [IQR, 0.12-0.15] min^-1, P=0.001) in voxel-wise models excluding cysts. HTKV correlated inversely with cortical perfusion (r: -0.83, P < 0.001), total kidney oxidative metabolism (r: -0.61, P<0.001) and M/I (r: -0.41, P = 0.03).

Limitations: Small sample size and cross-sectional design.

Conclusions: Adults with ADPKD and preserved kidney function exhibited impaired renal perfusion and kidney oxidative metabolism across a wide range of cysts and kidney enlargements.

Funding: Grants from government (National Institutes of Health, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) and not-for-profit (JDRF) entities.

Trial registration: Registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with study numbers NCT04407481 and NCT04074668.

Plain-language summary: In our study, we explored how a common genetic kidney condition, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), relates to kidney metabolism. ADPKD leads to the growth of numerous cysts in the kidneys, which can impact their ability to work properly. We wanted to understand the kidneys' ability to process oxygen and blood flow in ADPKD. Our approach involved using advanced imaging techniques to observe kidney metabolism and blood flow in people with ADPKD compared with healthy individuals. We discovered that those with ADPKD had significant changes in kidney oxygen metabolism even when their kidney function was still normal. These findings are crucial as they provide deeper insights into ADPKD, potentially guiding future treatments to target these changes.

## Introduction

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), a disorder characterized by development and growth of multiple kidney cysts, is the most common monogenic cause of kidney failure. 1 – 3 Yet, the mechanisms underlying the cyst expansion and kidney function decline are incompletely understood, which contributes to the limited therapeutic options available for people afflicted by ADPKD. Metabolic reprogramming including reduced kidney oxidative metabolism is emerging as a unifying pathway in the development and progression of ADPKD in animals and a potential therapeutic target. However, human data are limited. 4 – 8

The kidneys, ranking second to the heart in oxygen (O 2 ) consumption per tissue mass, are highly metabolically active and vulnerable to oxidative stress and hypoxia. 6 , 7 , 9 Polycystins, which are products of the PKD1 and PKD2 genes commonly mutated in patients with ADPKD, are speculated to play a key role in regulating mitochondrial energy production and insulin sensitivity across various tissues. 10 – 13 Emerging research suggests that the metabolic reprogramming in ADPKD includes insulin resistance, along with impairments in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), both critical pathways in cellular energy production. 4 – 8 The metabolic reprogramming in ADPKD thus potentially exacerbates the disease’s pathological effects on cyst expansion. 13

Imaging biomarkers in humans that can interrogate kidney oxidative metabolism in ADPKD are needed to inform our understanding of the metabolic determinants of cyst growth, and inform the development of new therapeutic targets while changes may still be reversible. Accordingly, in this pilot study we quantified kidney oxidative metabolism using positron emission tomography (PET) to determine 11 C-acetate turnover in the TCA cycle. Kidney volume and cyst burden were assessed by kidney magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Recognizing that insulin sensitivity is an important determinant of metabolic function and energetics, we also assessed whole-body insulin sensitivity by employing the gold-standard hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp. Fat and lean mass were quantified by composition using dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA). We hypothesized that young adults with ADPKD would exhibit lower kidney oxidative metabolism and whole-body insulin sensitivity compared to controls in normal weight category (NWC), associating with kidney cyst burden.

## Methods

Methods

Study design and participants

Two distinct groups, ADPKD and NWC (body mass index [BMI] < 27 kg/m 2 ), were included in this analysis, with participants from the cross-sectional PErfusioN, OxyGen ConsUmptIon and ENergetics in ADPKD (PENGUIN) study ( NCT04407481 ) and the cross-sectional Control of Renal Oxygen Consumption, Mitochondrial Dysfunction, and Insulin Resistance (CROCODILE) study ( NCT04074668 ), respectively. The participants with ADPKD were recruited from the University of Colorado PKD Recruitment database and NWC from advertisements. ADPKD was defined by the modified Pei-Ravine criteria. 14 Exclusion criteria are detailed in Figure S1 . The PENGUIN and CROCODILE cohorts were approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB). Participants provided informed consent to participate in the study ( Figure S1 ).

Clinical measurements

Laboratory assays were performed by the University of Colorado Clinical and Translational Research Centers (CTRC) Core Labs. Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was also determined from a 24-hour urine collection based on analysis from a sample of the total collection. GFR was estimated using the 2021 CKD-EPI Creatinine Equation. Adjusted body weight was calculated by subtracting out kidney weight, assuming a tissue density equal to that of water (1 g/cm 3 ), thus removing the contribution of kidney size to body mass index (BMI) classification. 15 BMI was then calculated using adjusted body weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (measured at clinical research clinics). The Mayo Clinic’s ADPKD Imaging Classification Model, considering kidney volume, age, and height, was used to classify participants into mild/moderate (Mayo 1A-C) or severe (Mayo 1D-E) categories. 16

Hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamps and dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA)

A hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp was performed with two stages (8 mU/m 2 /min and 40 mU/m 2 /min insulin infusion), along with an intravenous infusion of 20% dextrose titrated to maintain euglycemia, based on bedside glucose readings every 5 minutes as in our previous studies to determine insulin sensitivity. 17 , 18 Blood samples for insulin were collected at the beginning and during steady state of each stage of the clamp. Steady-state glucose infusion rate (GIR, mg/lean kg/min) at 40 mU/m 2 /min was used to determine whole-body insulin sensitivity. The M/I ratio was calculated by dividing the GIR (M-value) by the corresponding steady-state insulin concentration (I) achieved during the clamp procedure. The use of M/I ratio is preferable over M-value alone as it provides a more accurate assessment of insulin sensitivity by accounting for the achieved insulin levels during the clamp procedure, thereby minimizing the impact of inter-individual variability in insulin concentrations, such as differences in insulin clearance, body composition, and other physiological factors.

Participants also underwent a DXA scan by standard methods on a Hologic device (Waltham, MA) to determine lean and fat mass, as well as regional body composition including visceral fat. 19 Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) was indexed by dividing VAT in kg by height in m 2 . 20

Kidney MRI

All participants underwent a kidney MRI scan on a Skyra Siemens Magnetom 3.0T scanner at the University of Colorado Research Imaging Center. The conventional exam included T1 and T2 weighted axial and coronal images. Total kidney volumes (TKV) were computed using Analyze software (Analyze 14.0, Mayo, Rochester, MN), and normalized to height (HtTKV [mL/m] = TKV [mL] / height [m]). Segmentation of kidneys, liver, and cysts were performed by running deep learning-based automatic segmentation algorithms, as previously described. 21 – 23 Additional details about the kidney MRI are provided in Item S1 and example images of ADPKD including cyst segmentation are shown in Figure S2 and S3 .

11 C-acetate positron emission tomography (PET)

A Philips Gemini TF 64 was used for PET/CT imaging. A regional CT scan (40 mA·s) centered at the abdomen was performed for attenuation correction and PET registration.

PET image processing was performed in PMOD (version 3.7, Zurich Switzerland). The right and left renal cortex regions of interest (ROI) were defined semi-automatically using iso-contours on an average PET image (from 40 s to 4.5 min post-injection). Manual corrections were applied based on the corresponding CT image to remove large blood vessels and non-kidney tissue. A whole-kidney ROI was also defined based on the MRI kidney volumes registered to the PET/CT images. For participants with ADPKD, we also produced a cyst-free whole-kidney ROI using the MRI-based cyst segmentation. The blood signal was obtained from a region of interest (ROI) over the descending aorta, corrected for the presence of 11 C-labeled metabolites, 24 and scaled based on the blood sample activity.

The 11 C-acetate turnover in the TCA cycle 25 , 26 was estimated for each ROI using a one-tissue, two compartment, pharmacokinetic model with parameters K 1 (tracer uptake), k 2 (tracer clearance, rate of CO 2 production), and v b (blood volume fraction). Blood flow (F) was estimated using the K 1 values and assuming an extraction fraction of 0.52. 26 We also calculated the ratio of oxidative rate ( k 2 ) to perfusion (F), which serves as an index of the balance between oxygen demand and supply. An elevated k 2 /F ratio denotes increased oxygen demand relative to supply, highlighting a mismatch between oxygen demand and supply A voxel-wise pharmacokinetic model provided parametric maps of K 1 and k 2 by fitting a one-tissue compartment model using linear ridge regression with spatial constraint. 27 Finally, threshold values for K 1 and k 2 were applied to the parametric maps to isolate kidney voxels with normal perfusion/tissue integrity and normal metabolism respectively. Additional details about the 11 C-acetate PET are provided in Item S1 , and K 1 and k 2 within the context of the TCA cycle are illustrated in Figure S4 . Example images of 11 C-acetate PET scans by group are shown in Figures S5 and S6 .

Statistical analysis

The sample size of 10 participants per group aimed for 80% power to detect an effect size of d = 0.62 for any pairwise comparison of NWC, mild/moderate ADPKD and severe ADPKD in metabolically active kidney tissue volume. Participant characteristics were summarized as count and percentages, mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) based on visual inspection of histograms for distribution ( Figure S7 ). The NWC and ADPKD groups were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and using t -tests and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Multiple linear regression models were used for PET driven parameters to adjust for age, sex, and BMI. To avoid over-adjustment, associations between DXA parameters and kidney structure and function were not adjusted for BMI due to the DXA parameters’ collinear relationship with BMI. Pearson correlations among clamp, PET, MRI, and DXA parameters were computed. All correlations were calculated for the whole group, except for total liver volume, total cyst volumes, and total cyst numbers which were limited to the ADPKD group only. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Python v.3.9.6 and R v.4.2.2.

## Results

Results

Clinical characteristics of cohort

Twenty young adults with ADPKD and 11 NWC were included in the study. Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1 . Briefly, participants with ADPKD were slightly older (31±6 vs. 25±3 years) with a higher BMI (27 [23,30] vs. 23 [22, 24] kg/m 2 ) than NWC. The distribution of men and women did not differ significantly between the disease groups. Participants with ADPKD also exhibited lower eGFR and higher urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) than the NWC but were still within normal limits. In the Mayo ADPKD Imaging Classification, 1 participant with ADPKD met criteria for class 1B, 8 for class 1C, 4 for class 1D and 6 for class 1E. Participants classified as 1D-E (severe ADPKD; n=10) had slightly higher BMI than participants in 1A-C (mild/moderate ADPKD; n=9) and overall were higher than NWC ( Figure 1 , Table S1 ). Seventeen ADPKD participants had genotype data; 16 had a PKD1 and 1 had a PKD2 mutation.

Body composition and whole-body insulin sensitivity

Participants with ADPKD demonstrated greater body fat percentages (34.2±8.8 vs. 25.5±5.0%, p =0.001), fat mass (29.1±10.9 vs. 16.6±3.5 kg, p <0.001) and VAT index, a marker of central obesity (0.19 ± 0.09 vs. 0.11 ± 0.04 kg/m 2 , p<0.001) by DXA than the NWC although no significant difference was observed in lean mass (50.2±8.7 vs. 46.7±9.0 kg, p =0.3). Participants with ADPKD had lower M/I ratio than the NWC (0.424±0.171 vs. 0.586±0.205 (mg/kg lean/min) / (μIU/mL), p= 0.04) ( Table 1 ).

Kidney structure and cyst burden

HtTKV was much larger in the participants with ADPKD compared to NWC (731±370 vs. 159±31 mL/m, p<0.001). The difference between the groups remained statistically significant after adjusting for age, sex, and BMI (691 [CI: 536, 846] vs. 252 [CI: 24, 479] mL/m, p=0.005). We also quantified total liver volume in the participants with ADPKD, but these data were not available in the lean control group. Additionally, total kidney cyst volume and total kidney cyst number were 394 [185, 640] mL and 143 [95, 196] mL respectively in the participants with ADPKD ( Table 2 ).

Perfusion and oxidative metabolism by 11 C-acetate PET data

Using 11 C-acetate data, we documented substantially lower average cortical perfusion in participants with ADPKD vs. NWC (median [IQR]: 0.68 [0.47, 1.04] vs. 1.93 [1.80, 2.09] ml/min/g, p <0.001). Similarly, K 1 ( 11 C-acetate tracer uptake from blood to tissue) was also significantly lower in ADPKD than in NWC ( Table 2 ). Stratifying participants with ADPKD based on Mayo Imaging Classification, we found that participants with severe ADPKD demonstrated lower cortical perfusion and 11 C-acetate uptake compared to those with mild/moderate ADPKD ( Figure 1 ).

In voxel-wise models, average total kidney oxidative rates were 17–24% lower in ADPKD compared to NWC in the global ROI (median [IQR]: 0.13 [0.12, 0.15] vs. 0.17 [0.16, 0.19] min −1 , p< 0.001). The cyst-free ROI analyses showed similar differences between ADPKD vs. NWC in magnitude and significance ( Table 2 ). The ratio of oxidative rate to perfusion ( k 2 /F) was significantly higher in ADPKD vs. NWC ( Table 2 ). After adjusting for BMI, differences observed in the cortical perfusion, K 1 , k 2 (both global and cyst-free ROI), and k 2 /F remained significant ( p <0.05).

Figure 2 summarizes the proportions of voxels with average activity deviating more than 2 SD from the NWC mean for 11 C-acetate uptake ( K 1 ) and oxidative metabolism ( k 2 ) in ADPKD (mild/moderate and severe) and NWC. Average metabolically active kidney tissue in both K 1 and k 2 voxels were visibly much lower in participants with ADPKD compared to the NWC indicating illustrating an overall impairment in kidney metabolic activity in participants with ADPKD.

Associations among DXA, hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, PET, and MRI markers

VAT index and body fat percentage related inversely with F, K 1 and positively with ratio of oxidative rate to perfusion ( k 2 /F), while lean mass showed opposite associations ( Figure 3 ). Body fat percentage, lean mass percentage, VAT index and M/I ratio were strongly correlated with HtTKV, but only body fat percentage and VAT index remained significant after adjusting for age and sex ( Figure 3 ). M/I ratio also correlated with k 2 and the association remained significant after adjusting for age and sex ( Figure 3 ).

Associations among kidney oxidative rate, tracer uptake and perfusion and HtTKV

Kidney oxidative rate, tracer uptake and perfusion correlated inversely with HtTKV and remained significant after adjusting for sex, age, and BMI in linear regression ( Figure 3 ). The ratio between kidney oxidative rate and perfusion correlated directly with HtTKV ( Figure 3 ). Moreover, tracer uptake and perfusion correlated strongly and inversely with total cyst volume, total cyst numbers, and cyst parenchyma surface area ( Table S2 ), however not significantly with kidney oxidative rate. The ratio between kidney oxidative rate and perfusion correlated directly with total cyst volume, total cyst numbers, and cyst parenchyma surface area ( Table S2 ).

## Discussion

Discussion

We document that adults with ADPKD and preserved kidney function across a wide range of cyst and kidney volumes exhibit impaired renal perfusion and kidney oxidative metabolism by 11 C-acetate PET/CT as well as impaired whole-body insulin sensitivity by hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamps. Additionally, kidney oxidative metabolism, tracer uptake and renal perfusion associated inversely with HtTKV by MRI, and tracer uptake and renal perfusion associated inversely with cyst volume and cyst number. The ratio of kidney oxidative rate to perfusion, representing the balance between oxygen demand vs. oxygen delivery, associated strongly with HtTKV, cyst volume, and cyst number. Finally, the associations we observed among VAT index, M/I, kidney oxidative metabolism and cyst burden, suggest that adiposity and insulin sensitivity may affect renal metabolic efficiency and cyst burden in ADPKD.

ADPKD affects about 600,000 Americans and over 12 million people worldwide. 1 – 3 It is characterized by growth of kidney cysts which eventually destroy the normal parenchyma, leading to kidney failure in most afflicted patients. We contend that the current unidimensional imaging paradigm of ADPKD (e.g., assessing TKV and cyst burden) is insufficient to reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of the underlying metabolic disturbance that characterizes ADPKD.

The kidneys are highly metabolically active and susceptible to oxidative stress and hypoxia. 6 , 7 , 9 The hypoxia risk is thought to stem from the kidneys’ high energy expenditure. 28 ADPKD is characterized by processes that exacerbate the tubular workload and energy expenditure, such as increased filtered Na + load from single-nephron hyperfiltration, 29 , 30 and stimulated Na + absorption from hyperinsulinism, 13 , 31 – 33 renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS) activity and arginine vasopressin (AVP) activation. 34 – 38 In parallel, emerging animal data support that the kidneys are unable to compensate for the increased adenosine triphosphate (ATP) consumption due to ADPKD-related metabolic reprogramming including impaired TCA cycle and OXPHOS. 4 – 8 Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the metabolic reprogramming, including polycystins’ role in regulating mitochondria morphology and function. 8 , 11 , 39 – 44 Further, insulin resistance, which has been reported in people with overweight/obesity and ADPKD, may also impair OXPHOS. 11 , 13 , 39 – 44 Indeed, in the HALT Progression of Polycystic Kidney Disease (HALT-PKD) Study A and the TEMPO 3:4 trial, 45 we identified that overweight and obese participants with early ADPKD had a greater annual change in TKV compared to normal weight. 15 The net effect of the metabolic reprogramming is ultimately kidney hypoxia, which has been implicated in the pathogenesis of kidney cysts in ADPKD. 8 , 13 , 46

Kidney-specific oxidative metabolism can be measured using PET with 11 C-acetate to determine 11 C-acetate turnover in the TCA cycle, and has been validated against rigorous renal artery and vein catheterization techniques, 25 although only a handful of clinical studies have used 11 C-acetate PET outside of oncology and none in ADPKD. 25 , 26 , 47 In the present study, we developed a voxel-wise 11 C-acetate PET model to enable us to detect early regional changes in kidney perfusion and oxidative metabolism in ADPKD. Unlike MRI sequences used to quantify kidney oxygen availability, such as blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) MRI, PET applications are less constrained by abnormal tissue architecture and heterogenous cyst burden and are very sensitive to changes in oxidative metabolism (measures in the picomolar range). 48 , 49 Therefore, we contend that this model is ideally suited to quantify metabolically active kidney volume in people with ADPKD. Attenuated renal perfusion occurs early in ADPKD, precedes the decline in GFR, and predicts cyst enlargement. 50 Although we measured both oxidative metabolism and perfusion in our study, it should be emphasized that perfusion does not necessarily act as a confounding variable on the oxidative rate. Instead, both contribute to the development of hypoxia, particularly if the decrease in the supply of oxygen (perfusion) is more rapid than the oxidative rate.

There are several strengths and limitations of the current pilot study worth discussing. This study notably applied 11 C-acetate to assess kidney oxidative metabolism, a novel approach that contributes significantly to our understanding of perturbed metabolism in ADPKD. Furthermore, the voxel-wise pharmacokinetic model enabled us to measure the clearance of 11 C-acetate, i.e., the acetate turnover in the TCA cycle, in each kidney voxel, which allowed us to account for regional differences in kidney oxidative metabolism. Our PET analyses also provided estimates of renal perfusion as well as tracer uptake, a product of tissue perfusion and integrity. Although, kidney uptake of 11 C-acetate is dependent on tracer delivery and first-pass extraction, which will vary with perfusion, it is important to point out F (derived from K 1 ) and k 2 represent distinct physiological processes and are not expected to be collinear. F or K 1 is predominantly influenced by factors external to the cell (such as perfusion), while k 2 is more directly related to the internal cellular metabolic processes. This means that high perfusion (F) or high tracer uptake ( K 1 ) does not necessarily translate into high metabolic rate ( k 2 ), and vice versa. Changes in one do not inherently cause changes in the other, which underscores their independence. The kidney MRI analyses provided comprehensive quantification of kidney and liver volume, cyst volume and cyst number. To avoid bias, the PET and MRI analyses were conducted in a blinded fashion, carrying only the label of the study with anonymized participant information. Finally, we employed the gold-standard method, hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamps, to assess insulin sensitivity, as well as DXA to quantify lean, fat, and visceral mass.

However, our study is constrained by important factors. We acknowledge that the sample size of our study may be considered relatively small. This size constraint primarily results from the high costs associated with 11 C-acetate PET scanning. Despite this, it is important to point out that our study represents the most extensive application of 11 C acetate PET to measure kidney oxidative metabolism to date. Previously conducted studies in this field have generally been more limited, typically including only 10–18 participants. 25 , 26 , 47 Our ability to fully comprehend and interpret our findings is somewhat restricted by the scarcity of data on inter and intra-subject variability in this field. Despite these gaps, the limited available data on inter and intra-subject variability offer some reassurance. The findings from Shreve et al., for instance, suggest a relatively small degree of variability in repeat values for K 1 and k 2 parameters, with ranges of 2–12% for K 1 and 0–16% for k 2 . 47 This suggests that the 11 C acetate PET imaging technique could potentially have reliable reproducibility, at least within these ranges. However, further research is needed to affirm these findings and better understand the potential variability in ADPKD as well as larger and more diverse populations. PET/CT exposes participants to radiation (2.67 mSv), which might limit its broad application or acceptance, although it is still a fraction of the annual occupational exposure limit for radiation workers (50 mSv), and low compared to other radioactive tracers.
