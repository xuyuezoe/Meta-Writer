# Effective coverage of curative child health services in Ethiopia: analysis of the Demographic and Health Survey and Service Provision Assessment survey.

## Abstract

Objectives: Despite a remarkable decline, childhood morbidity and mortality in Ethiopia remain high and inequitable. Thus, we estimated the effective coverage of curative child health services in Ethiopia.

Design: We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of data from the 2016 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) and the 2014 Ethiopia Service Provision Assessment Plus (SPA+) survey.

Setting: Nationally representative household and facility surveys.

Participants and outcomes: We included a sample of 2096 children under 5 years old (from DHS) who had symptoms of one or more common childhood illnesses (diarrhoea, fever and acute respiratory infection) and estimated the percentage of sick children who were taken to a health facility (crude coverage). To construct a quality index of child health services, we used the SPA+ survey, which was conducted in 1076 health facilities and included observations of care for 1980 sick children and surveys of 1908 mothers/caregivers and 5328 health providers. We applied the Donabedian quality of care framework to identify 58 quality parameters (structure, 31; process, 16; and outcome, 11) and used the weighted additive method to estimate the overall quality of care index. Finally, we multiplied the crude coverage by the quality of care index to estimate the effective coverage of curative child health services, nationally and by region.

Results: Among the 2096 sick children, only 38.4% (95% CI: 36.5 to 40.4) of them were taken to a health facility. The overall quality of care was 54.4%, weighted from structure (30.0%), process (9.2%) and outcome (15.2%). The effective coverage of curative child health services was estimated at 20.9% (95%CI: 19.9 to 22.0) nationally, ranging from 16.9% in Somali to 34.6% in Dire Dawa regions.

Conclusions: System-wide interventions are required to address both demand-side and supply-side bottlenecks in the provision of child health services if child health-related targets are to be achieved in Ethiopia.

## Introduction

Introduction

In Ethiopia, child mortality has been remarkably reduced between 2000 and 2019. 1–3 However, under-5 mortality remained high in 2019 at 55 deaths per 1000 live births and was unevenly distributed, ranging from the highest in Afar region (125 deaths per 1000 live births) to the lowest in Addis Ababa City Administration (39 deaths per 1000 live births). 2 4 Nearly 55% and 80% of deaths among children under 5 years old in Ethiopia occur in the neonatal period and are due to preventable causes, respectively. 1 The majority of these deaths are due to pneumonia, acute respiratory infections (ARIs), diarrhoea and malaria and could be prevented by proven interventions such as childhood immunisation, access to safe water and sanitation, nutrition and healthcare. 5 6 Nevertheless, low coverage of these life-saving interventions remains a critical bottleneck to significantly reducing the high morbidity and mortality of children under 5 years old in Ethiopia. 7

To improve the survival and well-being of children under 5 years old and meet the Millennium Development Goal (MDG) 4 target of reducing child mortality by two-thirds between 1990 and 2015, Ethiopia adopted the Integrated Community Case Management of Childhood Illness (iCCM) as a key strategy in 2010. This strategy has been implemented by Health Extension Workers (HEWs) in primary healthcare (PHC) settings, mainly health posts. 8–10 The strategy focuses on improving health workers’ skills through training and supportive supervision, the availability of equipment and supplies, and referral systems at facility, community, and household levels. As a result, the proportion of sick children for whom care is sought has increased over the past decade from <10% in 2000 to 35% in 2016. 4 10–15 Although the use and coverage of child health services have improved in recent decades, a significant proportion of (sick) children still do not receive (curative) child health services. 16–18 As a result, child morbidity and mortality remain high, and the MDG 4 target of reducing under-5 mortality rate by two-thirds between 1990 and 2015 has not been met. This is partly due to bottlenecks in the implementation of child health strategies and services, including the iCCM strategy, notably shortages of trained staff, stockouts of essential drugs and supplies, and irregular supportive supervision. 19 Additionally, the lower-than-expected impact of iCCM on under-5 mortality was due to a narrow focus on the supply-side aspects of the strategy, with limited attention to key demand-side factors such as the financial affordability of healthcare. 20 In Ethiopia, most kebeles (the lowest administrative units) are geographically large and have several sparse villages with difficult topography, making access to healthcare difficult. 13 20 Additionally, delays in making decisions to use services, low trust in HEW curative care, perceived low-quality of care at primary health facilities, high indirect costs of seeking care, perceived low severity of childhood illness and low women’s empowerment were key barriers to iCCM uptake. 20–22

To help countries improve the quality of curative child health services, WHO has set guiding standards for the care of small and sick newborns and children, which can be used to address gaps in access to life-saving care in the context of universal health coverage (UHC). One of these approaches is effective coverage—‘the proportion of a population in need of a service that had a positive health outcome from the service’—which can be used to track health system performance taking into account the need for, use and quality of healthcare services. 23 24 Improving the effective coverage of health services would increase the health benefits of interventions at different levels, from individual benefits to national impact. This improvement would contribute to the achievement of UHC, focusing on both quality and coverage. 24 25 Thus, estimating the effective coverage of curative child health services in Ethiopia would help inform interventions to improve the quality and coverage of child health services and track the country’s health system performance towards the child health targets of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and UHC. It also helps to understand disparities across regions in Ethiopia in order to design targeted interventions. Despite growing reports of estimating effective coverage of essential services such as family planning, HIV testing and counselling, and health system outcomes in Ethiopia, 26–28 evidence on effective coverage of child health services is meagre. In this study, we aim to fill the evidence gap by estimating the effective coverage of curative child health services for common childhood illnesses (CCIs), which include diarrhoea, fever and ARI.

## Methods

Methods

Data sources and context

We conducted a secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from the most recent Ethiopian Standard Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) 2016 and the Service Provision Assessment Plus (SPA+) survey 2014. DHS is a nationally representative household survey focusing on indicators of reproductive, maternal and child health, nutrition and self-reported health behaviours among adults. 4 The SPA+ survey is a health facility survey developed by the DHS Program which collects information on the availability, readiness and quality of services including maternal, child and reproductive health services within a country’s health system. 29 The first SPA+ survey in Ethiopia was conducted in 2014 and involved health facility inventory, survey of healthcare providers, observation of care provision and exit survey of service users. 29

Ethiopia had a population of >120 million people in 2022, of which 16% were children under 5 years old, 30 and the majority (79.1%) were rural residents. At the time of the two surveys, Ethiopia had nine administrative regions and two city administrations. These regions and city administrations are further subdivided into woredas of approximately 100 000 people, which are further subdivided into kebeles. The city administrations are mostly urbanised and have better infrastructure and access to healthcare, education and transport than the regions. The Ethiopian health service delivery system is structured into three tiers: primary (health posts, health centres and primary hospitals), secondary (general hospitals) and tertiary (specialised hospitals) levels of care. 31

Sample size and sampling in DHS

The 2016 Ethiopian DHS used a two-stage stratified sampling design, using the 2007 Ethiopian Population and Housing Census as a sampling frame. The census used a complete list of 84 915 enumeration areas (EAs), which included the location, type of residence (urban/rural) and estimated number of households. First, samples of EAs were selected independently from each stratum, and then households were selected from the selected EAs using a systematic random sampling technique.

All women aged 15–49 years who were members of the selected households were eligible for the survey. In this study, we analysed responses from women who had under 5-year-old children who had symptoms of one or more of the three CCIs (diarrhoea, fever or ARI) in the 2 weeks prior to the survey. Accordingly, we included a total sample of 2096 sick children in the analysis ( figure 1 ).

Sample size and sampling in SPA+

The survey covered 1076 health facilities providing child health services (all hospitals and random samples of health centres, health posts and clinics). Data from all health facilities surveyed were included in our analysis. Additionally, data from a survey of randomly selected 5328 child healthcare providers, observations of care provided to systematically selected 1980 sick children and exit surveys of 1908 mothers/caregivers were included in our analysis.

Definition and measurement of variables

We described the sociodemographic characteristics (sex and age) of children under 5 years old with CCIs and their household place of residence (urban/rural) and wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer or richest) and their mother’s educational status (no education, primary, secondary or higher), employment status (not employed or employed) and marital status (single, married, divorced or widowed) at the time of the survey.

We recorded the type of health facility where care for CCI was sought in hospitals, health centres, health posts, and clinics and others. The clinics and other category included private clinics, private pharmacies, private doctors, shop/drug vendors, and other non-governmental health facilities. In SPA+, health facilities were coded as hospitals, health centres, health posts and clinics. In the Ethiopian context, outreach services are organised by health facilities, particularly by primary hospitals, health centres and health posts, for the catchment population especially. Therefore, their approach to service delivery was assessed and coded as static (onsite) and outreach (offsite).

We defined a child as having a CCI if the child’s mother reported symptom(s) suggesting at least one of the three CCIs (diarrhoea, fever or ARI) in the 2 weeks prior to the survey. In line with DHS, we defined ARI as having cough, short and rapid breathing (stridor) and/or chest-related breathing difficulty. We defined diarrhoea as the presence of frequent loose or watery stools. Fever was defined as an abnormally high body temperature, usually accompanied by shivering, headache and restlessness. 4

We measured the crude coverage of curative child health services as the percentage of sick children who were taken to a health facility. Thus, if a child with CCI was taken to a health facility for the symptoms of at least one of the illnesses (diarrhoea, fever and/or ARI), we considered the child to have ‘received care’; otherwise, ‘not received care’. We conducted weighted data analysis to account for the complex and multistage cluster sampling design of DHS. Accordingly, we applied women’s individual sample weights and made adjustments for clustering and stratification during the analysis.

We measured the quality of curative child health services using data from the SPA+ 2014 based on the Donabedian framework for quality measurement, including structure, process and outcome components. A total of 58 parameters were selected from the SPA dataset using the WHO’s standards for improving the quality of care for children and young adolescents in health facilities, the WHO reference manual on service availability and readiness assessment and the national Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) guideline as guides to measure the structure, process and outcome components of the quality of curative child health services ( online supplemental table 1 ). The structure component included 31 ‘yes or no’ parameters which were used to measure the availability and readiness of curative child health services; these parameters covered equipment and supplies, standard precautions, service provision, staff availability and training, communication, power sources, waiting area, and medicines and commodities. The process component included 16 ‘yes or no’ parameters that measured the healthcare providers’ adherence to standards of care and basic management of sick children. The outcome component included 11 ‘yes or no’ parameters which were used to measure mothers’/caregivers’ satisfaction with the services they received.

A weighted additive method, adapted from a DHS Methodological Report, was used to calculate the overall quality index of curative child health services. 32 The weighted additive method accounts for the relative contributions of potentially unequally weighted parameters, that is, for the three quality components (structure, process and outcome). We assigned weights to the components based on the number of parameters and adjusted these for the variation in the number of parameters within each domain. Accordingly, the weight assigned to structure-related parameters was 53.4% (31/58×100), whereas it was 27.6% (16/58×100) for process-related parameters and 19.0% (11/58×100) for outcome-related parameters. Characteristics of children under 5 years old with CCI

Of the 2096 children with at least one CCI included in this analysis, the majority (66.1%) had only one illness, while the remaining children had two (25.8%) or all three (8.1%) illnesses. More than two-thirds (43.2%) of the children were from Oromia region, followed by Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (21.0%) and Amhara (20.1%) regions, and the fewest (0.2%) were from Harari region ( table 1 ). Nearly 89% of children were from rural areas, and 50.6% were less than 24 months old.

Among children with any CCI who were taken to a health facility, 54.3% were taken to health centres, followed by clinics or other health facilities (25.7%), to health posts (13.9%) and to hospitals (6.1%) ( online supplemental table 2 ).

Description of facilities surveyed in SPA+

Among the initial health facilities sampled in the SPA+ survey (1327), data were collected from 1165 health facilities. Of these, 1076 health facilities providing curative care for children were included in this analysis; 206 were hospitals, 290 were health centres, 280 were health posts and 300 were clinics. The median availability of curative care services for childhood illnesses was 26 days per month (range: 0–30) for health facility-based (onsite) services and none for outreach services.

Quality of facility-based curative child health services

The quality score for the structural component of curative child health services was 30.0%, ranging from 25.0% in Somali region to 35.3% in Tigray region and from 26.1% in clinics to 37.3% in hospitals. The quality score for the process component of curative child health services was 9.2%, with the lowest in Harari (7.2%) and the highest in Tigray (11.4%). Similarly, the quality score for the outcome component of curative child health services was 15.2%, ranging from 12.6% in Harari region to 16.7% in Somali region and from 14.6% in hospitals to 15.9% in health posts. Accordingly, the overall quality score for curative child health services was 54.4% (the sum of the quality scores for the structure (30.0%), process (9.2%) and outcome (15.2%) components of care). The lowest overall quality score (49.1%) was in Somali region, followed by Gambela (49.8%) and Afar (50.3%) regions, and the highest was in Tigray region (62.6%) ( online supplemental table 3 ). By facility type, the lowest overall quality score was in clinics (50.0%), and the highest was in hospitals (61.1%) ( table 4 ).

## Discussion

Discussion

In this study, we combined data from the Ethiopia DHS and SPA+ surveys to estimate the effective coverage of curative child health services for children under 5 years old with CCIs. We found a very low effective coverage of 1 in 5 children (20.9%). This shows that despite efforts to improve the quality of curative child health services in the country, there remains a large gap in both crude and effective coverage of curative child health services. A low proportion of sick children with CCI sought healthcare during the course of their illness. We observed variation in the overall quality of curative child health services across regions and facility types, which may have contributed to the variation in effective coverages by these attributes. Our findings showed that there were differences in effective coverage across regions and residential settings, with urban settings generally having higher (yet still suboptimal) effective coverage than rural settings. In general, city administrations had the highest effective coverage of health services for CCI. This may be because they are predominantly urban and have higher quality of curative care and higher crude coverage than the national average, although poor quality services are common in low-resource urban settings.

We found that only 38.4% of sick children with CCIs were reported to have been taken to a health facility for care during their illness (crude coverage), which is consistent with findings from a study in Bangladesh, which reported crude coverage of 36% for diarrhoea and 37% for cough/fever. 35 In our study, children with a single CCI were less likely to have been taken to a health facility than those with two or three CCIs. This may be due to the low perceived severity of just a single CCI among mothers and families, 36 which can lead to complications of minor illnesses, which in turn may result in unforeseen healthcare costs for the family and health systems. 37

As reported in the secondary analysis of the SPA+ survey in this study, only 54.4% of sick children who visited health facilities received quality care, suggesting missed opportunities to increase the effective coverage of curative child health services. Suboptimal quality of care at health facilities could lead to communities bypassing of health facilities in search of good care, avoidance of care seeking and delayed care seeking (for minor illnesses), resulting in higher costs at the individual and health system levels. One-third (33.3%) of sick children did not receive all the recommended standard packages of care as per the national IMCI guideline; children who were taken to hospital had a better chance of receiving these packages of care during their illness. A study conducted in four countries (Namibia, Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda) also reported that adherence to IMCI guidelines was higher in hospitals than in health centres, which may be due to better availability of essential resources in hospitals and more advanced clinical knowledge and skills among hospital-based health providers. 38 Although hospitals had higher quality scores in our study, they are usually overcrowded with very sick patients. In response, mothers/caregivers of sick children prefer to go to health centres, health posts or clinics instead. 39 40 Therefore, addressing gaps in quality of care at these primary-level facilities could have a significant impact on improving child health outcomes.

In our study, the quality score for the structural components of curative child health services in clinics (mainly private) was low, indicating limited service availability and readiness in these settings. Therefore, given the significant proportion of children with CCIs taken to clinics (25.7%), strengthening the regulation of the clinics based on a sound public-private partnership approach is crucial to improve effective coverage, especially in urban settings. Although our study showed that most of the visits for care were to health centres and health posts, the quality of a structural component of curative child health services in these facilities was low in all regions, hampering effective coverage of curative child health services even with increased service use.

Curative child health services are crucial interventions to reduce the morbidity and mortality among children under 5 years old in low-resource settings such as Ethiopia, where 1 in every 18 children dies before the age of 5 years. 2 However, a significant proportion of sick children in these settings do not receive good quality care in health facilities. 25

There are reports of gradual gains in child health in Ethiopia, including reduced morbidity and mortality among children under 5 years old, due to the HEP and the embedded iCCM and community-based newborn care strategies. 41–43 However, challenges such as weak infrastructure and poor supply chain systems and geographical and financial barriers remain key bottlenecks to the implementation of these strategies. 44–46 The fact that the process and outcome quality components of curative child health services were highest in health centres and health posts, respectively, could be used as an opportunity to focus on strengthening services at the PHC level, as the lion’s share (78.7%) of the population lives in rural settings. This could be achieved by re-examining approaches to foster community engagement and empowerment, further decentralisation of curative child health services, intersectoral collaboration and strong leadership. 47 48 Although the Ethiopian PHC model provides a package of basic preventive and curative child health services to rural households, 49 our study found that the coverage of child health services is low and uneven. We also found that curative child health services may not be available in rural facilities, even though they are mandated to provide child health to rural communities. This may be explained by limited resources, weak support and supervision, low motivation among health providers and poor working environment in these facilities. 12 50–52

Given the progress to date and the bottlenecks in the provision of child health services in Ethiopia, the child health-related targets of the SDGs may not be achievable in the country unless significant corrective action is taken. 53 Therefore, it is crucial to develop and strengthen strategies to optimise close-to-community and high-quality healthcare services for children. This could be achieved by strengthening community-based PHC services using existing platforms/initiatives such as the health extension programme, iCCM, community-based newborn care and IMCI. 21 54 55

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study is one of the first to estimate the effective coverage of curative child health services in Ethiopia using a weighted additive method based on data from DHS and SPA+. The weighted additive method used to determine quality indices was key to minimising overestimation or underestimation of quality scores due to the number of quality parameters in each component (structure, process and outcome).

However, the study has some limitations. ## Conclusions

Conclusions

Nearly four-fifths of children with CCI(s) in Ethiopia did not receive quality care during their course of illness. Both health-seeking behaviour and quality of care were suboptimal, contributing to the low effective coverage. Therefore, efforts to improve the effective coverage of curative child health services should focus on improving health-seeking behaviour and access to high-quality curative child health services. Health system decision-makers should focus on strengthening the availability and readiness of curative child health services, particularly in primary care settings where most curative child healthcare is sought, especially in rural areas. Overall, system-wide interventions are needed to address both demand-side and supply-side bottlenecks in the provision of child health services if child health-related targets are to be achieved in Ethiopia. We also recommend that future research should prioritise linking supply-side data from actual health facilities where children receive care with household-based surveys to provide a more robust estimate of the effective coverage of curative child health services.
