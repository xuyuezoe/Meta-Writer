# Sex Differences in Clinical Features and Survival Outcomes of Esophageal Cancer: A Comparative Study in the Korean Population.

## Abstract

Purpose: Esophageal cancer is a predominantly male disease. However, the sex differences associated with esophageal cancer have not been thoroughly investigated. This study aimed to evaluate the differences between esophageal cancer in males and females in the Korean population.

Materials and methods: We assessed patients diagnosed with esophageal cancer between 2005 and 2015 at a tertiary referral center. The clinical features of patients, histopathologic characteristics of tumors, and treatment and survival outcomes were compared between male and female patients.

Results: We enrolled 2,068 patients, comprising 1,924 (93.0%) males and 144 (7.0%) females. The median age at diagnosis was younger for females than males (65 vs. 63 years, p=0.004). Squamous cell carcinoma was the predominant pathological type (99.0% in males and 93.1% in females); however, the proportion of adenocarcinoma cases was higher in females than males (0.8% vs. 5.6%, p vs. 53.5%, p=0.016) and recurrence-free survival rates (74.0% vs. 84.3%, p=0.036) were higher in females than in males.

Conclusions: We found significant sex differences in esophageal cancer among the Korean population, with female patients demonstrating distinct clinical characteristics and more favorable survival outcomes compared to male patients. These findings underscore the importance of considering sex-specific factors in the management and prognosis of esophageal cancer.

## INTRODUCTION

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal cancer is a significant global health concern, ranking as the eleventh most common cancer and the seventh leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [ 1 ]. This malignancy presents two major histopathological types: squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma, with SCC being more prevalent in Asian countries, including Korea [ 2 ]. In contrast, esophageal adenocarcinoma predominates in Western countries, driven by risk factors such as obesity and gastroesophageal reflux disease [ 3 ]. Despite these geographical differences, esophageal cancer disproportionately affects males, with a global male-to-female ratio of approximately 3.3:1 [ 1 ]. This sex disparity in esophageal cancer incidence has been attributed primarily to lifestyle factors, including smoking and alcohol consumption, both of which are strongly linked to SCC. However, recent studies have suggested that these factors alone do not fully explain the sex-based differences observed in clinical outcomes [ 4 5 ].

Sex disparities in cancer incidence, progression, and outcomes are increasingly recognized as crucial areas of study. These disparities are influenced by a complex interplay of genetic, hormonal, environmental, and behavioral factors [ 6 7 8 ]. For esophageal cancer, understanding these sex differences is vital for optimizing prevention strategies, tailoring treatment protocols, and improving prognostic accuracy. Previous studies have highlighted several sex-related differences in esophageal cancer [ 7 9 ]. For instance, females generally present with the disease at an older age compared to males [ 10 ]. Additionally, hormonal influences, particularly estrogen, are hypothesized to play a protective role in females, potentially contributing to differences in tumor biology and response to treatment [ 11 ]. Despite these insights, comprehensive data specific to the Korean population with SCC predominance remain sparse, necessitating further research to elucidate these differences.

This study aimed to evaluate the sex differences in esophageal cancer among the Korean population by analyzing clinical features, histopathologic characteristics, and survival outcomes between male and female patients. By leveraging a robust dataset from a tertiary referral center, this research seeks to provide a detailed understanding of how sex influences various aspects of esophageal cancer.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Data collection

We retrospectively analyzed patients with histologically confirmed primary esophageal cancer between January 2005 and December 2015 at a tertiary care hospital. Clinical characteristics such as age, sex, height, weight, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at initial diagnosis, comorbidities, alcohol and smoking history, and family history were investigated by reviewing medical records. Patients were categorized according to body mass index (BMI) based on the modified Asian-Pacific World Health Organization guidelines: underweight ( 30 cm). The clinical tumor stage was classified using the Seventh edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM (tumor, node, metastasis) staging system [ 13 ].

Patients underwent endoscopic resection, upfront surgery, induction therapy followed by surgery, surgery followed by adjuvant therapy, definitive chemoradiotherapy, palliative chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, or supportive care according to their tumor stage and the patient’s consent. Complete response was defined as achieving curative resection through endoscopic treatment or showing no clinical, endoscopic, or imaging evidence of residual tumor after the other treatment was completed. Recurrence was defined as local recurrence, surgical wound recurrence, or distant metastasis.

The follow-up period was defined as the interval from the diagnosis to the last outpatient clinic visit. The survival status of patients was obtained from medical records and National Health Insurance Service records (South Korea). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (approval no. 2011-0793).

2. Statistical analysis

Descriptive variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Differences in patients and tumor characteristics between males and females were appropriately compared using independent t-tests and chi-square or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Cox proportional hazard models were employed for both univariate and multivarable analyses to identify significant factors affecting overall survival (OS). Covariates that were significant in the univariate Cox analysis were included in the multivariate assessment. The OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Patients who were alive at the last follow-up and had not experienced recurrence were censored for OS and RFS, respectively. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (Released 2020; IBM Corp.).

## RESULTS

RESULTS

1. Clinical characteristics of patients

A total of 2,068 patients were enrolled in this study. The median age at diagnosis was 65 years (IQR, 58–71 years), and 93.0% were male, with a male-to-female ratio of 13.4:1. Table 1 compares the clinical characteristics between male and female patients. The median age at diagnosis was younger in females compared to males (65 vs. 63 years, p=0.004). Males smoked more (86.5% vs. 22.2%, p<0.001) and consumed more alcohol (89.9% vs. 26.4%, p<0.001) than females. Most patients had ECOG performance statuses of 0 and 1, but a higher proportion of females had ECOG 2 (7.2% vs. 11.9%) and ECOG 3 (1.4% vs. 4.2%) statuses compared to males (p=0.005). BMI did not significantly differ between the two groups.

2. Sex and clinicopathologic characteristics of tumors

Clinical and histopathologic characteristics of tumors are summarized in Table 2 . SCC was the major pathologic type (99.0% in males and 93.1% in females); however, the proportion of adenocarcinoma cases was higher in females than in males (0.8% vs. 5.6%, p<0.001). The male-to-female ratio of the 24 adenocarcinoma patients was 2:1, with a male predominance. Males tended to have tumors in the lower esophagus (46.0%), while females had a predominance of tumors in the middle esophagus (59.0%) (p<0.001). Stage 1 tumors were more common in males than females (32.6% vs. 23.8%), while stage 4 tumors were more frequently observed in females than males (8.6% vs. 11.2%, p=0.001). Poorly differentiated carcinoma was relatively more frequent in females than in males (11.2% vs. 15.3%, p=0.011).

3. Sex and survival rates for esophageal Cancer

The 5-year OS rate for all patients was 44.9%, while the median 5-year RFS rate was 71.7%. The 5-year OS rates by stage were as follows: carcinoma in situ , 93.6%; stage 1, 72.3%; stage 2, 45.4%; stage 3, 22.4%; and stage 4, 6.1% ( Supplement Fig. 1A ). The 5-year RFS rates by stage were: carcinoma in situ , 85.6%; stage 1, 85.6%; stage 2, 70.3%; stage 3, 56.9%; and stage 4, 40.5% ( Supplement Fig. 1B ).

The survival analysis revealed that female patients had better OS than male patients (44.8% vs. 53.5%, p=0.016) ( Fig. 1A ). Additionally, the RFS rate was also higher in female patients compared to male patients (74.0% vs. 84.3%, p=0.036) ( Fig. 1B ). An analysis of OS by tumor stage indicated that female patients exhibited a trend towards better OS compared to male patients across all stages ( Fig. 2 ). Notably, in stage 3, female patients demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in OS compared to male patients (21.0% vs. 41.7%, p=0.009). Similarly, RFS also showed a trend towards better outcomes for female patients across all stages, with a statistically significant improvement in stage 2 (76.4% vs. 88.1%, p=0.021) ( Fig. 3 ).

Additionally, 1,392 (67.3%) patients achieved complete remission after treatment (male vs. female; 67.2% vs. 68.1%, p=0.855). The cancer recurrence rate in these patients was significantly lower in female patients than in male patients (25.1% vs. 15.3%, p=0.037).

4. Prognostic factors for esophageal cancer

Univariate analysis conducted to evaluate factors associated with OS showed that age, sex, diabetes mellitus (DM), smoking, BMI, and tumor stage were related to OS ( Table 3 ). In the multivariate analysis, being female (hazard ratio [HR], 0.685; 95% confidence interval [95% CI], 0.548–0.857; p=0.001) and high BMI (≥25 kg/m 2 , HR, 0.432; 95% CI, 0.355–0.526; p<0.001) were associated with a significantly reduced risk of mortality. Conversely, age (HR, 1.027; 95% CI, 1.021–1.034; p<0.001), the presence of DM (HR, 1.235; 95% CI, 1.076–1.416; p=0.003), and advanced tumor stage (stage 4, HR, 12.684; 95% CI, 7.451–21.591; p<0.001) were related to a significantly increased risk of mortality.

5. Subgroup analysis of esophageal SCC

The subgroup analysis comparing males and females with esophageal SCC is summarized in Supplement Table 1 . In this cohort, the median age at diagnosis was younger in females compared to males (65 vs. 63 years, p=0.032). Males exhibited higher rates of smoking (86.5% vs. 21.6%, p<0.001) and alcohol consumption (90.0% vs. 26.1%, p<0.001) than females. While most patients had ECOG performance statuses of 0 and 1, a higher proportion of females had ECOG 2 (7.2% vs. 12.0%) and ECOG 3 (1.3% vs. 4.5%) statuses compared to males (p=0.006). There were no significant differences in BMI between the two groups.

Tumor location differed significantly, with males tending to have tumors located in the lower esophagus (46.3%), and more females had tumors in the middle esophagus (61.9%) (p<0.001). Stage 1 tumors (32.7% vs. 24.8%) were more common in males than females, while stage 2 tumors (30.9% vs. 33.1%) were more frequently observed in females compared to males (p<0.001). Tumor differentiation did not significantly differ between the two groups (p=0.758). Survival analysis revealed that female patients had better OS (p=0.017) and RFS (p=0.011) compared to male patients ( Supplement Fig. 2 ).

## DISCUSSION

DISCUSSION

Esophageal cancer is one of the male-predominant cancers. However, sex-specific differences in clinical characteristics, tumor biology, and outcomes have been inadequately investigated, particularly for SCC. Therefore, the analysis of sex differences in our study, with a predominance of SCC cases, is considered highly significant. Our study revealed significant differences in clinical characteristics and survival outcomes between male and female patients. Female patients had a lower smoking and alcohol history, a higher proportion of adenocarcinoma and middle esophagus tumors, and showed better OS and RFS compared to male patients. The subgroup analysis limited to patients with SCC also demonstrated similar results. Multivariate analysis revealed that female sex, high BMI, and early tumor stage were associated with better survival outcomes, highlighting the need for sex-specific considerations in managing esophageal cancer.

Globally, there were approximately 604,100 new cases of esophageal cancer in 2020, with esophageal SCC accounting for 85% of these cases [ 14 ]. SCC is the most common histological type worldwide, predominantly concentrated in Asia. In contrast, esophageal adenocarcinoma is the most prevalent histological type in the United States and other Western countries. Of all esophageal cancers, including adenocarcinoma and SCC, 70% occurred in males [ 2 15 ]. In Korea, esophageal cancer ranks as the sixteenth most common cancer and the tenth leading cause of cancer-related deaths in 2021. The male-to-female ratio was 9.6:1. SCC was the predominant pathology, comprising 92.1% of cases, followed by adenocarcinoma at 2.9% [ 16 ]. Similarly, in our study, 98.5% of esophageal cancer cases were SCC, with a male-to-female ratio of 13.4:1. The proportion of adenocarcinoma cases for each sex was higher in females (0.8% in males, 5.6% in females), although the overall male-to-female ratio was 2:1, indicating a higher prevalence in males similar to SCC.

Non-genetic risk factors for esophageal cancer include gastroesophageal reflux disease, smoking, and obesity as major risk factors for esophageal adenocarcinoma, while alcohol and smoking are primary risk factors for esophageal SCC, accounting for 60% of esophageal cancers in Western countries and 90.2% in Korea [ 4 17 ]. In our study, males had a significantly higher alcohol consumption and smoking history compared to females. However, it is difficult to explain the sex difference solely by smoking, a common risk factor for both adenocarcinoma and SCC. Previous research indicates that even after adjusting for smoking and age, esophageal cancer still shows a male predominance [ 8 ]. Therefore, in addition to lifestyle factors, it is essential to consider factors related to sex, such as reproductive hormones, central obesity, and premenopausal iron status [ 18 ].

Previous studies have shown that females tend to be diagnosed with esophageal cancer at an older age compared to males [ 19 ]. This finding supports the hypothesis that estrogen may inhibit esophageal carcinogenesis and thus provide protective effects for premenopausal females [ 20 21 ]. In our study, males were diagnosed at an older age than females (65 vs. 63 years). However, within the female cohort, 75.0% (108 of 144) of esophageal cancer cases were diagnosed in women >55 years of age, suggesting that they were postmenopausal [ 22 ]. This result supports the hypothesis regarding estrogen’s inhibitory effect on esophageal carcinogenesis. Additionally, it has been reported that estrogen’s protective effect is more significant in the SCC subtype than adenocarcinoma [ 23 ]. Similarly, our study found that the diagnosis rate of SCC was higher in postmenopausal than premenopausal females, with 94.4% (102 of 108) of cases in females >55 years of age compared to 88.9% (32 of 36) <55 years of age.

Several studies have examined the survival outcomes based on sex, with some indicating better outcomes for females compared to males, while others found no significant differences between the sexes [ 19 22 24 25 26 ]. Factors such as age, race, tumor grade, histology, and treatment patterns have been associated with sex-based variations in survival [ 26 ]. Additionally, treatment efficacy can vary by tumor subtype, and even within the same tumor subtype, treatment effects may differ between sexes, contributing to differences in survival outcomes [ 27 ]. Biological differences between sexes have also been proposed to explain the disparities in outcomes between male and female patients with esophageal cancer, similar to findings in other gastrointestinal cancers [ 28 29 ]. Specifically, studies analyzing esophageal SCC and gastric adenocarcinoma found that overexpression of estrogen receptor (ER) alpha was associated with poor prognosis and poor cell differentiation, whereas ER beta overexpression was linked to better prognosis and good cell differentiation [ 30 ]. Further research is required to elucidate the mechanisms related to the chemopreventive role of sex hormones in esophageal cancer. In our study, we observed that females exhibited better OS and RFS compared to males. This finding persisted even when the analysis was restricted to a cohort of patients diagnosed with SCC. Additionally, we compared survival outcomes by sex across different stages and found that females consistently showed better OS and RFS than males at all stages.

Our study had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at a single tertiary referral center. While this may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations, the fact that esophageal cancer treatment is commonly performed at tertiary referral centers due to its complexity suggests that our findings may still be relevant to broader clinical practice. Second, approximately 33% of the patients had missing data regarding recurrence status, which might have impacted our RFS calculations and caused potential bias. Third, as this was a retrospective study, we lacked data on the menopausal status of female patients, making it impossible to analyze the potential influence of menopause on esophageal cancer outcomes. Fourth, we did not have hormonal assay data, such as luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone levels, which limited our ability to investigate the relationship between hormonal factors and esophageal cancer incidence and progression. Future research should aim to address these limitations by incorporating prospective designs, ensuring comprehensive data collection, including hormonal and menopausal status, and expanding to multicenter cohorts to enhance the robustness and generalizability of findings.

## CONCLUSIONS

CONCLUSIONS

Our study sheds light on significant sex-based disparities in esophageal cancer among the Korean population. Our findings highlight distinct clinical, pathological, and lifestyle differences between male and female patients, with factors such as tumor location, histopathologic type, smoking, and alcohol consumption showing notable variations. Importantly, our results underscore that sex itself is an independent prognostic factor, with females exhibiting better OS and RFS, emphasizing the need for tailored approaches in the management and understanding of esophageal cancer based on sex-specific considerations.
