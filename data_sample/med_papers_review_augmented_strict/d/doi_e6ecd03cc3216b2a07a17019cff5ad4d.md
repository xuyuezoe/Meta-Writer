# Mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability are associated with differing diabetes-related complications in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.

## Abstract

Aims: To study the different effects of mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability on diabetes-related complications in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Methods: 1869 patients with type 2 diabetes were followed-up for a median of 9.5 years in a Diabetes Shared Care Program. Mean HbA1c (HbA1c-mean) and standard deviation of HbA1c (HbA1c-SD) were calculated during the first 5 years. The clinical outcomes included nephropathy (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio [UACR] > 300 mg/g and doubling of serum creatinine), retinopathy (any and advanced), and mortality (due to all-causes, and cardiovascular disease [CVD]).

Results: HbA1c-mean was independently associated with UACR > 300 mg/g (Hazard ratio [HR] 1.308 [95% confidence interval {CI}, 1.194-1.433]), any retinopathy (HR 1.274 [1.171-1.385]), and advanced retinopathy (HR 1.237 [1.014-1.509]). HbA1c-SD was independently associated with UACR > 300 mg/g (HR 1.478 [1.189-1.837]), doubling of serum creatinine (HR 2.133 [1.470-3.095]), all-cause mortality (HR 1.880 [1.561-2.266]), and CVD mortality (HR 1.431 [1.069-1.915]). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves showed HbA1c-mean was more associated with any retinopathy, whereas HbA1c-SD was more associated with doubling of serum creatinine, all-cause and CVD mortality.

Conclusion: Both HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD predicted most diabetes-related complications in patients with type 2 diabetes. However, HbA1c-mean was more effective at predicting retinopathy, while HbA1c-SD was more effective at predicting deterioration of renal function and increased mortality.

Keywords HbA1c variability Mean HbA1c Diabetes complications 1 Introduction Cumulative evidence shows that long-term glycemic control is the main risk factor for the development of micro- and macro-vascular complications in diabetic patients [1]. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels reflect average glycemia over about three months, so current guidelines usually use HbA1c to assess glycemic control [2]. However, HbA1c does not provide a measure of hypoglycemia and glycemic variability. Glycemic variability relates to fluctuations in blood glucose levels. The potential risks associated with glycemic variability seem to be related to possible vascular damage due to excess glucose fluctuations, as well as an increased risk of hypoglycemia and its consequences [3]. Short-term glycemic variability, represented by within-day and between-day glycemic variability, may eventually be reflected in increased HbA1c levels [4]. HbA1c variability relates to changes in glycemic control over longer periods, which results in HbA1c changes between visits. Therefore, HbA1c variability is most commonly used to assess long-term glycemic variability [5,6]. Several studies have revealed that HbA1c variability was positively associated with micro- and macro-vascular complications, as well as mortality. A recent meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the association of HbA1c variability with diabetes-related complications and mortality in patients with diabetes mellitus [7]. It revealed that HbA1c variability was positively associated with diabetes-related complications and mortality, independent of HbA1c levels, which suggested that HbA1c variability might play a role in clinical assessment in the future. More recent observational studies of diabetes indicated that greater visit-to-visit glycemic variability was also associated with some diabetes-related complications [8–13]. Cumulative evidence suggests that both hyperglycemia and increased glycemic variability play important roles in diabetes-related complications. However, there are few studies that compare the effects of hyperglycemia and glycemic variability on these complications. We hypothesize that mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability might have different effects on the occurrence of diabetes-related complications in patients with type 2 diabetes. Therefore, we conducted this study to examine the different effects of mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability on diabetes-related complications in patients with type 2 diabetes. 2 Research design and methods 2.1 Study population and baseline examination This was a prospective study designed to evaluate the relationship between glycemic control and diabetes-related complications in patients with type 2 diabetes. There were 4450 subjects with type 2 diabetes who were at least 20 years old and who provided written informed consent and subsequently participated in the Diabetes Shared Care Program. We removed patients with a UACR > 300 mg/g; a serum creatinine > 1.50 mg/dL; a diagnosis or history of retinopathy, myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular diseases, or malignancy at baseline and those who were followed up less than five years. Finally, we recruited 1869 patients for further analysis (Fig. 1). The baseline data were collected from January 2004 to December 2015. The subjects were regularly followed at the Taipei Veterans General Hospital between January 2004 and December 2019. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Taipei Veterans General Hospital in Taiwan. Figure viewer Fig. 1 Flow diagram showing the process of patient selection used in this study. Information about demographics, diabetes duration, prescribed medication, and smoking status were obtained by our diabetes case managers. Patients received examinations for height, body weight, and blood pressure. Venipuncture was performed, and fasting serum samples were collected for the measurement of fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, creatinine, cholesterol, and triglyceride levels. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using a prediction formula from the four-variable Modification of Diet and Renal Disease study equation [14]. Spot urine samples were prepared in the early morning and urinary albumin excretion was determined using the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). Color photographs of the retinas, including macula- and disc-centered views, were taken at an angle of 45° with a fundus camera after pharmacological mydriasis. The fundus photographs were evaluated by trained physicians. 2.2 Follow-up examinations All patients were followed up at our clinics according to the national Diabetes Shared Care Program. In general, fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels were determined every three months. UACR, serum creatinine, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglyceride levels, and eye fundi examinations were determined every year. The eye fundi examinations, urine UACR, and serum creatinine were our study outcomes and were calculated until death or December 31, 2019. The other parameters were calculated for either five years or death. 2.3 Assessment of glycemic control Glycemic control was documented in terms of serial HbA1c measurements. HbA1c was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography with the TOSOH Automated Glycohemoglobin Analyzer HLC 723 G7/G8 (Tosoh, Japan) from January 2004 to September 30, 2018. The inter-assay with between-batch coefficient of variance was less than 2.0% at mean A1C levels between 4.4% and 8.2%. From Oct 1, 2018, to Sep 30, 2021, HbA1c was measured using electrophoresis with the Sebia CAPILLARYS 3 TERA (Sebia, France). The inter-assay with between-batch coefficient of variance was less than 2.0% at mean A1C levels between 5.5% and 10.1%. Mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability were calculated in the first five years after patients participated in the Diabetes Shared Care Program. These subjects received an average of 19 HbA1c measurements, with the highest being 42 measurements and the lowest being 10 measurements over a span of five years. To avoid the impact of taking multiple measurements in a short period, median HbA1c values from each year were taken for the first five years and were then used to determine mean HbA1c (HbA1c-mean). 2.4 Assessment of HbA1c variability According to previous reports, both standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV) of HbA1c were used as markers of HbA1c variability. In our study, we found that there was a strong correlation between HbA1c-mean and standard deviation of HbA1c (HbA1c-SD) (Supplemental Figure 1, γ = 0.503, p 300 mg/g and doubling of serum creatinine), retinopathy (any retinopathy and advanced retinopathy), and mortality (all-cause mortality and mortality due to cardiovascular disease [CVD]). Retinopathy was assessed using a fundus camera to obtain a central view of the macula and disc at a 45° angle. We classified the severity of the retinopathy into the following four categories: no apparent retinopathy, mild-to-moderate non-proliferative retinopathy, severe non-proliferative retinopathy, and proliferative retinopathy. Patients with abnormal findings were referred to ophthalmologists for further evaluation. Advanced retinopathy was defined as proliferative retinopathy or having had post-laser photocoagulation or vitrectomy. Information on the date and cause of death were obtained from the Department of Health, Executive Yuan, ROC (Taiwan) and calculated until December 31, 2019. 2.6 Statistical analysis Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0 for Windows. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were presented as numbers with percentages. Participants were categorized into quartiles according to HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD. Trends across the four quartiles were analyzed using one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) for normally distributed variables and chi-square tests with Mantel-Haenszel ordinary trend tests for non-normally distributed variables. Triglyceride levels and UACR were expressed as medians with IQRs and were analyzed using non-parametric tests. Baseline variables were compared with the first quartile using a student t-test or other suitable methods. The non-continuous data were presented as percentages and analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square tests. HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD were investigated using Cox proportional hazard models and hazard ratios (HRs) of outcomes for 1 SD increments of HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD. HRs of incidences and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using multivariable Cox regression models. The Cox proportional hazard models were adjusted with prespecified variables. Model 1 was unadjusted, model 2 was adjusted with HbA1c-mean or HbA1c-SD, and model 3 was adjusted with HbA1c-mean or HbA1c-SD, age, sex, diabetes duration, blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol level, triglyceride level, and smoking status. To examine whether diabetic complications were associated with HbA1c-mean or HbA1c-SD, we used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to compare the HRs of HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD dissociation groups to assess their performance in predicting our study outcomes. We plotted the ROC curve and calculated the area under curve (AUC) values to compare the predictive ability of HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD on nephropathy (UACR > 300 mg/g, doubling of serum creatinine), retinopathy (any retinopathy, advanced retinopathy) and mortality (all-cause mortality and CVD mortality). 3 Results 3.1 Characteristics of the study population Fig. 1 shows a flow diagram of the patient selection process and the number of patients falling into each of the study outcomes that occurred during the follow-up period (Fig. 1). The average age at baseline was 63.2 ± 12.7 years, with 50.4% of the recruited patient population being men. The average body mass index was 26.10 ± 3.98 kg/m2, and the average initial HbA1c was 8.06 ± 1.77%. The median follow-up duration was 9.5 years (IQR 7.4–12.0). Upon analysis of our data, the average values for HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD were determined to be 7.69 ± 1.18% and 0.728 ± 0.528%, respectively. The baseline demographics by HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD quartile are shown in Supplemental Table 1 and Supplemental Table 2, respectively. Compared with the first quartile, the patients in the fourth quartile of HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD had increased HbA1c-mean, HbA1c-SD, diabetes duration, body weight, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol, triglyceride levels, UACR, and decreased age. However, patients in the fourth quartile of HbA1c-SD had higher serum creatinine and lower eGFR. In addition, there were more female patients in the fourth quartile of HbA1c-mean, whereas there were more male patients in the fourth quartile of HbA1c-SD. Open table in a new tab 4 Discussion To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to investigate the different effects of mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability on diabetes-related complications in patients with type 2 diabetes. Patients were monitored for a median of 9.5 years and, during that time, our crude model (Model 1) showed that patients with increased HbA1c-mean and HbA1c-SD had a significantly increased risk of developing diabetes-related complications, which were listed as our six study endpoints. After full adjustment of the model (Model 3), increased HbA1c-mean was associated with a significantly increased risk of a UACR > 300 mg/g and any and advanced retinopathy, while increased HbA1c-SD was associated with a significantly increased risk of a UACR > 300 mg/g, doubling of serum creatinine, and all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. Further analysis was conducted with ROC curves, which showed that the HbA1c-mean was more effective than the HbA1c-SD at predicting any retinopathy, whereas the HbA1c-SD was more effective at predicting the doubling of serum creatinine, all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. Recent evidence suggests that diabetes-related complications are predicted not only by HbA1c levels but also by HbA1c variability between visits, independent of average HbA1c and other risk factors present. Gorst et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate the association between HbA1c variability and outcomes in patients with diabetes. Their analysis showed that higher HbA1c variability was associated with a higher risk of renal disease, cardiovascular disease, and mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes [7]. The retinopathy appeared not to be associated with HbA1c variability in patients with type 2 diabetes [7], which we also found in our study. More recently, new evidence has also supported glycemic variability as an independent risk factor for nephropathy, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular disease mortality [8–13]. Although cumulative evidence has suggested a role for HbA1c variability in diabetes-related complications, most studies have not mentioned the correlation between HbA1c variability and retinopathy, and some studies gave contradictory findings for retinopathy. Our data provided evidence of a wide spectrum of associations between mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability with nephropathy and retinopathy in patients with type 2 diabetes. In our present study, both mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability were independently well-correlated with macroalbuminuria, presented as a UACR > 300 mg/g. However, only HbA1c variability was independently well-correlated with deteriorated kidney function, presented as a doubling of serum creatinine. In contrast, only mean HbA1c correlated independently with any and advanced retinopathy. According to our findings and previous reports, there may be different mechanisms that link glycemic control to the microvascular complications of diabetes. Although we cannot attribute poor prognosis to HbA1c variability per se, some underlying mechanisms may explain the associations observed in our study. The underlying mechanisms for the association between HbA1c variability and chronic complications may include oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial damage [15–18]. Some human studies have shown that oxidative stress occurs with glycemic variability [15] and that fluctuating blood glucose significantly deteriorates endothelial function because of oxidative stress, rather than mean glucose concentration [19]. Ceriello et al. suggested that the potential risks associated with glycemic variability seem to be related to possible vascular damage due to glucose fluctuations and an increased risk of hyperglycemia and its complications [19]. It is not clear whether these complications are increased in patients with high visit-to-visit HbA1c variability. An alternative mechanism may be related to accumulated epigenetic modifications induced by both hyper- and hypoglycemia [20–22]. Long-term glycemic variability is partly a reflection of ambient hyperglycemia because long-term glucose variability is significantly correlated with mean concentration of blood glucose [23] and HbA1c [5]. However, the possible mechanisms for long-term glycemic variability are not well-defined. Another explanation may simply be related to increased hypoglycemia in patients with higher HbA1c variability. Hence, it is clear that excessive short-term glycemic variability in the presence of target HbA1c levels usually leads to an increased risk of hypoglycemia. Our study has several strengths. Firstly, it is a prospective study, with a large cohort size, long follow-up duration, and comprehensive data. Secondly, we used time-weighted mean HbA1c to represent long-term glycemia and chose the highest and lowest values for each year to calculate HbA1c variability, which avoids the impact of taking multiple measurements in a short time period. Thirdly, we extensively studied six clinically important outcomes, including macroalbuminuria, doubling of serum creatinine, any retinopathy, advanced retinopathy, all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. Fourthly, our study was based on the real-world data of diabetes care in Taiwan, meaning our results directly translate into clinical practice. However, this study has some limitations. Firstly, we excluded patients who had HbA1c measures taken less than two times per year in the first five years after joining the Diabetes Shared Care Program. Therefore, we selected patients with better compliance for the study, which might create selection bias in our results. Secondly, we removed patients with a UACR > 300 mg/g, serum creatinine > 1.50 mg/dL, or retinopathy at baseline. Thus, there is uncertainty in the role of HbA1c variability and mean HbA1c in unselected patients. Thirdly, we did not investigate the mechanisms that underpinned our novel findings, including the mechanisms explaining why HbA1c variability was better at predicting deterioration of renal function and mortality, and why mean HbA1c was better at predicting retinopathy. Fourthly, this is an observational study, so we cannot confirm the causal relationship between glycemic control (average HbA1c and HbA1c variability) and diabetes-related complications. We also did not provide the medication dispensing data in our study, due to the fact that medication use was adjusted frequently in clinical practice. In conclusion, our study revealed that increased mean HbA1c and HbA1c variability were associated with a significantly increased risk of developing a UACR > 300 mg/g, which signified deteriorating kidney function. Increased mean HbA1c was associated with a significantly increased risk of retinopathy, while increased HbA1c variability was associated with a significantly increased risk of doubling serum creatinine (indicating deterioration of renal function), all-cause mortality and CVD mortality.
