# Risk of fracture in ulcerative colitis: a population-based study from Olmsted County, Minnesota.

## Abstract

Background & aims: Osteopenia is common in patients with ulcerative colitis (UC), but less is known about fracture risk. Previously we were unable to demonstrate increased fractures in a population-based cohort with Crohn's disease.

Methods: Medical records of 273 Olmsted County, Minnesota residents initially diagnosed with UC in 1940-1993 were reviewed for evidence of subsequent fractures, as were records of a control cohort of county residents matched on age and gender. Cumulative fracture incidence after diagnosis was estimated by using the Kaplan-Meier method. The hazard ratio of fracture in cases relative to control subjects was estimated by Cox proportional hazards regression, which was also used to evaluate potential risk factors for fracture.

Results: Median follow-up was 13 years (range, 1 day-53 years). The cumulative incidence of any fracture from time of diagnosis was 40% at 25 years versus 42% in control subjects (P=0.615). The hazard ratio in cases compared to control subjects was 1.1 (95% confidence interval, 0.8-1.6) for any fracture and 1.3 (95% confidence interval, 0.6-2.8) for any osteoporotic fracture (hip, spine, or wrist as a result of moderate trauma). Other causes of secondary osteoporosis were associated with increased fracture risk, whereas estrogen use was protective. One hundred three cases received any corticosteroids (38%), and 34 (12%) had taken corticosteroids for 6 months or longer. Corticosteroids and bowel resection were not associated with fracture risk.

Conclusions: In this population-based cohort of patients with UC, fracture risk was not elevated relative to matched community control subjects. Use of corticosteroids did not appear to significantly influence the risk of fracture.

Abbreviations CI (confidence interval) HR (hazard ratio) IBD (inflammatory bowel disease) IRR (incidence rate ratio) SIR (standardized incidence ratio) UC (ulcerative colitis) Ulcerative colitis (UC), one of the major subtypes of idiopathic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), might affect as many as 700,000 North Americans.1 Numerous studies have investigated the prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis in persons with UC, although the exact figures vary with study population and disease definitions.2–19 For example, a population-based study of UC patients from Oslo, Norway estimated the prevalence of osteopenia at 15% and osteoporosis at 8%,13 whereas studies from referral centers have generally indicated an even higher burden of metabolic bone disease.7,8,10,12,14,15,17,20 Bone loss has most often been attributed to corticosteroid therapy,2,7–11,13–15,17,21–23 but this has not been demonstrated in all studies.3,6,12 Indeed, IBD itself might predispose to osteoporosis, and several studies have reported osteoporosis and even vertebral fractures in newly diagnosed and steroid-naive patients.6,24 Other potential risk factors for bone loss include vitamin D insufficiency11,25 and reduced body mass index.2,13,15 The influence of bowel resection on osteoporosis is not entirely clear because small bowel resection has been reported to aggravate bone loss in Crohn’s disease,26,27 whereas proctocolectomy might reverse this.5,9 Relatively few studies have examined the actual risk of fracture among patients with UC. A large matched cohort study from Manitoba estimated that the risk of osteoporotic fracture in UC patients was increased 45% relative to that of matched control subjects, compared to a 36% increase in risk in patients with Crohn’s disease.28 A questionnaire-based study of members of a Danish IBD patient support organization indicated increased fracture risk among women with Crohn’s disease, but no increase in risk was noted in UC patients.29 Similarly, fracture risk was increased by 15%–20% among patients with Crohn’s disease, but no increased risk for overall fractures could be demonstrated in UC among 15,000 IBD patients identified in a national hospital registry in Denmark.30 By contrast, we were unable to detect a significantly increased overall risk of fracture, relative to matched control subjects, in a previous study of Olmsted County, Minnesota residents with Crohn’s disease.31 Neither duration, cumulative dose of corticosteroids, nor history of intestinal resection increased the risk of fracture in that study. In the present investigation, we assessed the long-term risk of fracture in a population-based inception cohort of Olmsted County residents with UC and attempted to determine the influence of various clinical features on this risk by a comprehensive review of complete (inpatient and outpatient) community medical records. Methods Olmsted County, Minnesota is well suited for studies of disease associations such as this because comprehensive medical records for the residents are available for review and because these records are accessible through a centralized index of diagnoses made by essentially all medical care providers used by the local population.32 Most gastrointestinal and trauma care, for example, is provided by the Mayo Clinic, which has maintained a common medical record with its 2 affiliated hospitals in the community (St. Mary’s and Rochester Methodist) for more than 95 years. This dossier-type record, therefore, contains both inpatient and outpatient data.33 Medical records of the other providers who serve the local population, most notably the Olmsted Medical Center (Olmsted Medical Group and its affiliated Olmsted Community Hospital), are also indexed into this system and are available for use in approved studies.32 This medical records linkage system (the Rochester Epidemiology Project) permitted the identification of all health care records containing a diagnosis of IBD whether resulting from inpatient or outpatient care, death certification, or autopsy. By using study criteria described in detail elsewhere,34,35 the complete medical records of each candidate case were retrieved and reviewed for the diagnosis of UC in the period 1940 through 1993. The Olmsted County residents with UC were matched by age (±2 years) and gender to control subjects from the county who had not had IBD. The control pool consisted of all Olmsted County residents who were medically attended during the calendar year of diagnosis of each case. Because almost all county residents have at least 1 contact with the medical system during any 3-year period, this pool is essentially an enumeration of the population.32 Among potential control subjects, the one with the closest medical registration number was chosen. Medical registration numbers are unique to each individual and are assigned at the initial visit for each patient, so this procedure matches for the duration of documented clinical history before the index date (the date of diagnosis for cases and the date of closest clinic visit for control subjects). Nine potential cases (and their matched control subjects) were excluded from the study because they had not provided authorization for review of their medical records for research.36 After approval by the Institutional Review Boards of Mayo Clinic and Olmsted Medical Center, the remaining 273 matched case and control pairs were followed forward in time through their linked medical records in the community (retrospective cohort study). Each subject’s complete inpatient and outpatient record at each local provider of medical care was searched by trained nurse abstractors for the occurrence of any fracture. Although it was not possible in a medical record study such as this to blind the reviewers to the subject’s status (case or control), it was only necessary to record the occurrence of specific fractures, and this objective end point is not prone to bias. Follow-up continued until death or the most recent clinical contact documented in community medical records. The records contained the clinical history and the radiologist’s report of each fracture, but the original roentgenograms were not consistently available for review. Consequently, the diagnosis of vertebral fracture was accepted on the basis of a radiologist’s report of compression or collapse of 1 or more thoracic or lumbar vertebrae.37 Fractures were also classified according to the circumstances of the injury; by convention, falls from standing height or less were considered moderate trauma, whereas motor vehicle accidents and falls from a height were deemed severe trauma. Fractures of the hip, spine, or distal forearm that resulted from minimal or moderate trauma at age 35 years or older were taken to represent osteoporotic fractures.38 The medical records of the cases were also abstracted for the presence of conditions associated with secondary osteoporosis (separately and in aggregate) including thyroid adenoma, hyperthyroidism, thyroidectomy, peptic ulcer, gastric resection, large/small bowel resection, renal failure, rheumatoid arthritis, increasing or decreasing adrenal function, increasing parathyroid function, pancreatitis, cirrhosis of liver, malabsorption syndrome, pernicious anemia, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, and substantial (meaning 6 months or more) use of thyroid supplements, anticoagulants, or corticosteroids. Records were also abstracted for the presence of risk factors for falling, including stroke, hemiparesis/hemiplegia, or transient ischemic attacks; balance disorders; cataract, blindness and other vision problems; heart arrhythmias, hypotension, or syncopal attacks; seizure disorder; parkinsonism; poliomyelitis; multiple sclerosis; or paraplegia. The influence of UC on fracture risk was evaluated by using 4 basic methods of analysis. In the primary analysis, the rate of occurrence (risk or hazard) of fractures in the cases was compared directly with that in the control subjects by using a stratified proportional hazards model with the case/control pairs forming the strata.39 In such analyses, the follow-up of both members of a pair is censored at the earliest event or follow-up date of either member. Hazard ratios (HRs) compared the rate of occurrence of fractures in cases and control subjects. In a secondary analysis, we calculated standardized incidence ratios (SIRs), comparing the number of fractures that were observed at each skeletal site (on the basis of the first fracture of a given type per person) to the number expected among cases and control subjects, separately, during their follow-up in the community. This approach provides a validation of the representativeness of the control cohort, as well as an independent assessment of the extent to which fractures were increased among the cases. Expected numbers of fractures were derived by applying age- and gender-specific incidence rates of these fractures from the general population of Olmsted County to the age- and gender-specific person-years of follow-up among the cases and control subjects separately and summing over the age-gender strata.37,40–43 Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (95% CI) for the SIRs were calculated assuming that the expected rates are fixed and the observed fractures follow a Poisson distribution.44 In the third method of analysis, the cumulative incidence of a new fracture (1 minus probability of survival-free-of-fracture) was projected for up to 25 years after the index date by using product-limit life table methods.45 Again, follow-up was censored at the earlier of the 2 last dates of follow-up for each case-control pair. A stratified log-rank test, with the case-control pairs forming the strata, was used to compare cumulative fracture incidence. Life table methods were also used to assess survival, relative to control subjects, and the survival curves were compared by using the log-rank test statistic.46 In the final approach, Cox proportional hazards regression models,39 which do not incorporate the population expected rates, were used to assess the impact of various covariates (e.g., age, calendar year of diagnosis, corticosteroid use, causes of secondary osteoporosis, risk factors for falling) on the subsequent risk of fracture among the cases. Univariate relationships between the risk of specific fractures and each clinical characteristic under consideration were first assessed. Stepwise methods with forward selection and backward elimination were then used to choose independent variables for the final models. The dependent variable was time until the first new fracture, and the independent variables were the clinical characteristics. For the final multiple models, as well as for the univariate models, the assumption of proportional hazards was examined and was not violated for the variables considered. All analyses were carried out in SAS (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC). Results During the period 1940–1993, 273 Olmsted County residents were first diagnosed with UC at a mean (± standard deviation) age of 37.4 ± 16.2 years (range, 7–87 years). There were 153 men (56%) and 120 women. All but 3 of the cases were white, in keeping with the racial composition of the community (98% white in 1980). The extent of disease involvement was proctitis in 100 (37%), left-sided colitis in 88 (32%), and extensive colitis in 82 (30%). The degree of involvement could not be determined in 3 patients (1%). Thirty-six patients (13%) had received corticosteroids within the first year of diagnosis of UC, and a total of 103 patients (38%) had received any corticosteroids during their clinical course. Thirty-four patients (12%) received steroids for a cumulative duration of at least 6 months. Altogether, 61 patients (26%) underwent at least 1 bowel resection, including 24 total proctocolectomies with Brooke ileostomy, 14 total proctocolectomies with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis, 10 total proctocolectomies with Kock pouch, 5 subtotal colectomies, 3 segmental colonic resections, and 5 miscellaneous procedures. Survival in the UC cases was no different than expected, 74% after 25 years versus 76% expected (P = 0.757), and the cohort of UC patients was followed subsequently for a total of 4034 person-years (median, 13.0 years per subject; range, 1 day–53 years). During this period of observation, 75 subjects experienced 154 different fractures. The distribution of these fractures by skeletal site and cause among the UC patients is delineated in Table 1. Sixty-six fractures (43%) were due to severe trauma (15 motor vehicle accidents, 18 falls from a height, 8 recreational, and 25 miscellaneous causes), and 77 (50%) were attributed to minimal or moderate trauma. Of the latter group, the 31 fractures that occurred “spontaneously” in the course of everyday activities mostly involved the thoracic and lumbar vertebrae (n = 27). Discussion Although the reported prevalence of osteopenia or osteoporosis is increased in numerous studies of IBD, only a few studies have determined the risk of fracture, the relevant clinical end point in these patients. In this population-based inception cohort of UC, we were unable to demonstrate any increase in the overall risk of fracture relative to age- and gender-matched control subjects. The cumulative risk of any fracture among UC cases was 40%, similar to the cumulative incidence among control subjects (42%). In a multivariate analysis, the only clinical variables independently associated with overall fracture risk were other causes of secondary osteoporosis. Importantly, corticosteroid use and bowel resection were not associated with an increased fracture risk in this study. In a secondary analysis based on SIRs (observed/expected) among the UC cases alone, the risk of osteoporotic fractures was increased 80% over that expected on the basis of previous studies in the county; however, this analysis was considered less rigorous than the primary analysis that compared cases and control subjects directly and could not demonstrate an increased risk. Age and left-sided colitis were independently associated with the risk of an osteoporotic fracture (hip/wrist/spine due to moderate trauma after age 35 years). Our study results are similar to the findings of 2 Danish reports with different methodologies (one relied on questionnaires,29 and the other used a large national hospital registry and an administrative database30) insofar as no increase in the overall risk of fracture could be identified in UC patients. However, our results are somewhat at variance with a study from Manitoba that used a large administrative database to identify cases, controls, and fractures.28 This apparent difference might be related to a number of methodologic issues including sample size and ascertainment of fractures. The sample size in the study by Bernstein et al.28 was 10 times the size of our sample, and total person-years of follow-up were nearly 5 times larger. Whereas fractures were identified in the present study by review of radiology reports in each patient’s complete inpatient and outpatient medical records, only fractures identified by an International Classification of Diseases-ninth revision-Clinical Modification code were included in the Manitoba study. Altogether, 221 incident fractures of the hip, spine, wrist, and ribs were observed among UC cases during 19,665 person-years of follow-up, for a crude annual incidence rate of 112 fractures per 10,000.28 In contrast, we observed 80 fractures of the hip, spine, wrist, and ribs during 4034 person-years of follow-up, for a crude annual incidence rate of 198 per 10,000. This suggests considerable underascertainment of (presumably asymptomatic) osteoporotic fractures in the study by Bernstein et al. Given the very large sample size of their study, however, the incidence rate ratio of 1.45 for fractures of hip, spine, wrist, and ribs was statistically significant. Interestingly, our study observed a similar HR of 1.2 (95% CI, 0.6–2.4) for the same fractures, but our sample size was considerably smaller. On the other hand, the Danish national hospital registry study, which is by far the largest of these population-based studies, detected a significantly increased incidence rate ratio (IRR) for fractures of the spine, ribs, and pelvis only in the period of observation before UC diagnosis (IRR, 2.0; 95% CI, 1.3–3.1).30 In that study, the risk of these fractures was not increased after the diagnosis of UC (IRR, 1.0; 95% CI, 0.7–1.6), and the risk of hip or other femur fractures was not increased either before or after diagnosis.30 Although many studies have examined the association between bone mineral density and corticosteroid use in IBD patients with conflicting results, there are fewer data regarding fracture risk and steroid therapy. A cross-sectional study of bone density and vertebral fracture prevalence among patients with Crohn’s disease from Europe and Israel about to enter a randomized clinical trial showed a discordance between the degree of osteopenia and vertebral fracture prevalence.47 Although bone density was significantly lower in steroid-dependent patients with Crohn’s disease compared to steroid-naive patients or those who had formerly received corticosteroids, the prevalence of vertebral fracture was nearly identical in all 3 groups (12%–16%); moreover, the fracture prevalence did not correlate with current steroid status or cumulative steroid dose.47 However, whether these findings in Crohn’s disease patients seen at referral centers are applicable to population-based UC cohorts is not at all clear. In the questionnaire-based Danish study, current or previous use of systemic corticosteroids was not associated with fracture risk.29 The influence of corticosteroids on the risk of fracture in UC was not evaluated in the Manitoba or Danish studies.28,30 In our present study, any use of corticosteroids, increased duration of use, and increased cumulative dose were not associated with fracture risk. The association between increased age and fractures has been noted in some but not all studies of fracture risk in IBD. In the population-based Danish study, increased age was associated with fractures overall for both UC and Crohn’s disease, both before and after diagnosis.30 Incidence rates of hip, spine, wrist, rib, and all fragility fractures were higher in the group older than 60 years of age for both UC and Crohn’s disease in the Manitoba study.48 In the inception cohort of Crohn’s disease from Olmsted County, age was the only independent predictor of overall fracture risk.31 On the other hand, 2 cross-sectional studies of vertebral fracture prevalence in Crohn’s disease could not detect an association between age and fracture.47,49 In the present study of UC patients, age was independently associated with osteoporotic fracture risk (age was associated with overall fracture risk in the univariate but not multivariate analysis). One of the strengths of our study was that the cohort was population-based and therefore reflected the wide spectrum of clinical severity of UC and its attendant complications seen in the community. In addition, the control subjects were selected from an enumeration of the entire Olmsted County population and therefore should have been representative of the local population generally.32 This is supported by the observation that, with the exception of vertebral fractures, there was no statistically significant difference in the number of fractures observed in the control subjects versus that expected on the basis of fracture incidence rates in the County. Furthermore, the UC patients were followed forward from the date of their initial diagnosis for up to 53 years (median, 13 years). Fractures were ascertained by using the resources of the Rochester Epidemiology Project, which allowed access to all outpatient and inpatient records (including radiology reports) so that outcomes could be assessed comparably in cases and control subjects.32 Except for vertebral fractures, some of which are never diagnosed,37 the ascertainment of clinically evident fractures is believed to be complete.43 Finally, potential risk factors could be evaluated at a level of clinical detail unavailable in administrative databases. Although the small size of our cohort precluded an ability to identify modest increases in the risk of specific types of fractures, our overall results indicate that fractures do not represent a substantial problem for most patients with UC. Moreover, we were unable to identify IBD-specific risk factors that might increase the likelihood of fractures.
