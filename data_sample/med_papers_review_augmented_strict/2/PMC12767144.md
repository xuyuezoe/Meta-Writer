# Onconephrology.

## Abstract

Subspecialized training in the field of onconephrology offers a uniquely informed approach to the care of the patient with both cancer and kidney disease. There is a significant knowledge gap that has been created by the fact that patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min are generally excluded from clinical cancer trials. Thus, oncologists rely on the clinical experience and expertise of onconephrologists to dose cytotoxic chemotherapies, to recognize and manage complications of newer targeted drugs and immunotherapies, to become partners in establishing guidelines relating to the management of these complications, and to collaborate on the design of future clinical trials in this cohort. In this section, we provide a glimpse into some common clinical scenarios encountered by the onconephrologist and demonstrate how clinical data and observations inform decision-making in these situations.

## Main text

Subspecialized training in the field of onconephrology offers a uniquely informed approach to the care of the patient with both cancer and kidney disease. During their subspecialty training, fellows are embedded with the hematology, oncology, supportive care, and surgical inpatient and outpatient services and thus emerge with a unique skill set that facilitates a more comprehensive approach to patient care as well as better communication with the multidisciplinary teams that care for patients with cancer and kidney disease. There is a significant knowledge gap that has been created by the fact that patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min are generally excluded from clinical cancer trials. Thus, oncologists rely on the clinical experience and expertise of onconephrologists to dose cytotoxic chemotherapies, to recognize and manage complications of newer targeted drugs and immunotherapies, to become partners in establishing guidelines relating to the management of these complications, and to collaborate on the design of future clinical trials in this cohort. It is not uncommon for patients to seek the advice and opinion of the nephrologist for decision-making regarding therapeutic interventions for their malignancy when these treatments can affect the risk for acute kidney injury (AKI), chronic kidney disease, and even dialysis dependency. In this section, we provide a glimpse into some common clinical scenarios encountered by the onconephrologist and demonstrate how clinical data and observations inform decision-making in these situations.

## CASE 1

CASE 1

MGRS refers to a hematologic disorder that produces a nephrotoxic monoclonal immunoglobulin (MIg) and does not meet previously defined criteria for treatment of a specific hematologic malignancy. The type of kidney lesion is dictated bytheunique ultrastructuralcharacteristicsandphysiochemical properties of the monoclonal immunoglobulin. The International Kidney and Monoclonal Gammopathy Research Group has proposed a categorization of MGRS based on kidney biopsy and immunofluorescence (IF) studies. MGRS lesions are initially categorized by the presence or absence of MIg deposits. Lesions with MIg are subcategorized into those with nonorganized or organized (fibrillar, microtubular, inclusions, or crystalline deposits). 1

Most cases of LC proximal tubulopathy (LCPT) have a kappa-restricted crystalline morphology. A less common noncrystalline morphology that is usually lambda restricted has also been described. 2 Somatic mutations in the variable region of the LC result in physiochemical properties that allow the LC to resist proteolysis in the proximal tubule cells, and they subsequently aggregate to form intracellular crystals. The crystalline morphology is commonly associated with a partial or complete Fanconi syndrome, characterized by normoglycemic glucosuria, hypophosphatemia, aminoaciduria, and metabolic acidosis. Fanconi syndrome is observed more commonly in LCPT than with LC cast nephropathy and can be a clue to this diagnosis.

Observational data in patients with MGRS lesions suggests that these kidney diseases are progressive if treated conservatively, and there is a high rate of recurrence and graft loss after kidney transplantation. Response to immunosuppressive therapy is also poor. 2 , 3 Patients treated with clonal-directed chemotherapy are able to achieve complete response (CR) or very good partial response (VGPR) hematologic responses. This is important because better renal outcomes have been observed in those who achieve deeper hematologic responses (CR/VGPR). 2 , 4 However, when baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate is below 20 mL/min, achieving a CR/VGPR may not be associated with improved renal outcomes. 5

Identifying the monoclonal proteins by immunofixation (IF) is essential to making the diagnosis of MGRS. This can be challenging with crystalline LCPT and pronase digestion on paraffin-fixed tissue is often needed to unmask kappa LCPT. 6 In this case, the initial IF on frozen tissue was negative for LCs, whereas intense staining for kappa was seen on pronase-digested paraffin sections. For patients with an MGRS lesion on the renal biopsy, the initial workup should include an evaluation for an underlying hematologic malignancy like multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and amyloid light chain amyloidosis ( Fig 1 ). If these conditions are not present and if the patient has CKD or ESRD and may be a candidate for a kidney transplant, they should be treated with clone-directed therapy prior to the kidney transplant. If they are not a kidney transplant candidate, then they can be observed until hematologic progression. Patients without CKD or ESRD should also undergo clonal-directed therapy. 7 For patients with an identifiable plasma cell clone, the International Kidney and Monoclonal Gammopathy Research Group recommends therapy with cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, and dexamethasone (CyBorD). 8 Addition of daratumumab to CyBorD may achieve better CR/VGPR. 9 , 10 For those with an underlying B lymphocyte hematologic disorder, recommended therapy includes cyclophosphamide, rituximab, and dexamethasone. 7

CASE 2

A 41-year-old female was recently diagnosed with IgA kappa multiple myeloma. She had presented to a local emergency room with new-onset edema and HTN associated with severe headaches. The BP was 200/98. She denied use of NSAIDs, Chinese herbal medications, oral contraceptives, or recreational drugs and was not taking any medications regularly.

Serum calcium and creatinine levels were 16 mg/dL and 7.8 mg/dL, respectively. A head CT showed no abnormalities, and CT of the chest/abdomen and pelvis showed no hydronephrosis, nephrolithiasis or vascular malformations. It is not uncommon for patients to seek the advice and opinion of the nephrologist when decision-making regarding therapeutic interventions for the malignancy affects the risk for AKI, possibly requiring dialysis. Reported rates of AKI post-auto-SCT range between 19% and 66%. This wide range is in part due to nonuniform definitions of AKI and nonuniform patient factors including the indication for the SCT, patient comorbidities, and choice of conditioning regimen. Risk factors for AKI post-SCT include advanced age, CKD, diabetes, HTN, and female gender. 11 Because this is a female patient with CKD, the risk of AKI is expected to fall at the higher end of the reported range.

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a chronic disease that is rarely cured. The overall goal when treating newly diagnosed MM is to maximize the depth of the hematologic response since deeper hematologic responses are associated with a longer duration of the first remission. 12 , 13 For patients who are transplant eligible, the current recommendation is to proceed with an auto-SCT following 3–4 cycles of induction therapy that includes an immunomodulatory drug, a proteosome inhibitor, and steroids ( Fig 2 ). 14 When compared to chemotherapy with bortezomib, lenalidomide and dexamethasone alone, the incorporation of auto-SCT in newly diagnosed patients is associated with superior PFS. Overall survival was not significantly different up to 84 months of follow-up, but median overall survival had not been reached in either arm. 12 Auto-SCT remains an integral part of therapy in patients with MM who are transplant eligible. The timing of the transplant is individualized based on patient age, patient preference, comorbidities, and tolerability to chemotherapy.

In this patient who had experienced radiographic response on an ICI and who now has progression of disease since the nivolumab was discontinued, rechallenge with nivolumab with the concomitant administration of low-dose corticosteroids and close monitoring is supported by a recent study by Gupta and colleagues. 15 Approximately 20% of 121 patients who presented with AKI associated with ICI and who were subsequently rechallenged developed recurrent ICI-AKI. Most of these patients received a dose of 10 mg daily. ICI rechallenge was offered to patients with partial or complete recovery of renal function after the AKI. Similar rates of recurrence have been reported in other single-center studies. 16

Among those who developed recurrent AKI after ICI rechallenge, most experienced AKI stage 1 or 2 and were also more likely to experience renal recovery following the initial AKI-ICI. All patients with recurrent AKI-ICI had their ICI therapy held, and most (14/20 [70%]) were treated with higher corticosteroid doses (40–60 mg daily). The National Comprehensive Cancer Network recommends rechallenge with ICI using low-dose corticosteroids. In cases of AKI-ICI, patients have been rechallenged with the same or other classes of immunotherapies, as deemed most appropriate by the treating oncologist. In patients without recovery of kidney function, the decision whether to rechallenge should be based on a multidisciplinary assessment of the risks/benefits and in discussion with the patient. 17 , 18

The most common indications for allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) in the United States are acute leukemias, myelodysplastic syndrome, and lymphomas. Multiple myeloma is the most common indication for autologous HSCT. 19 , 20 While the incidence of AKI post-HSCT is as high as 73% in some series, the reported incidence of glomerular pathology is only 1–2%. 21 Yap and colleagues reported on 2204 patients who underwent HSCT. De novo glomerular disease developed in 1.4% of patients over a 22-year period. 22 In this series, the most common glomerular diseases were transplant-associated thrombotic microangiopathy (TA-TMA) (38.7%), membranous nephropathy (MN) (25.8%), mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis (12.9%), and close to 10% had minimal change disease and focal and segmental glomerulosclerosis. Earlier reports of smaller sample sizes reported MN as the most common glomerular disease in this patient population. 23 – 25 Transplants from unrelated donors are associated with a higher risk for developing glomerular disease as compared to autologous or haploidentical HSCT. Increased HLA mismatches are associated with an increased risk for developing systemic GVHD. 21 , 26

GVHD has been hypothesized to involve the kidney. The proposed mechanism for nephrotic syndrome (NS) post-HSCT is immune dysregulation from donor lymphocytes with reactivity toward glomerular antigens. 27 Nephrotic syndrome typically presents more than 6 months following taper of the immunosuppression against GVHD. It can be diagnosed 8 to 14 months following HSCT and is observed more commonly in patients with systemic GVHD. 28 Characteristics for NS include nephrotic range proteinuria ≥3–3.5 g protein, hypoalbuminemia 350 mg/dL ( Table 4 ). Recognition can begin with a routine urinalysis, and new-onset proteinuria should prompt consideration for a kidney biopsy to identify the underlying glomerular pathology and to inform therapeutic intervention. While NS is a rare complication following HSCT, MN and TA-TMA should be suspected when this occurs. 27 Most patients with HSCT-associated MN are negative for antiphospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) antibodies. 24 Antibodies directed against glomerular target antigen protocadherin FAT 1 and neural epidermal growth factor-like 1 have been reported. 29 , 30 Treatment for PLA2R-negative membranous nephropathy is not well defined. Observational studies suggest rituximab as initial immunosuppressive therapy and a calcineurin inhibitor as an alternative. 31 Glucocorticoid monotherapy for MN in this setting is not recommended given variable treatment response. 32 A response generally occurs within 6 months and is measured by improvement in serum albumin and decrease in proteinuria.

Transplant-associated TMA is another cause of NS following HSCT and is the result of endothelial injury. Calcineurin inhibitors, chemotherapy, total body irradiation, infections, and abnormalities of the alternate complement pathway have all been demonstrated to produce endothelial injury. 33 Clinical and laboratory features of TA-TMA that are different from NS due to MN include the presence of new onset or worsening HTN, thrombocytopenia, anemia, decreased haptoglobin, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and the presence of schistocytes on a peripheral smear. It is important to appreciate that TA-TMA can be renal limited without the presence of these clinical or laboratory characteristics, so this diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion and a renal biopsy. However, the presence of thrombocytopenia can impair the ability to obtain a renal biopsy. Discontinuation of calcineurin inhibitors is not recommended since this may increase the risk of GVHD. 34 There is some functional, genetic, and therapeutic evidence for a role of complement in the pathogenesis of TA-TMA. A recent systematic review of the literature concluded that for those with biomarker evidence to support complement activation, therapy to inhibit C5 could improve outcomes but that there is insufficient evidence to support systematic use of complement inhibition therapy in all patients. 35 Thus, therapy for TA-TMA should be based on an assessment of the complement pathway and in consultation with the patient’s hematology team. Plasma exchange is not routinely recommended. Prognosis in patients is poor with a mortality rate as high as 50% in patients with systemic TA-TMA following HSCT. 36

BK viremia can occur as a complication post-HSCT due to BK virus reactivation while on immunosuppression. Plasma cell BK levels >10,000 copies per milliliter are more indicative of renal injury than elevated urinary levels. 37 BK viremia does not typically present with NS. Radiation nephropathy is a recognized complication post-HSCT and generally occurs 6 to 12 months following high-intensity total body irradiation. Radiation-induced vascular, glomerular, and tubulointerstitial damage can produce clinical features of HTN, hemolysis, and proteinuria, but other findings of NS (hypoalbuminemia, nephrotic range proteinuria) are not typically present.

CASE 5

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) manifests as a syndrome of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and end-organ damage dysfunction. The triggering factor in TMA is thought to be endothelial damage caused by antibodies, circulating immune complexes, endotoxins, or drugs. This results in platelet aggregation and activation of the complement system. 38

The pathophysiology of drug-induced thrombotic microangiopathy (DITMA) may be attributed to 2 potential mechanisms: immune-mediated and nonimmune-mediated. The nonimmune-mediated pathway involves endothelial injury that is dose-dependent or duration-dependent, leading to platelet aggregation and complement activation. In contrast, immune-mediated DITMA is linked to the formation of drug-induced antibodies and drug-dependent binding to platelets, typically occurring within hours to approximately 21 days of exposure. Gemcitabine is thought to induce TMA through both immune and direct cytotoxic effects, as evidenced by the timeline and therapeutic response. 39 – 41

Patients may present with increased creatinine levels, worsening or new-onset HTN, proteinuria, and edema. Hypertension is observed in 75%−90% of patients experiencing chemotherapy-associated TMA. The proteinuria often falls within the subnephrotic range but can occasionally reach nephrotic range. Comprehensive laboratory assessment should include a complete blood count with differential, LDH, haptoglobin, and a peripheral smear for schistocytes, testing for ADAMTS13, complement levels, and an aHUS panel. Haptoglobin levels decrease due to binding with free hemoglobin, which is then cleared by macrophages. Elevated LDH levels are often indicative of tissue ischemia and cell lysis. Schistocytes arise from the fragmentation of RBCs within the microvasculature, and a count exceeding 1% or ≥2 per hpf is considered significant. It is important to note that hematologic features of TMA may not always be evident in DITMA. An integral aspect of the diagnosis involves ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura by measuring ADAMTS13 activity. 42 , 43

While the introduction of therapeutic plasma exchange, immunosuppression, and complement inhibition has enhanced outcomes in morbidity and mortality for primary TMAs, the approach to secondary TMAs, especially drug-induced TMA, is still not well defined. 44 , 45 Plasma exchange has not been shown to improve outcomes in DITMA. According to the American Society for Apheresis guidelines, therapeutic plasma exchange for DITMA (specifically caused by gemcitabine) is a Grade 2 C/Category IV recommendation (ineffective). 46 Eculizumab is a recombinant humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to C5 and prevents the formation of the proinflammatory C5a and membrane attack complex C5b9 in the alternative pathway of the complement system, thereby inhibiting inappropriate systemic coagulation. 47 The positive outcomes observed with its use in aHUS have prompted consideration of its use in DITMA. Rogier and colleagues describe renal function recovery and return of hematologic parameters to baseline after 7 doses of eculizumab in a patient with gemcitabine-induced thrombotic microangiopathy (GiTMA) that failed to respond to plasma exchange. 47 – 49 There is a significant knowledge gap regarding the role of eculizumab for DITMA. It is important to emphasize that 1) there is generally a publication bias for positive outcomes as seen in Rogier and similar case series, 2) that the complement pathway was not assessed in all reported cases, and 3) no randomized controlled studies have been performed in this area. 50 , 51 Given the substantial and sudden deterioration in renal function seen in this patient, it may not be advisable to reintroduce gemcitabine following a TMA episode. Given the evidence of complement abnormalities and the ongoing decline in renal function after discontinuing gemcitabine, initiating a trial of eculizumab may be a reasonable next step in this case.

## SUMMARY

SUMMARY

In this section, we aimed to highlight some of the common questions that the onconephrology consultant is presented with. The questions focused on the intersections between kidney disease and multiple myeloma, bone marrow transplantation, cancer chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and monoclonal gammopathies. Although multiple myeloma accounts for only 1.8% of all new cancer cases per year, 50% of patients experience some type of renal injury during the course of their disease, and it is the most common malignancy that leads to end stage kidney disease. Because they are rare but clinically significant cancer treatment-related complications, primary glomerulonephritis post-ASCT, allergic interstitial nephritis from immune checkpoint inhibitor, and AKI from drug induced thrombotic microangiopathy are featured in this section because they require a high index of suspicion and awareness to properly diagnose and manage. A common thread in all the scenarios presented is the multidisciplinary nature of onconephrology and the need for the nephrologist to have some fluency with how our hematology and oncology colleagues approach decision-making. This is especially important for the patient diagnosed with MGRS.
