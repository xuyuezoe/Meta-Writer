# Real-world evaluation of the efficacy and safety of disitamab vedotin (RC48) in urothelial carcinoma.

## Abstract

Background: Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is an aggressive malignancy with limited options for patients unresponsive to chemotherapy or immunotherapy. Disitamab vedotin (RC48), a HER2-targeted antibody-drug conjugate(ADC), has shown promising results in clinical trials, but real-world data are lacking.

Methods: We conducted a single-center retrospective study of patients with histologically confirmed UC who received RC48 alone or in combination with PD-1 inhibitors between August 2022 and June 2024. Key inclusion criteria were at least one measurable lesion and adequate organ function; patients with incomplete records, concurrent active malignancies, or severe organ dysfunction precluding systemic therapy were excluded.

Results: A total of 71 patients were included. The ORR was 69.0%, including 15 complete and 34 partial responses. The median PFS was 24 months, and OS data were immature at cutoff. In subgroup analyses, patients receiving RC48 + PD-1 inhibitors achieved an ORR of 70.0% versus 63.6% with RC48 monotherapy. Grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events occurred in 21.1% of patients, most commonly anemia and decreased appetite; events were generally manageable with supportive care and dose modification.

Conclusions: RC48 showed encouraging activity and manageable safety in a real-world UC cohort. Prospective multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings, compare monotherapy versus combination strategies, and define the optimal role of RC48 across UC disease stages.

## Introduction

Introduction

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is one of the most common malignancies of the urinary system, primarily arising in the bladder (accounting for approximately 90% of cases), ureters, and renal pelvis. Globally, UC is associated with a high incidence and mortality rate, with a significantly higher prevalence observed in men compared to women [ 1 ]. It remains a leading cause of cancer-related death among urologic malignancies [ 2 ]. With the ongoing impact of population aging and persistent exposure to risk factors such as smoking, the incidence of UC continues to rise, and its treatment remains a major clinical challenge [ 3 ].

Data indicate that 20% to 30% of newly diagnosed UC cases present with metastatic disease at initial diagnosis, and the 5-year overall survival rate for metastatic UC remains below 5%[ 4 ]. For patients with locally advanced or metastatic UC (la/mUC), platinum-based chemotherapy remains the standard first-line treatment. However, approximately 50% of patients are unable to benefit due to impaired renal function, poor treatment tolerance, or primary resistance [ 5 ]. In recent years, the introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has provided a new option for some patients, but their objective response rate (ORR) is only about 20%−30%, and most patients will eventually progress [ 6 ]. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to develop more effective therapeutic strategies, particularly for patients who have failed both chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

The emergence of antibody-drug conjugates (ADC) has brought transformative progress to the treatment landscape of UC[ 7 ]. Disitamab vedotin (RC48) is the first domestically developed and approved ADC for UC in China. It consists of a humanized anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody, a cleavable linker, and the cytotoxic payload monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), enabling selective delivery to HER2-overexpressing tumor cells [ 8 , 9 ]. Early clinical trials, such as RC48-C005 and RC48-C009, reported overall ORR of 51.2% and 50.0%, respectively, and a disease control rate (DCR) of 76.6%. Importantly, RC48 was associated with a lower incidence of treatment-related adverse events compared to conventional chemotherapy, highlighting its favorable safety profile and promising antitumor activity [ 10 , 11 ]. Preliminary data also suggest potential benefit when combined with PD-1 inhibitors [ 12 ].

However, current evidence is largely derived from highly selected clinical trial populations, which may not fully capture the heterogeneity of treatment responses and safety profiles in real-world settings. For example, elderly patients, those with renal insufficiency, and those who have failed multiple lines of therapy are underrepresented in clinical trials, and patients with low HER2 expression (IHC 1+) or even negative (IHC 0) may also benefit from RC48 treatment.

Based on this context, the present study aims to systematically evaluate the real-world efficacy(ORR, PFS, OS) and safety of RC48 in patients with UC. Furthermore, we sought to explore its potential synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors. The findings are expected to provide practice-oriented evidence for optimizing individualized treatment strategies and to support the broader integration of ADC-based combination therapies in the management of urothelial carcinoma.

## Materials and methods

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This was a single-center, retrospective real-world study conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Huzhou University. We adopted a broad inclusion approach enrolling patients with histologically confirmed UC treated with RC48 alone or with PD-1 inhibitors between August 2022 and June 2024.

Data collected included: age, sex, tumor differentiation, primary tumor site, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), prior immunotherapy history, site(s) of metastasis, presence of visceral metastases, surgical history, HER2 expression status, treatment regimen, and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). All data were anonymized before analysis. For patients with La/mUC, RC48-based systemic therapy was the primary treatment modality in this cohort. No surgical management (e.g., radical cystectomy or metastasectomy) was performed after RC48 initiation, although some patients had prior surgical interventions (e.g., radical cystectomy or TURBT). The inclusion of NMIUC and non-metastatic MIUC cases reflects our institutional treatment policy, in which RC48 may be offered to patients with high-risk NMIUC or MIUC who are ineligible for radical surgery or standard perioperative chemotherapy due to comorbidities, patient preference, or prior treatment failure.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed UC, with or without variant histology; (2) treatment with RC48, either as monotherapy or in combination with immunotherapy or chemotherapy; (3) at least one measurable response assessment; and (4) adequate baseline hematologic, hepatic, and cardiac function. Exclusion criteria included incomplete medical records, concurrent active malignancies, and severe organ dysfunction precluding systemic therapy.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided written informed consent prior to treatment initiation. Routine evaluations, including CT scans, bone scans, and MRI of both primary and metastatic lesions, were performed every 6 weeks before and during treatment. Before each treatment cycle, liver and kidney function, blood routine, biochemistry, coagulation function, tumor markers and electrocardiogram were reviewed. Additional imaging or diagnostic assessments were performed at the discretion of the treating physician based on the patient’s clinical condition.

Process of follow-up

From the initiation of RC48 monotherapy, patients were followed through medical chart review, outpatient visits, and telephone contact, until one of the following occurred: death, disease progression, intolerable treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), or voluntary treatment discontinuation. The follow-up period continued until December 31, 2024.

RC48 was administered as an intravenous infusion at a dose of 2.0 mg/kg every two weeks, with treatment discontinued or dosing intervals extended in cases of severe adverse reactions or intolerance. For combination regimens, a PD-1 inhibitor (toripalimab 240 mg or tislelizumab 200 mg) was administered concurrently once per cycle. The decision to administer RC48 plus a PD-1 inhibitor versus RC48 monotherapy followed institutional practice and physician judgment, considering tumor burden, pace of disease, comorbidities, and patient preference. The primary endpoint was ORR. Secondary endpoints included PFS, OS, and safety (treatment-related adverse events [TRAEs]). The median number of RC48 cycles administered was 8 (1–16). Following RC48 discontinuation, subsequent treatments included immunotherapy ( n = 10), chemotherapy ( n = 18), best supportive care ( n = 36), and others ( n = 7).HER2 expression was primarily assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and interpreted according to the HER2 scoring criteria established for breast cancer.

Outcomes and assessments

Antitumor response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. Adverse events were monitored and graded based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from initiation of RC48-based treatment to either radiographic disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the start of RC48-based therapy to death from any cause. Tumor responses were categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). The objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR. For NMIBC patients, all had undergone prior TURBT for diagnosis and local control. In these cases, treatment response was primarily evaluated by cystoscopic examination and urinary cytology, supplemented by imaging (CT/MRI) where measurable lesions were present, and RECIST v1.1 criteria were applied to radiographically measurable disease.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.0. Descriptive statistics, including percentages, means, and medians, were used to summarize patient baseline characteristics and adverse events. The Clopper-Pearson method was used to calculate the ORR and its 95% confidence interval (CI). Time-to-event endpoints, such as PFS and OS, were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. A p -value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 71 patients with UC who received RC48 treatment were included in this study. Demographic and baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1 . The median age was 71 years (40–90), and 80.3% ( n = 57) were male, which aligns with the known epidemiological trend of UC predominantly affecting elderly males. Regarding performance status, ECOG scores were 0 in 43.7%, 1 in 42.3%, and ≥ 2 in 14.1%, indicating that most patients had preserved functional capacity. The median body mass index (BMI) was 22.2 kg/m2 (14.1–33.1), and the median serum creatinine level was 91.0 μmol/L. An estimated 78.87% of patients had a baseline creatinine clearance rate (CCr) ≥ 60 mL/min, suggesting that renal function was generally well-preserved in this cohort. Demographics and clinical characteristics of patients with urothelial carcinoma receiving RC48 based therapy

With respect to disease stage, non-muscle-invasive urinary cancer (NMIUC) accounted for 7.0%, muscle-invasive urinary cancer (MIUC) for 74.7%, and locally advanced or metastatic UC (la/mUC) for 18.3%. The bladder was the most common primary tumor site (77.5%), followed by the renal pelvis or ureter (22.5%). Variant histology, including glandular and squamous differentiation, was uncommon, reported in 4.2% and 5.6%, respectively. HER2 expression was distributed as follows: IHC 0 in 2.8%, 1 + in 7.0%, 2 + in 52.1%, 3 + in 19.7%, and unknown in 18.3% of patients. A total of 49.3% had a history of transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT), while 35.2% had undergone radical surgery.

Regarding prior treatment, 42.3% received RC48 as first-line therapy, and 57.8% as second-line or beyond. The majority of patients (84.5%) received RC48 in combination with PD-1 inhibitors, whereas 15.5% received RC48 monotherapy. As for metastatic status, 80.3% of patients had no detectable distant metastases at baseline. A minority of patients had metastases to the liver, bone, lymph nodes, pelvis, or other sites, each accounting for ≤ 4.2%. These baseline characteristics provide a critical foundation for further subgroup analyses evaluating the differential efficacy of RC48 across clinically relevant patient populations.

Efficacy

In this study, RC48 demonstrated notable antitumor activity in patients with UC(Fig. 1 ). Among the evaluable population, 15 patients achieved complete response (CR) and 34 achieved partial response (PR), while only 5 patients experienced disease progression (PD) during follow-up, resulting in an overall ORR of 69.0% (49/71). Subgroup analyses revealed differences in ORR based on age. Patients aged < 70 years achieved an ORR of 76.5% (26/34), compared to 62.2% (23/37) in those aged ≥ 70 years. Similarly, treatment outcomes varied according to ECOG performance status (PS): patients with ECOG 0–1 showed a significantly higher ORR (72.1%, 44/61) than those with ECOG ≥ 2 (50.0%, 5/10), indicating better responses among patients with preserved functional status. When comparing treatment strategies, patients receiving RC48 plus PD-1 inhibitors achieved an ORR of 70.0% (42/60), whereas those treated with RC48 monotherapy had an ORR of 63.6% (7/11). Given the small number of monotherapy cases, these results should be interpreted with caution. Renal function also appeared to influence treatment response. Patients with a creatinine clearance (CCr) ≥ 60 mL/min had an ORR of 71.4% (40/56), while those with CCr 30–60 mL/min had an ORR of 69.2% (9/13). Notably, patients with severe renal impairment (CCr < 30 mL/min) exhibited no objective responses (0/2), suggesting that significantly impaired renal function may compromise the efficacy of RC48.

The median follow-up duration was 11.0 months (range, 1–28) (Fig. 2 ). As of the data cutoff, 18 patients (25.4%) had died. Although the median OS had not yet been reached, the median PFS was 24 months. The median follow-up time was 11.0 months, and therefore these survival estimates should be interpreted with caution when compared to metastatic-only trial populations. Importantly, patients with high HER2 expression (IHC 2+/3+) experienced significantly longer PFS compared to those with low HER2 expression (IHC 0/1+) ( P = 0.02) (Fig. 3 ), supporting the hypothesis that HER2 expression may serve as a predictive biomarker for RC48 efficacy in UC. Safety

In this study, all 71 patients with UC who received RC48 monotherapy or in combination with immunotherapy underwent systematic monitoring and assessment of treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). The incidence of Grade ≥ 3 events was relatively low, indicating that RC48 was generally well tolerated. ## Discussion

Discussion

This study evaluated the real-world efficacy and safety of RC48 in patients with UC, both as monotherapy and in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors.

In terms of efficacy, the ORR reached 69.0%, and the median PFS was 24 months(Fig. 2 )—a notable improvement compared to traditional chemotherapy regimens, which typically achieve an ORR of approximately 40% and a median PFS of 6–8 months [ 13 ]. In previous trials, RC48 achieved an ORR of 50.0% in la/mUC patients in RC48-C009, a complete response rate of 66.7% in high-risk NMIBC in TRUCE-04[ 14 ], and a pathological complete response (pCR) rate of 62.1% in MIBC in the neoadjuvant setting in RC48-C017[ 15 ]. Our finding are generally consistent with the conclusions of previous studies. While our observed results appear favorable, direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to substantial differences in patient populations, including the inclusion of NMIBC and non-metastatic MIBC cases in our cohort.

Subgroup analysis revealed that patients with HER2-positive tumors (IHC 2+/3+) had significantly longer PFS compared to those with HER2-low expression ( P = 0.02), which is consistent with findings from recent studies [ 16 ]. Furthermore, recent preclinical studies suggest that RC48 can elicit a bystander killing effect, enabling antitumor activity even in tumors with low HER2 expression [ 17 ]. This may help explain the clinical benefit observed in the 18.3% of patients with unknown HER2 status in our study. Taken together, these findings support the routine use of standardized HER2 testing in clinical practice. However, low HER2 expression should not be considered an absolute exclusion criterion for RC48 treatment, given its potential efficacy beyond strictly HER2-high populations.

It is noteworthy that although this study did not restrict inclusion based on PD-L1 expression, 84.5% of patients received RC48 in combination with PD-1 inhibitors, reflecting a growing real-world trend toward immunotherapy-based combination regimens [ 13 ]. In this cohort, RC48 combined with PD-1 inhibitors (such as tislelizumab or toripalimab) demonstrated favorable clinical efficacy, although the mechanisms underlying this synergy remain to be fully elucidated. Preliminary mechanistic studies suggest that ADC can induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby promoting T-cell infiltration and enhancing antitumor immune responses [ 18 ]. These findings provide a rationale for the combination of ADC with immune checkpoint inhibitors, supporting further clinical and translational investigations into this synergistic approach.

The variation in RC48 efficacy across different clinical subgroups holds important implications for individualized treatment strategies. Notably, although patients with an ECOG performance status (PS) ≥ 2 exhibited a lower ORR (50.0%) compared to those with ECOG 0–1, the response rate still exceeded historical outcomes associated with conventional chemotherapy in this frail population [ 19 ]. While retrospective real-world cohorts allow inclusion of such patients who are often underrepresented in clinical trials, this heterogeneity is a double-edged sword—enhancing generalizability but also introducing risks of confounding and bias. To mitigate this, we performed subgroup and stratified analyses to better account for population heterogeneity.

However, severely impaired renal function (CCr < 30 mL/min) appeared to negatively affect treatment efficacy, with no objective responses observed in this subgroup [ 20 , 21 ]. This may be attributed to the accumulation of MMAE (monomethyl auristatin E) metabolites, which are primarily eliminated via the kidneys, as well as the limited number of cases, which could introduce statistical uncertainty [ 13 , 19 ]. Therefore, caution is warranted when considering RC48 in patients with CCr < 30 mL/min, and a risk–benefit assessment should be conducted carefully. Dose modification, such as reducing the dose to 1.5 mg/kg, may be considered in select cases. Encouragingly, RC48 maintained robust efficacy in elderly patients (≥ 70 years), those with mild to moderate renal impairment (CCr 30–60 mL/min), and in heavily pretreated populations, underscoring its practical value in complex real-world patient cohorts [ 12 , 13 ].

The safety analysis demonstrated that the adverse event (AE) profile of RC48 in the real-world setting was generally consistent with previous reports. However, the incidence of Grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) was 21.1%, which appears to be lower than the 30.0% reported in clinical trials. This reduction may reflect the growing clinical expertise in managing ADC-related toxicities in routine practice.

Peripheral neuropathy (PN) remains a key toxicity of concern, occurring in 35.2% of patients, with the majority (80.0%) being Grade 1–2. This is consistent with the MMAE-related neurotoxicity profile previously reported in clinical studies [ 22 , 23 ]. Supportive measures such as vitamin B supplementation and neurotrophic agents may provide symptom relief, while neuropathic pain can be managed with gabapentin in combination with physical therapy, with referral to neurology specialists if necessary. For patients developing ≥ Grade 2 PN, treatment should be withheld until symptoms improve to ≤ Grade 1, after which therapy may be resumed at a reduced dose or with extended dosing intervals. Other common AEs, including nausea (40.8%) and anemia (28.2%), were generally well managed with supportive measures, such as prophylactic antiemetics and the use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) where appropriate [ 24 ].

Despite the encouraging findings, this study has several notable limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, it is subject to potential selection bias and information bias. Second, HER2 expression status was unknown in 18.3% of patients, which may limit the accuracy of efficacy stratification based on biomarker expression. Third, the median follow-up duration was relatively short, and OS data remain immature, requiring longer follow-up to confirm long-term clinical benefit. Additionally, there was an imbalance in sample size between the combination therapy group and the monotherapy group, which limits the ability to perform direct comparative analyses between treatment strategies. Our treatment allocation reflects institutional practice, physician judgment, and patient preference. This real-world decision-making may correlate with baseline risk and thus limits generalizability, particularly regarding indirect comparisons between monotherapy and combination therapy.

## Conclusion

Conclusion

In this real-world study, RC48 demonstrated encouraging efficacy and manageable safety in patients with UC. Clinical benefit was observed in both HER2-high and selected HER2-low or unknown cases. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously in view of the single-center retrospective design, relatively short follow-up, and the imbalance between monotherapy and combination therapy groups. Importantly, the unusually long PFS observed in our cohort partly reflects the inclusion of earlier-stage patients, limiting direct comparability with metastatic-only trial populations. Future multicenter prospective studies with longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings, clarify the relative efficacy of monotherapy versus combination regimens, and determine the optimal role of RC48 in different UC disease stages.
