# Human Placenta-Derived Cells (PDA-002) in Diabetic Foot Ulcer Patients With and Without Peripheral Artery Disease: A Phase 2 Multi-Center, Randomised, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Trial.

## Abstract

The management of diabetic foot ulcers in patients with peripheral artery disease remains challenging. Human placenta-derived cells (PDA-002), a mesenchymal stromal cell-like population obtained from full-term placental tissue, possess angiogenic and tissue regenerative properties. Participants were stratified based on peripheral artery disease status. A total of 159 individuals were randomly assigned to receive intramuscular PDA-002 at one of three doses (3 × 10^6, 10 × 10^6 and 30 × 10^6 cells) or a placebo. This Phase 2 multi-center, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluated adults with chronic diabetic foot ulcers with and without peripheral artery disease. The primary efficacy endpoint was the proportion of participants achieving complete wound closure of the index ulcer within 3 months, with sustained closure maintained for an additional 4 weeks. PDA-002 was well-tolerated, with no treatment-related serious adverse events. Intramuscular PDA-002 treatment achieved the highest efficacy at the 3 × 10^6 cell dose within the peripheral artery disease subgroup (38.5% vs. 22.6% for placebo), meeting a stringent 4-week durability endpoint that surpassed the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's recommended 2-week sustainability criterion. PDA-002 shows promise as a breakthrough treatment for diabetic foot ulcers and peripheral artery disease, demonstrating efficacy with two intramuscular doses and no re-treatment. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT # 02264288.

## Section 1

Summary Intramuscular administration of human placenta‐derived cells (PDA‐002) resulted in clinically meaningful and durable wound closure in patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) complicated by peripheral artery disease (PAD), a population with limited non‐surgical therapeutic options. Two low‐dose (3 × 10 6 cells) intramuscular injections of PDA‐002 achieved a 38.5% complete ulcer closure rate versus 22.6% with placebo, with healing sustained for an additional 4 weeks. Treatment with PDA‐002 was associated with improved ulcer severity (Wagner grade), enhanced vascular status (Rutherford classification), and a reduced incidence of de novo gangrene and infection. PDA‐002 was well tolerated, with no treatment‐related serious adverse events and a long‐term safety profile comparable to placebo through 24 months of follow‐up.

## Introduction

Introduction

The prevalence of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) in the United States is a growing public health concern, primarily due to the increasing incidence of diabetes. Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is a key contributing factor to DFUs, as it impairs blood flow, delays wound healing, and significantly increases the risk of infection and amputation. Each year, approximately 2 million individuals in the United States develop DFUs [ 1 , 2 ], with PAD present in nearly 50% of these cases [ 3 , 4 ]. The 5‐year mortality rate among DFU patients ranges from 30% to 70% [ 5 , 6 ], and approximately 20% of individuals with DFUs ultimately require amputation [ 5 , 6 ].

The cost of treating a single DFU varies substantially, ranging from $3000 to $30 000 [ 7 ], contributing to an estimated total annual economic burden of over $9 billion [ 6 , 8 ] in the United States. The primary objective of DFU management is early wound closure to accelerate wound healing. However, patients with PAD‐complicated DFUs face greater healing challenges than those without PAD.

Current treatment approaches primarily focus on medical management and surgical interventions, including endovascular and open procedures to restore blood flow. Although the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved five therapies for DFUs, no treatments are currently approved for DFUs associated with PAD, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches. Emerging research highlights stem cell therapy [ 9 , 10 ] as a promising avenue for wound healing, with multiple studies suggesting that mesenchymal stem cells promote angiogenesis, accelerate re‐epithelialisation, reduce inflammation and enhance DFU repair [ 11 , 12 ].

Within the broader field of regenerative medicine, stem cell–based therapies are emerging as a promising strategy for diabetic foot ulcers. PDA‐002, a placenta‐derived mesenchymal stromal‐like cell product, represents a novel approach distinguished by its potent angiogenic, immunomodulatory, and wound‐repair properties, offering potential advantages over other investigational stem cell therapies.

Human placenta‐derived cells (PDA‐002) are cellular immunomodulatory agents with therapeutic potential. PDA‐002 is a mesenchymal stromal‐like cell population derived from healthy, full‐term human placental tissues. PDA‐002 cells are culture‐expanded as a plastic‐adherent, undifferentiated, in vitro cell population that expresses the nominal phenotypes [ 13 , 14 ] CD34−, CD90+, CD105+ and CD200+. PDA‐002 cells constitutively express moderate levels of human leukocyte antigen Class I while exhibiting undetectable levels of human leukocyte antigen Class II [ 14 ]. The potential therapeutic effects of PDA‐002 on neurovascular cellular components [ 15 ] have been evaluated in various in vitro and in vivo experimental models.

Given its potential to modulate immune responses and enhance angiogenesis [ 15 ], PDA‐002 was initially assessed in a Phase 1 safety study (PDA‐002‐DFU‐001) [ 16 ]. This study demonstrated promising efficacy in promoting healing and closure of index ulcers in patients unresponsive to conventional therapy.

Based on encouraging Phase 1 findings ( ClinicalTrials.gov NCT 01859117 ), this Phase 2 trial proposed that escalating doses of PDA‐002 would achieve enhanced therapeutic benefit in promoting healing among patients with DFU and PAD compared to standard treatment.

## Methods

Methods

Study Design

This Phase 2, randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, multi‐center trial was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of PDA‐002 in patients with DFU. A total of 35 clinical sites across the United States were enrolled and randomised in this study. The Institutional Review Board (approval number 201501390) approved the study protocol before initiation. The trial was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines outlined in the International Conference on Harmonisation Guideline E6. All patients provided written informed consent. The trial has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov . The first visit of the first patient occurred on October 23, 2014, and the final visit for the last patient was completed on February 27, 2018. The study was terminated by the sponsor on February 28, 2018, due to business considerations. At the time of study closure, all patients had either completed treatment with at least 6 months of follow‐up or had undergone an early termination visit.

Study Population

Adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus with a haemoglobin A1c level ≤ 12%, with Grade 1 (full thickness only) or Grade 2 ulcers on the Wagner grading scale of greater than 1 month's duration, which had not adequately responded to conventional ulcer therapy, were included in the study. Most patients had type 2 diabetes mellitus (89.4% across all PAD status; 97.8% in the placebo group), whereas a smaller proportion had type 1 diabetes (9.7% across all PAD status; 2.2% in the placebo group). The ulcer size was required to be at least 1 cm 2 , except if it was present on the toe, and the maximum lesion size of the index ulcer was ≤ 10 cm 2 . In cases of bilateral limb ulcers, the largest qualifying ulcer was designated as the index ulcer for treatment. Patients were stratified into two groups: those with PAD and those without PAD. PAD was defined based on specific laboratory criteria (ankle‐brachial index [ABI] between 0.4 and 0.8, toe‐brachial index [TBI] between 0.30 and 0.65, or transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2) ≤ 40 mmHg). The main exclusion criteria at screening included active infection (including osteomyelitis), reduction of wound ≥ 30% during the screening period, pain at rest due to limb ischemia, abnormal liver function tests (aspartate aminotransferase or alanine aminotransferase > 2.5 times the upper limit of normal), uncontrolled diabetes (haemoglobin A1c level > 12%), uncontrolled hypertension (diastolic blood pressure > 100 mmHg or systolic blood pressure > 180 mmHg), suspected hypersensitivity to any product components, and history of malignancy within 5 years of signing the informed consent. Pregnant or lactating women, as well as women of childbearing potential not using adequate contraception, were also excluded.

Randomisation and Blinding

Three dose levels of PDA‐002 (3 × 10 6 , 10 × 10 6 and 30 × 10 6 cells) and placebo were evaluated using a 2:2:1:2 double‐blind randomisation approach, with a lower proportion of patients assigned to the highest active dose. Treatment assignment at baseline was performed using an interactive voice or web response system. All patients, investigators, study personnel and sponsor personnel were blinded to the randomisation. The investigators were responsible for all wound assessments.

Treatment Administration

Eligible randomised patients received one of four treatments on days 1 and 8: 3 × 10 6 PDA‐002 cells, 10 × 10 6 PDA‐002 cells, 30 × 10 6 PDA‐002 cells or placebo. Under aseptic conditions, intramuscular injections were administered at 15 sites on the targeted limb, with an injection volume of 0.30 mL per injection site, using a 3‐mL syringe with a 20‐ to 22‐gauge, 1.5‐in. needle. The target regions for injection were below the knee (gastrocnemius muscle) and above the ankle (soleus muscle). Injections were at least 1 cm apart horizontally and vertically, at a depth of approximately 1–4 cm. Each patient received 15 injections in a distributed pattern of three to five injections around the leg, with careful avoidance of intravascular administration. The mean compliance with treatment was approximately 98% across all PAD status groups. Although all patients in the safety population received the day 1 dose, only five patients did not receive the second dose.

Study Procedure

All patients underwent a screening period of at least 2 weeks to determine study eligibility and ensure that the ulcer size did not change appreciably with standard treatment within 28 days of study day 1. Patients were randomised following the screening period. During the treatment period, they received intramuscular injections of PDA‐002 on study days 1 and 8. All patients who received any dose of PDA‐002 or placebo entered the 24‐month follow‐up period starting from day 9. Follow‐up visits were conducted on day 15 and months 1, 2 and 3, with the primary efficacy endpoint assessed at month 3. Long‐term follow‐up visits occurred at months 4, 5, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 24. After the early termination of the study, data analysis through month 6 was designated as the final analysis. An independent external data monitoring committee monitored the safety and efficacy of the data.

The index ulcer was evaluated and measured after debridement (if necessary). The index ulcers and all other ulcers, including bilateral ulcers, were graded using the Wagner grading scale. Target ulcers were documented using an imaging system (Aranz Medical [ 17 ]) at each visit. Additionally, all the limbs were assessed using the Rutherford Classification of Chronic Limb Ischemia (i.e., clinical descriptions of Rutherford criteria), and baseline diabetic neuropathy levels were evaluated using the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument.

In PAD patients, reduced arterial blood flow impairs oxygen and nutrient delivery, hindering tissue repair and exacerbating DFUs. PAD is a key factor in DFU progression, typically assessed via ABI, where values < 0.9 indicate ischemia. However, diabetes‐related arterial calcification can falsely elevate ABI, necessitating alternative assessments such as the TBI [ 18 , 19 ], with values < 0.7 indicating ischemia. Thus, PAD status in this study was determined using ABI or TBI. ABI and TBI were measured bilaterally, with ABI calculated by assessing both the posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis arteries, and TBI determined using the first or second toe digital artery. These values were obtained by dividing the systolic blood pressure at the ankle or toe by the systolic blood pressure in the arm, using the Doppler technique. Patients were also assessed for amputation status and underlying causes, including pain, gangrene, local or systemic infection and osteomyelitis. All patients were monitored for adverse events (AEs) during the study period. Assessments included monitoring any or all the following parameters: the patient's clinical symptoms; laboratory, pathological, radiological or surgical findings; physical examination findings; or findings from other tests and/or procedures. All the patients received standard medical care for DFU, including pressure off‐loading, wound care and debridement.

## Results

Results

Patient Disposition

A total of 341 patients with DFU were screened for study eligibility, of whom 182 patients (53.3%) did not meet the inclusion criteria. The remaining 159 patients, including those with and without PAD, were randomised. The IIT population comprised 99 patients with PAD and 60 patients without PAD. Within the PAD group, one patient was excluded from the EE population due to missing post‐baseline efficacy assessments, resulting in 98 evaluable patients. Among the 158 efficacy‐evaluable patients, 154 patients (96.9%) completed the study treatment, and 34 patients (21.4%) completed the full study (Figure 1 ). The most common reason for study discontinuation was sponsor termination on February 28, 2018, which occurred in 57.1% of PAD patients and 70.9% of non‐PAD patients who discontinued the study. The higher discontinuation rate among non‐PAD patients was due to their later enrollment, as they were included following Protocol Amendment 2.

Efficacy Evaluation

The primary efficacy endpoint was defined as complete ulcer closure within 3 months, sustained for at least 4 weeks. The highest response rate was 38.5% (95% CI) in the 3 × 10 6 PDA‐002 dose group, followed by 29.6% (95% CI: 13.8%–50.2%) in the 10 × 10 6 PDA‐002 dose group and 35.7% (95% CI: 12.8%–64.9%) in the 30 × 10 6 PDA‐002 dose group. Both active treatment groups exhibited higher response rates than the placebo group, which had a response rate of 22.6% (95% CI: 9.6%–41.1%) (Table 3 ). In PAD patients, the Kaplan–Meier analysis of the first complete wound closure over 6 months revealed a clear divergence between the placebo group and 3 × 10 6 PDA‐002 dose group, particularly between months 1 and 4. ## Discussion

Discussion

This Phase 2 randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, multicenter trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of PDA‐002 in patients with DFU. The findings revealed that PDA‐002 treatment notably improved response rates in patients with PAD compared to those without PAD, with the most pronounced treatment effect observed at the lowest active dose (3 × 10 6 PDA‐002 cells). The response rate of 38.5% in the treatment group, compared to 22.6% in the placebo group, demonstrates a substantial improvement, highlighting the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of PDA‐002 in PAD patients. The study presents novel and impactful findings, as the utility of human placenta‐derived cells for treating DFUs complicated by PAD has not been previously demonstrated in humans.

Although no significant differences were observed in ABI and TBI measurements, improvements in the Rutherford criteria suggest that PDA‐002 provided substantial benefits for PAD patients. This is evidenced by a nearly two‐fold increase in the responder rate, marked by improvement in at least one Rutherford scale category at month 3, with this benefit consistently sustained through month 6. The Wagner grading scale analysis further supports the efficacy of PDA‐002 in promoting ulcer healing among PAD patients. Although both treatment groups exhibited similar distributions of Grade 1 and Grade 2 ulcers at baseline, a notably higher proportion of patients in the PDA‐002 group with baseline Grade 1 ulcers achieved complete resolution by month 3 compared to the placebo group. This positive trend continued through month 6, with a greater proportion of PDA‐002‐treated patients achieving improved ulcer status. These findings highlight the sustained efficacy of PDA‐002 in facilitating ulcer resolution and improving clinical outcomes in this high‐risk population.

Foot wound infections were reported less frequently in the 3 × 10 6 PDA‐002 dose group compared to the placebo group, suggesting a potential protective effect of PDA‐002 in reducing infection risk among PAD patients. Although PDA‐002 treatment did not entirely eliminate the risk of major amputation, the observed cases were predominantly associated with poor off‐loading adherence and underlying comorbidities, such as uncontrolled diabetes, progressive peripheral neuropathy, and a history of prior amputation. Importantly, a marked reduction in de novo gangrene and index ulcer reopening in PDA‐002‐treated patients demonstrated its strong therapeutic potential to significantly mitigate these complications. Importantly, with only two intramuscular doses and no subsequent re‐treatment, the 3 × 10 6 PDA‐002 group achieved a 38.5% response rate compared to 22.6% with placebo. Although the difference did not reach conventional statistical significance ( p > 0.05), this outcome suggests a clinically meaningful benefit in PAD patients, supported by parallel improvements in vascular and wound‐healing measures. The lack of statistical significance most likely reflects the limited sample size rather than the absence of biological effect.

The results obtained with this more rigorous endpoint also demonstrate the ability of PDA‐002 to achieve durable ulcer closure beyond FDA criteria. According to the FDA guidelines for chronic cutaneous ulcers [ 20 ], complete wound closure is conventionally defined as skin re‐epithelialisation without drainage or dressing requirements, confirmed at two consecutive study visits separated by 2 weeks. This standard definition has served as the benchmark for all FDA‐approved DFU therapies and has been uniformly applied in clinical trials. In contrast, our study defined complete wound closure as index ulcer closure within 3 months of dosing, with sustained closure for an additional 4 weeks. Using this stringent definition, the 3 × 10 6 PDA‐002 group achieved a 38.5% response rate compared to 22.6% with placebo, underscoring its potential efficacy in PAD patients and representing a notable strength of this study.

Mesenchymal stem cells represent a promising therapeutic approach for DFUs complicated by PAD due to their regenerative and immunomodulatory properties. PDA cells contribute to this effect by downregulating key proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐18, MCP‐1, CCL3, CCL5, CXCL1 and CXCL10. Furthermore, PDA cells secrete essential mitogens and cytokines that drive angiogenesis, including HGF, VEGF, PDGF and FGF. These combined paracrine effects create an optimal environment that reduces inflammation, stimulates vascular growth, and promotes tissue regeneration, ultimately improving wound healing outcomes. Currently, besides revascularisation, no FDA‐approved medical treatment specifically addresses DFUs in patients with PAD, highlighting a critical unmet medical need [ 4 ]. PAD worsens DFUs by reducing tissue perfusion, impairing wound healing, and increasing the risk of chronic complications [ 21 , 22 ]. Although current treatment strategies for ischemic DFUs focus on blood flow restoration, wound management, and complication prevention, a significant gap remains in targeted therapies that address local wound‐healing deficits and vascular pathology. As placenta‐derived mesenchymal stromal‐like cells, PDA‐002 demonstrated clinically significant improvements in DFU patients with PAD compared with the placebo.

The two‐year long‐term safety analysis clearly demonstrates that PDA‐002 treatment exhibits a favourable safety profile and is well‐tolerated in DFU patients. Biodistribution studies confirm that PDA‐002 does not persist beyond 7 days in any tissue. In vivo hind limb ischemia models have demonstrated the efficacy of PDA cells in enhancing angiogenesis and tissue regeneration. Moreover, intramuscular administration of PDA‐002 reported promotes DFU closure via paracrine [ 14 ] modulation of the wound microenvironment. The inverse dose–response observed in this study, where the lowest PDA‐002 dose produced the greatest clinical benefit, is an intriguing finding. Higher doses may impair tissue distribution through increased viscosity or accelerate nutrient depletion and premature cell death, thereby shortening the duration of growth factor secretion. In contrast, lower doses may sustain paracrine signalling and cell viability, supporting angiogenesis and immune modulation [ 23 , 24 ]. Similar patterns have been described in preclinical MSC hind‐limb ischemia models, where lower doses optimised paracrine activity and angiogenic effects [ 25 , 26 ]. Although PDA‐002 has demonstrated angiogenic activity in preclinical ischemia models, the specific mechanisms underlying this inverse response remain incompletely understood. Together, these observations support the hypothesis that an optimal therapeutic window may exist in which lower cell doses maximise biological activity while avoiding the detrimental effects of higher concentrations.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was calculated based on an assumed 10% placebo response rate, whereas the observed rate was 22.6%. This difference reduced statistical power and may have contributed to wider confidence intervals and non‐significant p ‐values, even in the presence of clinically meaningful trends in the PAD subgroup at 3 months. Another limitation is that off‐loading was not enforced after treatment; variable adherence may have contributed to ulcer recurrence and affected assessments of closure durability. Standardised and objectively monitored off‐loading protocols will therefore be important in later‐phase studies. In addition, treatment effects in non‐PAD patients remain inconclusive due to small sample size and limited power, underscoring the need for larger cohorts in this subgroup. Finally, no adjustments for multiplicity were made across dose groups or endpoints, and missing data were primarily handled using LOCF. Phase 3 trials will incorporate pre‐specified multiplicity controls and more advanced methods for handling missing data.

In the absence of FDA‐approved targeted therapies that address DFUs complicated with PAD, these findings provide strong evidence supporting PDA‐002 as a transformative treatment option for this high‐risk population. This Phase 2 trial provides important clinical evidence supporting PDA‐002 in patients with PAD‐associated DFUs.
