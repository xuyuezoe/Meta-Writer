# Knowledge attitude and practice of pregnant women on postnatal depression in Henan Province China.

## Abstract

Postnatal depression is a common maternal mental health problem with substantial public health implications, yet limited attention has been given to pregnant women's perceptions and responses to it. There are few studies explore pregnant women's Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) towards postnatal depression. This study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of pregnant women regarding postnatal depression in Henan Province, China, between June and November 2023. A random sampling method was used to recruit eligible pregnant women attending antenatal care. This cross-sectional survey was conducted between June and November 2023 at the Henan Provincial People's Hospital among pregnant women. Demographic information and KAP scores were collected by a self-designed questionnaire. Descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and structural equation modeling (SEM) were conducted using R version 4.3.1. A total of 500 (95.97%) valid questionnaires were enrolled, with mean age of 30.78 ± 4.09 years. The mean KAP scores were 14.91 ± 2.385 (possible range: 0-20), 41.06 ± 4.160 (possible range: 11-55), 45.34 ± 7.309 (possible range: 13-65), respectively. The structural equation modeling results showed that residence and education, number of deliveries had direct effect on knowledge. Knowledge, education, and family relationships had direct effect on attitude. Knowledge, attitude, and experiencing mental illness, number of deliveries and education had direct effect on practice. Pregnant women had adequate knowledge, positive attitude, and suboptimal practice towards postnatal depression. The findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions, family engagement, and a focus on mental health experiences to enhance clinical practices in addressing postnatal depression among pregnant women.

## Introduction

Introduction

Globally, depression affects more than 264 million individuals of all ages, making it a leading cause of disability and a significant contributor to the overall global burden of disease 1 , 2 . Postnatal depression, often defined as depressive symptoms emerging within 6 weeks postpartum, is associated with adverse child outcomes 3 , 4 . During pregnancy, psychological symptoms such as anxiety, irritability, and mood lability are prevalent in two-thirds of women in the first and third trimesters 5 . Moreover, experiences of depression are common, with suicide identified as a leading cause of maternal deaths in the year following pregnancy 6 . Approximately 80% of women may encounter a complex of emotional difficulties after childbirth, and the estimated prevalence of postpartum depression is 7–19% 7 . In China specifically, a meta-analysis of 30 studies revealed a pooled prevalence of postpartum depression of 14.7%, with regional variations ranging from 10.9 to 18.8% across different provinces 8 . Recent studies conducted in urban and rural settings in China have reported prevalence rates ranging from 13.1 to 26.3%, with higher rates typically observed in economically underdeveloped regions and among women with lower education levels 9 , 10 . Researchers emphasize the under-diagnosis of the condition, suggesting a much higher actual occurrence. The substantial ramifications of postnatal depression extend beyond individual well-being, exerting influence not only on the physical and mental health of postpartum women but also potentially yielding profound effects on infant development and family dynamics 11 . Specifically, postnatal depression can disrupt mother-infant bonding, potentially leading to impaired cognitive, emotional, and behavioral development in children 12 . Within the family unit, it can strain spousal relationships, increase marital discord, and create an overall negative home environment 13 . Furthermore, women with postnatal depression often experience difficulties returning to work, decreased productivity, and economic hardship due to increased healthcare costs and potential job loss 14 . Their social functioning is equally compromised, with many experiencing social withdrawal, isolation, and a diminished capacity to maintain social relationships, which further exacerbates their depressive symptoms 15 . This underscores the urgent need to address postnatal depression as a significant public health problem worldwide.

Several risk factors have been identified as contributing to the development of postnatal depression. These factors can be broadly categorized as biological, psychological, and social determinants. Biological factors include hormonal fluctuations during the postpartum period, particularly rapid declines in estrogen and progesterone levels 16 , genetic predisposition 17 , and pre-existing medical conditions 18 . Psychological risk factors encompass a history of depression or anxiety, prenatal depression, stressful life events, and negative birth experiences 19 . Social determinants include inadequate social support, marital discord, financial strain, and cultural factors such as gender-based role expectations 20 . In China specifically, additional influential factors include the traditional “doing the month” (zuo yuezi) postpartum practices, pressure from the one-child policy era, intergenerational conflicts regarding childcare, and rapid socioeconomic transitions affecting family structures 21 . Understanding these multifaceted risk factors is crucial for developing targeted interventions and preventive strategies for postnatal depression.The Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) model posits that individual behaviors are influenced by both knowledge and attitude, particularly in the domain of public health, where the evaluation of behavioral practices often involves concurrent assessments of knowledge and risk perception, commonly conducted through KAP surveys, making this theory crucial for understanding health-related behaviors 22 – 24 .

Previous studies have examined knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding depression across various populations, with mixed findings. A systematic review of mental health literacy found that recognition of depression symptoms varies considerably, ranging from 30 to 70% across different cultural contexts 25 . In perinatal populations specifically, research from high-income countries suggests that while awareness about depression is improving, significant gaps in knowledge about treatment options persist 26 . Studies in Asian settings reveal generally lower awareness levels, with one study in Malaysia reporting that only 23% of pregnant women could correctly identify postnatal depression symptoms, and another in Hong Kong finding that despite moderate awareness (65.5%), only a small percentage of women would seek professional help for depressive symptoms 27 . These findings highlight the importance of examining KAP regarding depression in diverse cultural contexts, particularly in perinatal populations.

Given that pregnant women are in a distinct life stage, anticipating the arrival of a newborn, significant changes occur in both their bodies and lifestyles during this period. Furthermore, the psychological well-being of pregnant women holds potential implications for the development of the newborn and the overall family environment. Therefore, research in this direction can provide a deeper understanding of their cognitions and coping mechanisms concerning postnatal depression, contributing to the establishment of healthier parent-child relationships and family environments. It is noteworthy that, although prenatal depression serves as a robust predictor of postnatal depression, the prevalence of prenatal depression surpasses that of postnatal depression 28 . Yet, there is a dearth of KAP studies specifically targeting this unique group of pregnant women. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the KAP of pregnant women regarding postnatal depression.

## Methods

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted between June and November 2023 at the Henan Provincial People’s Hospital. Pregnant women who visited to the Department of obstetrics and gynecology were selected as subjects. The study inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pregnant women attending routine prenatal care visits; and (2) age ≥ 18 years at enrollment. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) inability to complete the questionnaire due to cognitive impairment, language barriers, or other limiting conditions; and (2) refusal to provide written informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethic Committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital [(2023)-(1-137)] on May 26, 2023, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Questionnaire introduction

The questionnaire design drew upon the “ Expert consensus on guidelines for the prevention and management of postnatal depression (based on obstetric and community physicians , 2014 edition) ” and relevant literature 29 . Subsequently, the questionnaire underwent revision based on feedback from three experts in the field of Obstetrics and gynecology, resulting in a minor reduction of 46 items. The revised questionnaire demonstrated robust internal consistency, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.945, indicating a high level of reliability among the survey questions. Construct validity was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure, and a KMO value of 0.829 was obtained, indicating good sampling adequacy for factor analysis.

The final questionnaire, presented in Chinese (a version that translated into English was attached as an Appendix ), encompassed four dimensions for information collection, totaling 41 items. Among these, basic information comprised 15 items. The knowledge dimension comprised 10 items, all with correct answers, scored as 2 for a correct response, 1 for an unclear response, and 0 for an incorrect response, resulting in a potential score range of 0 to 20 points. The attitude and practice dimensions primarily utilized a five-point Likert scale, gauging the degree of positivity from very positive (5 points) to very negative (1 point). The attitude dimension consisted of 11 items, with specific scoring rules for items 7/8/9/10/11 and the remaining items, resulting in a possible score range of 11 to 55 points. The practice dimension comprised 7 items, with item 1 consisting of 7 subitems. All items and subitems employed a five-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 5 to 1, contributing to a potential overall score range of 13 to 65 points. Attaining scores exceeding 70% of the maximum in each section denoted adequate knowledge, positive attitude, and proactive practice 30 .

Questionnaire distribution and quality control

We applied the following criteria to filter out specific questionnaires: three instances where the response time was less than 90 s; one questionnaire indicating an incorrect age; three questionnaires with inaccurate weight entries; one questionnaire reporting an unusually high number of pregnancies; two questionnaires where the number of births exceeded the stated number of pregnancies; one questionnaire lacking information on insurance status and one including insurance information; and ten questionnaires lacking pregnancy-related complications but indicating the presence of other health conditions.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using the formula for estimating a population proportion in cross-sectional studies:

Based on this calculation, the minimum sample size required was 384. To account for an estimated 80% response rate, the final target sample size was adjusted to 480.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R 4.3.1. Continuous variables were described using mean ± standard deviation (SD), and between-group comparisons were performed using t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were presented as n (%). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to assess the correlations between knowledge, attitude, and practice scores. Structural equation modeling (SEM)-path analysis is employed to explore the path relationships between KAP and demographic information based on the following hypothesis: (1) Residence, education, and birth have effect on knowledge. (2) Knowledge, education, and family relationships have effect on attitude. (3) Knowledge, attitude, birth, prenatal education, and mental illness have effect on practice. Statistically significant variables in the basic information were included in the path analysis model, and the basic information variables that were not statistically significant in the first round of model (unadjusted) were eliminated, then, the adjusted path analysis model was obtained. Mediation analysis was performed based on the adjusted path analysis model. Two-sided p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in this study.

## Results

Results

Demographic information on participants

Initially, 521 questionnaires were collected, and subsequently, 500 questionnaires were retained after data quality control, yielding a validity rate of 95.97%. Participants’ mean age was 30.78 ± 4.09 years, mean BMI was 24.92 ± 3.76 kg/m 2 and mean pregnancy week was 26.34 ± 9.74 weeks. 225 (45.00%) had one pregnancy, 247 (49.40%) had not delivered, 191 (38.20%) had a history of abortion. 269 (53.80%) had a very good family relationship/atmosphere and 171 (34.20%) had received prenatal education. The mean score of knowledge, attitude, and practice were 14.91 ± 2.385 (possible range: 0–20), 41.06 ± 4.160 (possible range: 11–55), 45.34 ± 7.309 (possible range: 13–65), respectively. Knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were more likely higher among the pregnant women that lived in urban (all of P < 0.001), postgraduate or above (all of P < 0.001), employed ( P = 0.001, P < 0.001, and P = 0.008), with monthly family income more than 10,000 Yuan ( P < 0.001, P = 0.001, and P < 0.001), and never delivered ( P = 0.003, P = 0.016, and P = 0.003). Concurrently, those with health insurance were more likely to have higher knowledge and practice scores ( P = 0.011 and P = 0.002), those without mental illness were more likely to have higher practice scores ( P = 0.029), and those with underlying disease were more likely to have higher attitude scores ( P = 0.011). Furthermore, those with good family relationship/atmosphere were more likely to have higher attitude and practice scores (both of P < 0.001) (Table 1 ).

Knowledge

The distribution of knowledge dimensions revealed/shown that the two questions with the highest number of participants choosing the “Correct” option were “Even transient and mild depressive symptoms should be taken very seriously.” (K8) with 94.80% and “After childbirth, estrogen levels drop rapidly, causing changes in mood and behavior. Therefore, postpartum emotional changes are not entirely within individual control.” (K7) with 86.40%. On the other hand, the three questions with the highest number of participants choosing the “Unclear” option were “The most direct impact of postnatal depression is a reduction in breast milk secretion.” (K9) with 42.00% and “Postnatal depression is a common postpartum mental syndrome that occurs within the first 4 weeks after childbirth.” (K1) with 34.40% (Table S1 ).

Attitude

Approximately 65.00% and 59.60% of respondents vehemently disagreed with the notion that postpartum depression reflects emotional weakness (A1) and that it is an insignificant issue (A2), respectively. Additionally, 55.20% concurred that there is no necessity to excessively fret about postnatal depression, implying that adhering to their responsibilities would suffice (A7). Over 70% of pregnant women expressed varying degrees of confidence in restoring both their work (A8) and life (A9) to normalcy. Notably, a substantial 87.8% recognized the importance of depression screening for pregnant women (A9). Furthermore, an overwhelming 97.50% articulated a need for emotional support and assistance from their family members (A10) (Fig. S1 ).

Practice

When it comes to related practices, 51.00% were very willing to encourage their friends and relatives to receive treatment if they suffer from postpartum depression (P7). Also, more than 70% of the participants had a very great or greater willingness to record good events (P1.3) and to communicate and spend more time with their family (P1.7). In addition, 60.80% and 56.20% had a neutral practice towards taking vitamin B (P1.1) and vitamin D (P1.2), respectively. It is worth noting that the practice chosen by the largest proportion (25.20%) with only a very small willingness were taking medication with minimal side effects and closely monitoring drug concentrations and their impact on breast milk (P6) (Fig. S2 ).

## Discussion

Discussion

Pregnant women had adequate knowledge, positive attitude, and suboptimal practice towards postnatal depression. The findings underscore the significance of tailored educational interventions, family engagement, and addressing mental health experiences to advance clinical practices in addressing postnatal depression among pregnant women. By illuminating the complexities of pregnant women’s perceptions and behaviors regarding postnatal depression, this study aims to make a substantial contribution to the current knowledge base and offer practical insights for healthcare professionals. The findings are expected to inform targeted interventions, improve maternal mental health outcomes, and promote a comprehensive approach to postnatal care.

The study’s main findings illuminate the complex interplay of demographic and health-related factors affecting pregnant women’s knowledge, attitude, and practice towards postnatal depression. The positive correlation between knowledge, attitude, and practice aligns with existing literature emphasizing the interconnected nature of these constructs 31 . Disparities between urban and non-urban residents resonate with previous research indicating variations in mental health outcomes based on geographical location 32 . Education emerged as pivotal, with postgraduate women exhibiting higher scores, consistent with studies emphasizing the role of education in maternal mental health 33 . In light of the disparities between urban and non-urban residents, community-based interventions and health outreach programs for non-urban areas are imperative. These initiatives should increase awareness, dismantle geographical barriers to healthcare access, and foster a supportive environment for pregnant women. The mediation analysis further underscores the complex influences of demographic factors such as residence and education on knowledge and practice, which in turn suggest targeted interventions should also consider these nuances in their design.

When comparing our findings with previous studies investigating knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding postnatal depression, several similarities and differences emerge. Our findings of adequate knowledge and positive attitudes align with studies conducted in high-income countries such as Australia and the UK, where research has reported that approximately 70% of women demonstrated satisfactory knowledge about perinatal depression symptoms 34 . However, our observation of suboptimal practices contrasts with some Western studies, suggesting potential cultural differences in help-seeking behaviors. Studies from other Asian countries reveal context-specific variations; research in Malaysia reported lower knowledge levels (45%) than our sample 35 , while a study from Japan showed similar knowledge levels but less positive attitudes toward treatment, with greater emphasis on self-management 36 . These comparisons highlight the influence of cultural context on perinatal mental health literacy and emphasize the importance of culturally tailored interventions that address not only knowledge gaps but also cultural beliefs that may impact attitudes and practices.

Notably, the unexpected negative influence of education on practice prompts further investigation. This contrasts with conventional wisdom and studies indicating a positive impact of education on health outcomes 37 . The unexpected negative influence of education on practice observed in the study may be attributed to the intricate interplay of socioeconomic and cultural factors. Higher education levels, while typically associated with improved access to information and resources, may paradoxically heighten awareness of the challenges linked to postnatal depression. This heightened awareness could lead to increased stress and anxiety, potentially impacting individuals’ perceived ability to effectively cope with these challenges. Additionally, the influence of cultural perspectives cannot be overlooked, as higher education might expose individuals to a broader range of cultural norms and values, influencing their interpretations and responses to postnatal depression. Previous study supports the idea that higher education can contribute to increased stress, emphasizing the need for a nuanced exploration of these potential mechanisms to unravel the complexities underlying the observed negative relationship between education and postnatal depression practices 38 . The mediation analysis reveals that while education has a direct negative effect on practice, it also has a significant direct and indirect positive effect on attitudes, suggesting that educational strategies might need to address these contradictory impacts more explicitly.

In comparison to previous studies, the positive impact of family relationship quality on attitude and practice scores resonates with the growing body of literature emphasizing the significance of social support in maternal mental health 39 . This underscores the need for interventions that enhance family support systems during the perinatal period. The study’s identification of mental illness as a factor positively influencing practice aligns with the call for routine mental health screening during prenatal care, as suggested by various studies 40 , 41 . Such screenings can enable early identification and intervention for pregnant women at risk of postnatal depression. These aspects are also strongly supported by the mediation analysis, which highlights the direct influence of family relationships and mental illness on both attitudes and practices, reinforcing the need for integrated mental health services and family-centric educational approaches in prenatal and postnatal care.

The examination of participants’ knowledge about postnatal depression reveals a mix of accurate understanding and notable misconceptions. While there is commendable awareness of factors contributing to postnatal depression and the severity of associated symptoms, such as tendencies toward suicide or infanticide, significant misconceptions persist, including the belief that postpartum depression does not require treatment and that its primary impact is a reduction in breast milk secretion. These findings echo concerns highlighted in previous research about widespread misinformation regarding postnatal depression. To address these gaps, targeted educational interventions are recommended. Initiatives should focus on clarifying the necessity of treatment for postpartum depression and dispelling misconceptions related to breastfeeding 42 , 43 . This aligns with successful interventions outlined in the literature and emphasizes the importance of integrating educational programs into routine prenatal care for widespread accessibility and impact 44 .

The examination of attitudes towards postnatal depression unveils a complex spectrum of perceptions among participants, combining stigmatizing views and nuanced recognitions of its severity. While a significant portion stigmatizes postnatal depression as fault-finding or pretentious, rooted in emotional weakness, others acknowledge its potential for extreme consequences. These findings align with the pervasive stigmas highlighted in previous research on mental health issues. To address this, interventions should draw from successful destigmatization initiatives, employing educational campaigns and personal narratives. Recognizing the positive inclination towards depression screening and the desire for emotional support from family members, it is crucial to integrate routine depression screening into prenatal care and offer family-based interventions 45 , 46 . These targeted improvements aim to reshape societal attitudes, normalize mental health assessments during pregnancy, and enhance familial support, collectively fostering a more empathetic and informed approach towards postnatal depression 47 , 48 .

The assessment of postnatal depression prevention and treatment practices among participants reveals a complex landscape marked by a willingness to engage in positive practices but notable reluctance towards certain evidence-based behaviors. The hesitancy towards adopting practices such as vitamin supplementation and physical exercise suggests potential gaps in understanding or reluctance to embrace recommended preventive measures. Cultural and social factors influence healthcare decision-making, as seen in the preference for experiential knowledge and the hesitation towards medication. To address these gaps, targeted interventions should prioritize cultural sensitivity, offering comprehensive education on evidence-based preventive measures while acknowledging the value of experiential knowledge. Drawing from successful interventions in the literature, programs can destigmatize mental health treatments, promote peer support, and provide platforms for open discussions, fostering a supportive community that encourages seeking professional help when needed 49 , 50 .
