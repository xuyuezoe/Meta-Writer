# Akkermansia muciniphila supplementation in patients with overweight/obese type 2 diabetes: Efficacy depends on its baseline levels in the gut.

## Abstract

Akkermansia muciniphila is a promising target for managing obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2D), but human studies are limited. We conducted a 12-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involving 58 participants with overweight or obese T2D, who received A. muciniphila (AKK-WST01) or placebo, along with routine lifestyle guidance. Both groups showed decreases in body weight and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), without significant between-group differences. In participants with low baseline A. muciniphila, AKK-WST01 supplementation showed high colonization efficiency and significant reductions in body weight, fat mass, and HbA1c, which were not found in the placebo group. However, AKK-WST01 supplementation showed poor colonization and no significant clinical improvements in participants with high baseline A. muciniphila. These findings were verified in germ-free mice receiving feces with low or high A. muciniphila. Our study indicates that metabolic benefits of A. muciniphila supplementation could depend on its baseline intestinal levels, supporting the potential for gut microbiota-guided probiotic supplementation. (ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT04797442).

Graphical abstract Keywords type 2 diabetes obesity Akkermansia muciniphila AKK probiotics Introduction Obesity is a global health challenge with a continuously increasing prevalence worldwide. It is estimated that nearly two billion people (24% of the world’s population) will have obesity by 2035.1 However, obesity has a complex pathophysiology that posed a grim challenge for the control of its related morbidity and complications such as type 2 diabetes (T2D). Gut microbiota is an important environmental factor regulating host health and energy metabolism,2,3,4 and the associations of gut microbial dysbiosis with obesity and T2D have been well established.5,6,7,8,9 Previous studies have demonstrated that modulating the gut microbiota could potentially serve as a therapeutic strategy to improve glucose homeostasis.10,11 Among the numerous intestinal bacteria, Akkermansia muciniphila is a commensal species that has been repeatedly reported as a beneficial microorganism for human metabolism.12 A. muciniphila makes up 1%–5% of the total intestinal bacteria and colonizes the mucus layer of the gut using mucin as the source of carbon and nitrogen to protect the gut from pathogens.13,14 Evidence has accumulated that decreased abundance of Akkermansia has been found to be associated with obesity and T2D.7,15,16,17 Our previous study reports that A. muciniphila is significantly reduced in individuals with obesity and rebounds significantly after weight loss by bariatric surgery.7 Animal experiments demonstrate the role of A. muciniphila supplementation on the improvement of adiposity and glucose metabolism in mice fed chow diet or high-fat diet (HFD).18,19 A. muciniphila is reported to produce the outer membrane compounds of A. muciniphila (Amuc-1100)20 and protein 9 (P9)21 that are involved in the regulation of gut barrier function, glucose metabolism, and immune response. Together, lines of evidence suggest that A. muciniphila may be a potential probiotic for the treatment of metabolic diseases.22 Recently, in a pilot randomized controlled trial of volunteers with excess weight and insulin resistance (IR), pasteurized A. muciniphila is proved to improve insulin sensitivity and reduce insulinemia.11 However, whether A. muciniphila supplementation has anti-obesity and anti-diabetic effects in individuals with T2D who had overweight or obesity, and which individuals have better response to the treatment, remain to be determined. Hence, in this study, we isolated a strain of A. muciniphila, named Akkermansia muciniphila WST01 (AKK-WST01), from a healthy individual and optimized the culture medium for human use. We designed a randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled, multicenter phase 2 trial to investigate the effects and safety of AKK-WST01 supplementation in patients with T2D who had overweight or obesity. We found that AKK-WST01 reduced body mass index (BMI) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) only in participants with low baseline levels of A. muciniphila and further validated these findings in germ-free (GF) mice transplanted with feces from different donors with low and high abundance of A. muciniphila, providing evidence for precision medicine using probiotics guided by gut microbiota. Clinical and biochemical measurements At each visit, body weight was measured in a fasting state, after voiding, and wearing lightweight clothing. Weight was recorded to one decimal place (Inbody 770, Inbody Co. Ltd., Seoul, Korea), and BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters based on the height measured during the first visit. Whole-body composition was determined by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) (Lunar iDXA, GE Healthcare, Madison, WI, USA) including the fat mass, fat-free mass, and visceral and subcutaneous fat mass. HbA1c, serum insulin, and C-peptide assessments were conducted at the central laboratory in Ruijin Hospital (Shanghai Institute of Endocrinology and Metabolism, Shanghai, China). HbA1c levels were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography with the VARIANT II Haemoglobin Testing System (BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Serum insulin and C peptide levels were measured using the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) technique on cobase601 immunoassay analyzers (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The sensitivity range, intra-assay coefficient of variability (CV) and inter-assay CV for HbA1c were 3.5-19.0%, 0.39 and 0.45; for insulin was 0.2-1000 μIU mL−1, 1.1 and 3.6; C-peptide was 0.01-40.0 ng mL−1, 0.7 and 1.95, respectively. Metabolism measurements (energy expenditure and substrates oxidation) Twenty-four-hour energy expenditure and substrates oxidation were measured by whole-room indirect calorimetry (metabolic chamber) as previously described.31 Day and night energy expenditure was calculated between 9:00 AM to 10:00 PM and between 10:00 PM to 6:00 AM, respectively. Sleeping metabolic rate was calculated as the average energy expenditure between 2:00 and 4:59 AM. Basal metabolic rate was calculated as the average energy expenditure between 6:00 and 7:00 AM. Briefly, the metabolic chamber is an airtight room with a volume of 30,000 liters, maintained at a constant temperature of 25.0 ± 0.1°C, with air pumped out at a fixed rate of 80 L/min. Concentrations of oxygen (O2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) in the sample air were measured by the Promethion (Model GA-06/FG-01) integrated instrumentation (Sable Systems International, Las Vegas, USA). The gas analyzer was calibrated weekly using standard gas for best performance. O2 consumption and CO2 production were calculated by Henning method. To minimize the error of the metabolic chamber, we calibrated the accuracy by propane combustion. The precision of metabolic chamber was 99.3 ± 0.8% for O2 consumption and 100.0 ± 0.6% for CO2 production during the study. O2 consumption and CO2 production data produced by Henning’s method were obtained at the frequency of 1 min. And then we aggregated these minute-based data into daily-based for statistical comparison. Macronutrient oxidation and energy expenditure were calculated using the Weir equation with urinary nitrogen excretion. DNA extraction and quantification of the abundance of A. muciniphila in feces Fecal DNA was extracted from stool samples of each participant at baseline (Visit 2), Week 4 (Visit 3) and Week 12 (Visit 5) using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kits (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fecal DNA concentration was detected using a NanoDrop ND2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). The qPCR was performed using a QuantStudio Dx Real-Time PCR Instrument (Applied Biosystems Instrument) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). Diluted genomic DNA of cultured A. muciniphila with a concentration gradient was used to produce the standard curve. The abundance of A. muciniphila was expressed as log10 per gram of fecal DNA. The primer sequences for qPCR were listed in Table S7. We then chose log10 AKK ng/g fecal DNA < 0 as the low A. muciniphila group threshold, since the abundance below this threshold was close to undetectable levels. Metagenomic data generation, preprocessing and analysis Fecal DNA from the studied participants were subjected to library construction and metagenomic sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing platform (MGI, Shenzhen, China). Initially, SOAPnuke (version 2.2.1)32 was employed for data filtration, targeting ambiguous bases, low-quality sequences, and adapter sequences in the raw sequencing data. Bowtie2 (version 2.2.5)33 was utilized for alignment against the host reference genome (GRCh38) to remove human-derived sequences. After quality control and host removal, Kraken234 and UHGG35 databases, were employed for taxonomic annotation for each sample. Bracken2 was utilized to estimate species relative abundances within the samples. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA)36 was conducted based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity37 at the species level (R vegan package, version 2.6-2). The compositional differences between groups were assessed using the PERMANOVA (the function adonis, the R vegan package, version 2.6-2).38 Alpha (Shannon index) and beta (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity) diversities were calculated based on the species profile (the function diversity and vegdist, R vegan package, version 2.6-2). For strain level analysis, four reference genomes from the A. muciniphila species cluster were downloaded from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) (Release 220),39 including A. muciniphila (represented by SGB9226), A. muciniphila_A (represented by SGB9227), A. muciniphila_B (represented by SGB9228) and A. muciniphila_C (represented by SGB26761). The pairwise Mash distances between the four reference genomes and our supplemented strain AKK-WST01 were estimated using mash v2.3.40 InStrain v1.9.041 was applied to identify A. muciniphila strains in metagenomic samples before and after the intervention, using SGB9226, SGB9227, SGB9228, SGB26761, and AKK-WST01 as reference sequences. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) For FMT experiments, 0.9g of stool sample was resuspended in 6 ml of PBS with 0.5% L-cysteine hydrochloride monohydrate under anaerobic conditions, homogenized, and the supernatant was collected. The recipient GF mice received twice of oral administration of 200 μL bacterial suspension for the initial two days. Body composition analysis and measurement of whole-body energy expenditure Body composition was measured using a whole-body composition analyzer (EchoMRI). Energy expenditure (EE) was measured using a Comprehensive Laboratory Animal Monitoring System (Promethion metabolic measurement system, Sable) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EE was assessed by the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with body weight as a covariate. Tissue collection Mice were anesthetized and blood from orbital plexus was collected in tubes containing EDTA. Blood samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 3500 rpm, 4 °C. Plasma was isolated and collected at −80 °C. Epidydimal white adipose tissue (eWAT), inguinal subcutaneous white adipose tissue (iWAT), and intestine of each mouse were precisely dissected, weighed and immediately stored at −80°C for further analysis. RNA extraction and real-time PCR analysis Total RNA was extracted from frozen intestinal tissue using the Eastep Super Total RNA Extraction kit (ls1040, Promega Biotech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme). qPCR was performed using an ABI system (Life Technologies) with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). 36B4 was used as the reference gene for mouse samples. The primer sequences for qPCR were listed in Table S7. Alcian Blue/periodic acid Schiff (AB/PAS) staining The distal colon (with colon contents) was fixed with Carnoy’s Fixative (Servicebio, G1120) overnight and embedded in paraffin. Sections were subjected to a dewaxing process. Initially, the sections were immersed in Xylene for 20 minutes (twice), followed by a transfer to 100% ethanol for 5 minutes (twice), then to 75% ethanol for 5 minutes, and finally rinsed with tap water. The sections were subsequently stained with AB/PAS, dehydrated with 100% ethanol and Xylene, and sealed with neutral gum. Immunofluorescence analysis The distal colon (with or without colon contents) was fixed in Carnoy's Fixative (Servicebio, G1120) overnight and embedded in paraffin. The sections were dewaxed, blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature, and incubated with primary antibodies against MUC2 (Abcam, ab272692, 1:700), ZO-1 (Servicebio, GB111402, 1:500), Occludin (Servicebio, GB111401, 1:500), and Claudin (Servicebio, GB12032, 1:500). The slices were subsequently incubated with secondary Cy3 (Servicebio, GB21303, 1:300) and Alexa Fluor-488 (Servicebio, GB25303, 1:400) antibodies. DAPI (Servicebio, G1012) incubation was then performed at room temperature for 10 min. Images were acquired using an Olympus Microscope BX51 confocal system equipped with CellSens Standard software. Four mice per group were subjected for colon sectioning and immunofluorescence staining. For each section, three random fields were selected. The mean density of immunofluorescence assessment was quantified by ImageJ software (2.9.0_153t). Intestinal permeability assay Mice were gavaged with FITC-dextran (mol wt 4kDa, 46944, Sigma-Aldrich) with a dose of 60 mg/kg body weight and plasma FITC-dextran concentrations were assessed after 4 h. Fluorescence intensity was determined at emission and excitation wavelengths of 485 nm and 528 nm, respectively. FITC-dextran concentration was calculated based on standard curves generated by serial dilution of FITC-dextran. Inflammatory cytokine and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) detection Plasma TNF-α and IL-12p70 were analyzed using RayPlex Mouse Inflammation Array 1 (RayBiotech, Inc., Norcross GA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Plasma LPS levels in mice were determined by a Mouse LPS ELISA Kit (Cusabio) following the standard protocols. Quantification and statistical analysis Sample size calculation Sample size calculation was based on a 95% confidence level, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a power (1-β) of 0.8. It is assumed that HOMA-IR reduces by 1.575 (±1.89) in the experimental group, and by 0.52 (±0.62) in the control group according to the results in a previous human study,11 and a sample size of 24 per group has been established. Considering a dropout rate of 20%, a total sample size of 60 participants was deemed necessary. It is noted that the planned sample size was also adequate for evaluating body weight based on the human study.11 Thus, the total enrollment of 60 participants was planned, eligible participants will be randomized with 1:1 ratio into the experimental (AKK-WST01) and placebo groups. Clinical and experimental data For clinical data, statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26, Armonk, NY). All the P-values reported in the present study were two-sided. Since the lost to follow-up rate of all the participants was <5% overall, the data analyses were performed using ITT principles based on randomized intervention assignments with all available data without missing value imputation. One participant in the AKK-WST01 group lost to follow-up and his clinical data upon body weight and fasting plasma glucose at last visit (Visit 4) was used in ITT analysis. For subgroup clinical analyses according to participants’ baseline fecal A. muciniphila abundance, data from 55 participants (27 in the AKK-WST01 group and 28 in the placebo group) were used. One participant in the placebo group used antibiotics during intervention which might interfere in the quantification of A. muciniphila abundance in the feces, one participant lost to follow-up at the last visit and another one with last visit fecal A. muciniphila abundance remaining low (Log10[AKK ng/g fecal DNA] < 0 and lower than baseline value) in the AKK-WST01 group, were excluded from the subgroup analyses. For the stool sample analysis, another participant in the placebo group failed to provide fecal sample at Week 12 and was further excluded for A. muciniphila colonization analysis, leading to 54 participants having both baseline and last-visit feces included in the A. muciniphila colonization analysis. Additionally, six participants in both the AKK-WST01 group and placebo group failed to provide fecal samples, resulting in missing data for A. muciniphila colonization analysis at Week 4. Baseline data was reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as n (%) for categorical variables. Normality testing was performed by Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Q-Q plots. Group comparisons were executed employing the independent-sample t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, and the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. The mean change (SE) from baseline of each treatment group, the differences between AKK-WST01 and placebo group and its 95% CI were calculated. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed paired data and paired t tests for normally distributed data were employed to assess within group differences before and after the intervention. Interaction between intervention group and baseline fecal A. muciniphila abundance on the outcomes were calculated using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), including intervention group (AKK-WST01 vs Placebo), baseline fecal A. muciniphila abundance level (low vs high), age and sex as main effect, and baseline fecal A. muciniphila abundance level ∗ Treatment Group as interaction effect, to assess effect of interaction and main effects on primary and applicable secondary efficacy endpoints. In addition, subgroup analysis was performed to examine treatment effect within each fecal A. muciniphila abundance level (low vs high) if the interaction effect indicates potential interaction between the fecal A. muciniphila abundance level and treatment effect. Adjustments for multiple hypothesis testing were applied when necessary by the BH procedure for controlling FDR. Details regarding the statistical analysis are provided in the Methods S1. For animal experiments, statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Prism 10) and SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26, Armonk, NY). Effects were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, with baseline abundance of A. muciniphila (Low or High) and intervention (PBS or WST01) as independent variables. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used for comparisons of two curves with multiple time points. An ANCOVA with body weight as a covariate were performed for EE analyses. Variables without normal distribution were logarithmically transformed and conducted by LSD post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Data are reported as mean ± SEM.
