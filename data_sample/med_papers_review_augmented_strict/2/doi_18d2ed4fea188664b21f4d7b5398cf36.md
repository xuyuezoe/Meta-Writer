# Urinary tract infections in pregnancy.

## Abstract

Background: Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are the most common infection among pregnant women and have been associated with maternal and foetal complications. Antimicrobial exposure during pregnancy is not without risk. International guidelines recommend a single screen-and-treat approach to asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB); however, this approach has been questioned by recent studies.

Objectives: The aim of this narrative review was to assess the pathophysiology, current risk factors and management of UTI during pregnancy, its impact on pregnancy outcomes, and to develop recommendations on the best use of antimicrobials.

Sources: PubMed, Cochrane database, and ClinicalTrials.gov.

Content: Owing to the physiological changes related to pregnancy, pregnant women are at higher risk of UTI. All types of UTIs combined have been estimated to affect approximately 2% to 15% of women. ASB affects 2% to 7% of pregnant women. Recent studies do not provide good-quality evidence for an association between ASB and acute pyelonephritis if ASB is untreated. There is low-to-moderate-quality evidence that treatment of ASB results in a reduction in the incidence of low birth weight and preterm birth, which justifies screening practices for ASB with only a single urine culture in the first trimester. If the clinician opts for treatment, a short course of β-lactams, nitrofurantoin, or fosfomycin should be favoured. Studies on cystitis during pregnancy are limited. Acute pyelonephritis has been shown to be associated with increased maternal complications and in some studies has also been associated with preterm delivery and low birth weight. Preferred antimicrobials for the management of pyelonephritis are amoxicillin combined with an aminoglycoside, third-generation cephalosporins, or carbapenems. Studies on recurrent UTIs during pregnancy are limited, making it difficult to draw conclusions regarding prophylactic measures.

Implications: Further research is required to understand the true incidence of ASB-related complications and the benefit and modalities of screening for ASB and to further explore prophylactic measures.

Keywords Asymptomatic bacteriuria Cystitis Pregnancy Pyelonephritis Urinary tract infection Abbreviations UTI (Urinary Tract Infections) ASB (Asymptomatic Bacteriuria) APN (Acute Pyelonephritis) Background Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are the most common bacterial infection in pregnancy and have been estimated to affect 2% to 15% of the pregnant population, although recent data suggest that this may be higher. Using data from the National Birth Defects Study population (41 869 women), Johnson et al. [1] found that 11% to 26% of women reported being diagnosed and/or treated for UTI during pregnancy. Screening and treatment for asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) during pregnancy have long been implemented as routine practice in numerous countries as a result of historical studies indicating that in up to 30% of women with untreated ASB, acute pyelonephritis (APN) develops and that it is associated with a risk of preterm delivery [2]. The pertinence of these recommendations should be reviewed because recent trials have provided conflicting results regarding the risks of UTI on maternal and neonatal outcomes [3]. The risks of antimicrobial therapy also require consideration. The objectives of this narrative review were (a) to reassess the pathophysiology, current risk factors and management of UTI in pregnancy, and its impact on pregnancy outcomes and (b) to develop the recommendations for best antimicrobial use. An online search of PubMed, the Cochrane database, and ClinicalTrials.gov was performed to identify randomized controlled trials, clinical trials, and reviews published from 2001 to 2021. The search terms were ‘urinary tract infection’, ‘pyelonephritis’, ‘asymptomatic bacteriuria’, and ‘pregnancy’. Relevant references discovered during the literature search were also reviewed. Pathophysiology Physiological changes related to increased levels of progesterone place pregnant women at the risk of developing UTI. High progesterone level causes smooth muscle relaxation, resulting in reduced ureteric peristalsis and increased bladder capacity, thus favouring physiological ‘hydronephrosis’ and urinary stasis [4]. These risks are further compounded by compression of the ureters by the gravid uterus and increased renal function. The most common uropathogenic organisms identified by Belete and Saravanan [5] were Escherichia coli (55%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (14%), and Staphylococcus aureus (8%). In a study of APN, Wing et al. [6] reported that the main causative organisms were E. coli (82%), group B Streptococcus (21.4%), and K. pneumoniae (7%). Proteus mirabilis, Enterococcus spp., and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are also commonly identified [5,6]. The uropathogenic potential of enterococci alone, in healthy women (pregnant or not) without catheters, urinary tract abnormalities, and who are not immunosuppressed should be questioned. They are rarely found in bladder specimens despite their abundance in midstream ‘clean-catch’ urine specimens drawn from non-pregnant women with acute cystitis, suggesting that they are either contaminants or urethral colonizers [7]. Risk factors for the development of UTI during pregnancy include young maternal age, primiparity, diabetes, history of UTI, anatomical abnormalities, malnutrition, and low socio-economic status [1,5,8,9]. Genital hygiene practices have previously been reported as a risk factor but the evidence for this is very low [10]. Antimicrobials and pregnancy The use of antimicrobials in pregnancy requires careful consideration for multiple reasons. First, an association with birth defects has been reported: oral clefts with nitrofurantoin (OR, 2; 95% CI, 1.1–3.5), oesophageal atresia with trimethoprim (OR, 5.3; 95% CI, 1.4–20.2), and anorectal atresia with cephalosporins (OR, 5; 95% CI, 1.3–18.8) [11]. However, it is important to note that the absolute risk of these birth defects is low: oral clefts, 0.99%; oesophageal atresia, 0.22%; and anorectal atresia, 0.41% [11]. Second, antibiotics may alter the foetal gastrointestinal flora and may affect bone, cardiovascular, and immune system development [12,13]. The ORACLE II (Overview of the Role of Antibiotics in Curtailing Labour and Early Delivery) study demonstrated that in utero exposure to erythromycin or amoxicillin-clavulanic acid was associated with a higher long-term risk of cerebral palsy [14]. Finally, the current context of increased antimicrobial resistance is a further reason to reassess the current recommendations of antibiotic use for UTI during pregnancy. Asymptomatic bacteriuria ASB refers to the isolation of a quantitative count of ≥10 00 000 CFU/mL of one or more species of bacteria in a urine sample in the absence of symptoms and irrespective of pyuria [15]. This diagnostic cut-off value for the diagnosis of UTI is derived from the study by Kass [16] in 1956 in which urine samples from catheterized women with pyelonephritis and asymptomatic women were compared. This value has since been used as a consensus for the diagnosis of ASB [16]. Clean-catch voided urine samples are recommended as contamination of the urine by periurethral and vulval flora occurs regardless of cleansing methods or midstream collection [17]. Urine dipstick should not be used in the diagnosis of ASB because the detection of nitrites and/or leucocytes has been shown to have a low and variable sensitivity of 34% to 92% [18–20]. Studies from low-to-middle–income countries have highlighted the need for cheaper diagnostic tests with higher sensitivities; this is attributed to the scarcity of microbiology laboratories and time-consuming as well as costly urine culture tests that may require more laboratory resources [9,21]. In the pregnant population, an incidence of 2% to 7% has been reported among high-income countries but higher rates have been reported in Nigeria (25–45%) and Ethiopia (21%) [8,22–24]. ASB during pregnancy persists in 86% of women if left untreated [22]. In previous studies, untreated ASB was associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, in particular preterm labour, prematurity, APN, and maternal septic shock that are discussed later in this article. This justified the implementation of routine screening and treatment. Nevertheless, the evidence regarding the impact of ASB on obstetric outcomes is conflicting, with historical studies and some recent studies suggesting an association with preterm birth [2,25]. Recently, Wingert et al. [26] studied the benefits and harm of screening for ASB and reviewed the management of ASB comparing antibiotic treatment with placebo. They found a statistically significant reduction in the incidence of APN in the screening group (risk ratio, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.15–0.54; absolute risk reduction, 18/1000 cases) but no difference depending on whether screening was performed at the first antenatal visit or whether it was repeated throughout pregnancy. The quality of this evidence was graded as very low. They did not find any significant difference in the rates of foetal loss at ≤28 weeks, preterm delivery, or foetal abnormalities. When comparing antibiotic treatment with placebo for ASB, there were 44 of 1000 fewer cases of low birth weight in the treatment group (risk ratio, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.45–0.90). However, no significant differences were observed in perinatal mortality, foetal loss, neonatal sepsis, or preterm delivery [26]. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force identified a reduction in the incidence of low birth weight with the introduction of screening for ASB (risk ratio, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.46–0.90); however, no differences were noted in preterm births and perinatal mortality. Overall, the evidence in favour of screening was graded as a moderate benefit in its reduction of rates of APN [27]. Kazemier et al. [28] conducted a prospective cohort study with an embedded randomized controlled trial, comparing the outcomes of ASB positive and ASB negative women and comparing nitrofurantoin for ASB versus placebo. They found an incidence of APN of 2.4% in the ASB positive group and 0.6% in the ASB negative group (adjusted OR [aOR], 3.9; 95% CI, 1.4–11.4) but did not identify any change in rates or preterm delivery at 10 000 CFU/mL [36]. In a prospective study investigating culture results in pregnant women with a diagnosis of UTI, Jido [37] found that only a minority (26.9%) of women reporting symptoms of lower UTI had positive test results for cultures, suggesting that diagnosis of UTI based on symptoms alone may lead to a high number of false positive diagnoses [4,18,37]. Treatment of cystitis is the same as that of ASB. Acute pyelonephritis in pregnancy The incidence of acute pyelonephritis in pregnancy (APN), estimated in an 18-year retrospective cohort study, was 0.5% (5.3/1000 births) [6]. Kazemier et al. [28], in a Dutch multi-centre prospective cohort study with an embedded randomized controlled trial, reported that 2.4% of pregnant women with untreated first-trimester ASB developed APN (5 cases of APN in 208 patients with untreated ASB) compared with 0.6% women who were ASB negative (24 cases among 4035 ASB negative women). APN occurs at all gestational ages but appears to be more frequent in the second half of pregnancy [38]. In addition to the previously cited risk factors for UTI, further risk factors for APN are a history of APN in the preceding year (OR, 8.5; 95% CI, 7.4–9.7) [39]. APN is typically defined as the presence of fever with a temperature of >38°C; flank pain; costovertebral angle tenderness; with or without nausea or vomiting and chills; bacteriuria (20 bacteria per high power-field); and pyuria [36,38,40]. Increased white blood cell count and inflammatory marker levels are usually present. DeYoung et al. [41] evaluated fever in the context of APN in 110 pregnant women and found that in their American tertiary referral hospital, its presence has a high positive predictive value but that >75% of patients were afebrile on admission. However, all patients requiring intensive care unit admissions subsequently developed fever [41]. APN in pregnancy is associated with sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), acute kidney injury (AKI), disseminated intravascular coagulation, and anaemia. In a systematic review, Grette et al. [42] found that 49% of cases progressed to sepsis, ARDS developed in 47%, AKI in 10%, disseminated intravascular coagulation in 6%, and anaemia in 33%. They also report a 6% maternal mortality rate, 23% preterm birth rate, and 6% foetal mortality rate. However, the data for 49 of 107 cases reviewed dated before 1990 [42], thus calling into question the applicability of these findings in modern high-income settings. A retrospective cohort study conducted from 1993 to 2010 in large Californian maternity units and including 2894 cases of APN found a similar rate of anaemia (26%) but lower rates of other maternal complications [6]. Nevertheless, these remained significantly higher than in women without APN (sepsis: 1.9%; aOR, 56.5; 95% CI, 41.3–77.4; AKI: 0.4%; aOR, 16.5; 95% CI, 8.8–30.7; ARDS: 0.5%; aOR, 12.5; 95 % CI, 7.2–21.6; chorioamnionitis: 4.5%; aOR, 1.3; 95% CI, 1.1–1.5). A modest increase in the risk of preterm delivery at 38°C, nausea and vomiting, recurrent UTI, signs of sepsis, significant medical history, and uterine contractions [44]. However, these recommendations are not evidence-based, as there are only two previous randomized controlled trials that address this topic [45,46]. Further studies are needed to take into account current advances in medical care. Investigations for APN should include full blood cell count, serum chemistry (including renal function), and urine cultures. The routine use of blood cultures has been questioned as it has been shown that they are not required for the management of ≥90% of cases and that blood culture results only differ from urinary isolates in 1.3% of cases [44,47]. A Cochrane review including ten trials involving 1125 patients, evaluating the treatments for cystitis and pyelonephritis, was unable to draw any conclusions regarding the best antimicrobial therapy in terms of class, route, or regimen [40]. The choice of antimicrobial therapy should be directed by urine cultures, local resistance patterns, and patient preference. Drugs commonly used are amoxicillin combined with an aminoglycoside or third-generation cephalosporins. Carbapenems should only be used if there is a suspicion of infection by organisms resistant to narrow-spectrum antimicrobials. Ceftriaxone has been shown to result in a significant decrease in uterine activity that may result from APN [48]. Parenteral therapy should be favoured initially and until 48 hours after apyrexia has been achieved, and an oral switch is then possible. Patients should receive a total course of 14 days directed by culture results [36]. Some clinicians recommend a urine culture 2 weeks after the completion of antimicrobial therapy; however, randomized trials would be needed to confirm this approach. Prevention of recurrent UTI in pregnancy The term ‘recurrence’ is used to describe relapse or reinfection. ‘Relapse’ refers to the persistence of the pre-therapy isolate within the urinary tract despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, whereas ‘reinfection’ describes de novo infection after sterilization of the urine [22,49]. One possible explanation is that the pathogen is able to recolonize the bladder, having not been eliminated from the rectal flora [49]. When comparing nitrofurantoin with surveillance, no differences in preterm birth rates were observed; however, the data regarding prophylactic pharmacological measures are limited [49]. Non-pharmacological measures have an important role to play in reducing the use of antimicrobials. A systematic review compared cranberry use, hygiene measures, immunization, canephron, and ascorbic acid and concluded that hygiene measures were the only approach shown to have an effect on the prevention of UTIs [50]. However, eight studies were included, of which only four were randomized controlled trials, and the findings regarding hygiene measures were based on limited evidence (a case-control trial and a cohort study [n = 420]) [10,50]. Conclusion UTIs are the most common bacterial infection in pregnancy and are classified as ASB, cystitis, and APN. There is some evidence that untreated ASB can progress to APN, which is associated with higher maternal and neonatal morbidity. The associated risks of routine use of antibiotics for ASB should also be considered. On the basis of the current evidence, it is reasonable to suggest a single urine screen for ASB in the first half of pregnancy to reduce the risk of developing APN and its complications, while limiting antibiotic consumption and exposure. In countries where women have reduced access to care, a screen-and-treat approach is particularly relevant because it reduces the need for medical visits and the risk of maternal and neonatal complications. Current recommendations for the management of UTI during pregnancy are based on low-quality evidence, highlighting the need for further trials assessing the benefit of screening for and treating ASB and its impact on maternal and neonatal outcomes. Prophylactic measures, both non-pharmalogical and pharmalogical, and more cost-effective diagnostic modalities also require further study.
