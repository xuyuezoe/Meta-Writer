# Neoadjuvant durvalumab improves survival in early triple-negative breast cancer independent of pathological complete response.

## Abstract

Background: Addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is a promising strategy in early breast cancer, but the optimal duration of therapy is currently unknown. In the GeparNuevo (NCT02685059) trial, addition of durvalumab to NACT as previously reported led to a moderate increase in pathological complete response (pCR) rate by an absolute 9% (P = 0.287).

Patients and methods: Patients with cT1b-cT4a-d triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) received durvalumab 1.5 g or placebo every 4 weeks added to nab-paclitaxel 125 mg/m^2 weekly for 12 weeks, followed by durvalumab/placebo every 4 weeks plus epirubicin/cyclophosphamide every 2 weeks followed by surgery. Durvalumab was not continued after surgery. The primary objective was pCR. Secondary endpoints included invasive disease-free survival (iDFS), distant disease-free survival (DDFS) and overall survival (OS).

Results: A total of 174 patients were randomised between June 2016 and October 2017. After a median follow-up of 43.7 months, 34 events had occurred. Despite a non-significant increase in the pCR rate, significant differences were observed for 3-year iDFS, DDFS and OS: iDFS was 85.6% with durvalumab versus 77.2% with placebo [hazard ratio (HR) 0.48, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.24-0.97, stratified log-rank P = 0.036]; DDFS 91.7% versus 78.4% (HR 0.31, 95% CI 0.13-0.74, P = 0.005); OS 95.2% versus 83.5% (HR 0.24, 95% CI 0.08-0.72, P = 0.006). pCR patients had 3-year iDFS of 95.5% with durvalumab and 86.1% without (HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.05-1.06). In the non-pCR cohort 3-year iDFS was 76.3% versus 69.7% (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.29-1.54). Multivariable analysis confirmed a durvalumab effect independent of the pCR effect. No new safety signals occurred.

Conclusions: Durvalumab added to NACT in TNBC significantly improved survival despite a modest pCR increase and no adjuvant component of durvalumab. Additional studies are needed to clarify the optimal duration and sequence of checkpoint inhibitors in the treatment of early TNBC.

Key words early triple-negative breast cancer neoadjuvant chemotherapy checkpoint inhibitor durvalumab survival Introduction Treatment with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1) or anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1) antibodies improved progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in PD-L1-positive metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC) and has become an integral part of first-line treatment in mTNBC.1-3 Checkpoint inhibitor (CPI) therapy has entered clinical practice as a promising new neoadjuvant therapeutic approach in early TNBC (eTNBC), in particular in combination with classical chemotherapy, assuming an additive or synergistic interaction between both treatment strategies. It was shown that adding either pembrolizumab or atezolizumab to an anthracycline/taxane-containing neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) with or without carboplatin leads to an increase in pathological complete response (pCR) rates.4,5 Immunohistochemically PD-L1 expression in the tumour and/or lymphocytes predicts response to CPIs in patients with mTNBC.1,2 In contrast to the PFS improvement in mTNBC, the pCR improvement in the neoadjuvant setting is independent of the PD-L1 status. And although PD-L1-positive tumours as well as tumours with high tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) have a higher pCR rate after chemotherapy, none of these factors predict a response to CPIs.6-12 So far, there is no scientific rationale to continue treatment with a CPI after surgery. Yet, all registrational studies implemented an adjuvant component of CPI into the treatment concept. The question remains whether a CPI needs to be continued after surgery and if so how we can identify patients with the highest benefit. Considering the relevant long-term side-effects of CPI immunotherapy, it is important to focus this therapy on those patients who have the highest benefit and try to limit the exposure. Up to now no biomarkers could be detected that clearly predict the CPI effect measured as pCR. pCR remains, however, one of the most important prognostic factors after neoadjuvant therapy. Durvalumab is a PD-L1 inhibitor which has shown efficacy in bladder and lung cancer.13,14 The Safir study investigated durvalumab monotherapy as maintenance therapy in mTNBC.15 The GeparNuevo study is a randomised phase II placebo-controlled trial investigating the addition of durvalumab to an anthracycline/taxane-containing chemotherapy in eTNBC. The addition of durvalumab to nab-paclitaxel followed by dose-dense epirubicin/cyclophosphamide led to a small improvement of the pCR rate by an absolute 9% from 44% to 53% [odds ratio (OR) 1.45; P = 0.287).9 Here we report the secondary survival endpoints of the GeparNuevo trial, which are particularly interesting considering the moderate increase in pCR rate as well as the restriction of the CPI to the neoadjuvant therapy phase. We exploratorily evaluated clinical parameters as well as biomarkers to identify patients who benefit most or least from neoadjuvant durvalumab. Methods Study design and patients GeparNuevo (NCT02685059) is a multicentre, prospective, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II trial investigating the efficacy of NACT including nab-paclitaxel, followed by dose-dense epirubicin/cyclophosphamide with durvalumab versus placebo in patients with primary non-metastatic TNBC. The study was approved by the ethics committee and authority. Study details have been previously published.9 Briefly, women with untreated uni- or bilateral primary, non-metastatic invasive TNBC were enrolled after providing written informed consent for study participation and translational research. Further eligibility criteria are provided in Supplementary material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annonc.2022.07.1940. Follow-up was collected prospectively. Random assignment and masking The randomisation was carried out centrally at German Breast Group GBG headquarters in a 1 : 1 ratio using a computerised system. Assignment was stratified by stromal TILs (sTILs) at baseline [low (≤10%), intermediate (11%-59%), high (≥60%)]. Procedures Patients received one injection of durvalumab 0.75 g i.v./placebo monotherapy 2 weeks before the start of chemotherapy (window-phase), followed by durvalumab 1.5 g i.v./placebo every 4 weeks plus nab-paclitaxel 125 mg/m2 weekly for 12 weeks, followed by durvalumab 1.5 g i.v./placebo every 4 weeks plus epirubicin/cyclophosphamide 90/600 mg/m2 every 2 weeks for four cycles (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annonc.2022.07.1940).9 Durvalumab was only given as part of the neoadjuvant treatment. After surgery patients received postsurgical local and systemic treatment according to investigators’ decision. Outcomes The primary efficacy endpoint was pCR (ypT0 ypN0) as previously reported.9 Secondary time-to-event endpoints included invasive disease-free survival (iDFS) [equal to event-free survival (EFS) because no inoperable progressions had occurred], distant disease-free survival (DDFS), and OS. All time-to-event endpoints were defined as time in months from the date of random assignment until the first event occurred.16 Detailed definitions of time-to-event endpoints are provided in Supplementary material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annonc.2022.07.1940. Biomarker assessment PD-L1 expression using the SP263 antibody was assessed as percentage of tumour cells with membranous staining and percentage of PD-L1-positive TILs with membranous or cytoplasmic staining (relative to total TILs). PD-L1 positivity was defined as ≥1% in one or both of these. The dynamic biomarker changes of intratumoural-infiltrating lymphocytes (iTILs) were evaluated as previously described.9 TILs were assessed prospectively in the pretherapeutic core biopsy before randomisation. In addition, TILs were evaluated retrospectively in core biopsies after the window phase as well as in the residual tumour; all TIL evaluations were carried out using a standardized method developed by the TIL Working Group.17 All biomarker assessments were carried out blinded to treatment arm and outcome. Statistical considerations All patients who started treatment after random assignment were included in the modified intent-to-treat (mITT) population (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annonc.2022.07.1940). Median follow-up time was estimated with the inverse Kaplan–Meier method, and completeness of follow-up was assessed as described previously.18 iDFS, DDFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method based on the mITT population and compared between treatment groups using the log-rank test stratified by sTILs; 3-year rates with 95% confidence interval (CI) were presented. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CI for the time-to-event endpoints, unstratified as well as stratified by sTILs. Subgroup analyses of iDFS, DDFS, OS and pCR in prespecified subgroups as well as multivariate Cox regression models of the time-to-event endpoints were carried out as described in Supplementary material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annonc.2022.07.1940. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was done. The significance level was set to a two-sided α = 0.05. An improvement in pCR rates of 18% was assumed, which should translate to an iDFS HR of ∼0.773 according to Berry.19 A total of 474 events would have been required to achieve 80% power to detect this HR at a significance level of 0.05. Even when α = 0.2 would have been used, the required number of events would exceed the number of patients. Therefore, and given the hypothesis-generating character of this analysis, a time-to-event endpoint analysis was carried out after a median follow-up of 3.5 years, considering that most events are expected in the first 3-4 years. All analyses including pCR as a covariate were carried out using landmark analysis (5.5 months for patients in window phase, 5.1 months for patients without window) to avoid guarantee-time bias.20 Only patients at risk at the landmark time were included in the analysis. Analyses were carried out with SAS (Statistical Analysis Software, Cary, NC), Version 9.4 with SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.1. Results Between June 2016 and October 2017, a total of 235 patients were screened, of whom 174 were randomised and started treatment (88 durvalumab, 86 placebo) (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annonc.2022.07.1940) as previously reported.9 After a median follow-up of 43.7 months (range 4.9-56.1 months) a significantly longer 3-year iDFS was observed with the addition of durvalumab; 3-year iDFS 85.6% (95% CI 76.0% to 91.6%) with durvalumab and 77.2% (95% CI 66.3% to 85.0%) with placebo [HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.24-0.97), log-rank P = 0.036] (Figure 1A). Similar survival differences were also observed for DDFS and OS. The 3-year DDFS was 91.7% versus 78.4% [HR 0.31 (95% CI 0.13-0.74), log-rank P = 0.005] and 3-year OS was 95.2% versus 83.5% [HR 0.24 (95% CI 0.08-0.72), log-rank P = 0.006] (Figure 1B and C, Table 1). Discussion In the phase II GeparNuevo study, the addition of durvalumab to NACT in patients (cT1b-cT4a-d) with TNBC significantly improved iDFS, DDFS and OS, although the primary endpoint pCR did not meet statistical significance. In this study, no patient who achieved a pCR with durvalumab had a distant relapse or died from breast cancer after a median follow-up of 44 months. These patients seem to have an excellent outcome although they did not receive any adjuvant durvalumab therapy after surgery. These results are particularly remarkable considering the small pCR difference of 9%, the use of the CPI exclusively as neoadjuvant therapy and the phase II trial design powered only for pCR. Nevertheless, our results induced a discussion about the optimal therapeutic strategy in eTNBC. This is especially important in the light of the KN522, which continued pembrolizumab after surgery, and also showed an improved long-term effect.21 We hypothesise that systemic immunological effects triggered by the addition of durvalumab to chemotherapy resulted in better control/eradication of distant tumour cells/micrometastases. The two weeks of durvalumab therapy alone before the start of chemotherapy induced a change in iTILs within the tumour tissue. This change in iTILs predicted the durvalumab pCR effect.9 In the pCR analysis a significantly higher pCR rate with durvalumab was confined to the patients in the window cohort which remained otherwise unexplained. The group with induction therapy in the window cohort, however, did not have a better survival with durvalumab than the group without induction therapy. It cannot be excluded that the pCR effect induced by the induction therapy might be caused by a selection bias after the implementation of the amendment which removed the induction treatment. Differences in pCR rates in different phases of a trial have also been seen in other trials including the KN522 study.22,23 KN522 has generated evidence to include the CPI pembrolizumab into neoadjuvant therapy for patients with stage II-III TNBC. Cross-trial comparisons have their limitations but might help to better understand differences between treatments and cohorts, especially in this context where one trial continued CPI therapy after surgery and the other one did not. Despite the main difference in treatment duration and a slightly different risk profile of the included patient population, there is a remarkable consistency between the two trials regarding the 3-year EFS/iDFS. KN522 reported an 84.5% iDFS versus 85.6% in GeparNuevo with the CPI and 76.8% versus 77.2% in the control arm. The difference between the two trials regarding the subgroups of patients with pCR and no pCR should not be overinterpreted, as the GeparNuevo trial is a small phase II trial and the subgroups are even smaller. Differences could therefore be chance findings. The differences observed can also be due to a different trial population, with 25% stage I patients in the GeparNuevo study compared with none in KN522. This does not explain, however, the general comparable results between KN522 and GeparNuevo, one using CPI as neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy without chemotherapy and the other one using the CPI only before surgery given concomitantly with the chemotherapy. We observed a relatively high rate of treatment discontinuations before surgery in the GeparNuevo study.9 Treatment with a CPI is associated with potential lifelong severe immune-related side-effects, especially endocrinopathies. Careful patient selection and investigation of the optimal treatment duration is critical to minimize long term side-effects, even considering the recent data from KN522 showing that most side-effects occurred during the neoadjuvant part of the treatment.21 Impassion 031 combined the PD-L1 inhibitor atezolizumab with nab-paclitaxel followed by AC every 2 weeks as in GeparNuevo and has reported a significant increase in the pCR rate from 41.1% with chemotherapy alone to 57.6% by adding atezolizumab. After 20 months follow-up, 10.3% of the patients in the atezolizumab and 13.1% in the placebo arm had reported an EFS event (corresponding to a HR of 0.76).5 Neoadjuvant studies investigating CPIs could not yet demonstrate a significant role for the immune biomarkers TIL or PD-L1 for pCR or iDFS endpoints, which contrasts with the findings in metastatic disease. Early breast cancer seems different which might explain why so far no well distinguished subgroups have been identified predicting response to CPI therapy, but long-term outcome data in relation to biomarkers are scarce.1,2,4,5 Another more likely explanation for this difference is the different patient populations in eTNBC and mTNBC. In early breast cancer an immune activation leads to an improved response to conventional chemotherapy as well as immunotherapy, without a specific predictive effect to immunotherapy. As a result, many patients with an activated antitumour immunity will have a pCR as well as an improved survival. As a consequence, the patient cohorts in metastatic studies are enriched for patients without any immune information, but they contain also a number of patients who have evidence of immune activation, but did not achieve a pCR. In mTNBC the identification of this patient group leads to improved survival with CPIs. The biomarker analysis in this phase II study is mainly exploratory as the study is small. It could be shown that immunotherapy can induce changes in the immune infiltrate.24 Fewer immunogenic tumours are less likely to achieve a pCR with chemotherapy alone.25,26 With CPIs, however, the long-term and pCR effects seem to be independent of the sTIL status and PD-L1 status, whereas other immune markers might be more informative. TILs in residual disease of patients treated with durvalumab indicate an excellent prognosis questioning the additional adjuvant therapy in these patients. Further translational research needs to be done to investigate the role of immune infiltrate and to better select patients for neoadjuvant and adjuvant CPI therapy. All other neoadjuvant phase II-III trials evaluating CPIs in eTNBC as part of the neoadjuvant therapy published pCR data, ranging from no difference to a pCR increase of up to 16%.4-6 Some of them have reported long-term outcome after a relatively short follow-up, revealing fewer events in the experimental arm compared with the control arm.4,5 In general the pCR difference by adding a CPI to NACT is modest, but this does not seem to exclude a long-term survival effect.9 The neoadjuvant studies with a CPI used different strategies and chemotherapies, the NeoTRIP trial6 used an anthracycline-free regimen with nab-paclitaxel plus carboplatin, the NSABPB-59/GeparDOUZE study uses the same chemotherapy backbone as in KN522 but allowed capecitabine in patients not achieving a pCR.27 All studies with a CPI showed that patients with a pCR have a significantly better survival than non-pCR patients,4,7 and although the pCR increase by adding the CPI to neoadjuvant therapy is modest, the neoadjuvant approach might still be the preferred clinical strategy. In vivo data in mouse models suggest that a neoadjuvant approach in breast cancer is superior to the adjuvant one when using immune therapies, probably due to a higher quantity and better functionality of the T cells.28 The results from the adjuvant ALEXANDRA/IMpassion030 study (NCT03498716)29 are still pending, as well as two studies investigating CPIs in patients with residual disease—the SWOG S1418 (NCT02954874) comparing pembrolizumab versus observation and the A-BRAVE trial (NCT02926196) investigating avelumab. The latter ones will inform us about the efficacy of CPI monotherapy. All those data will help to finally address the contribution of the neoadjuvant versus the adjuvant component of CPIs in eTNBC. The GeparNuevo study has several strengths and limitations. GeparNuevo is a placebo-controlled phase II study and not powered for iDFS or OS. The follow-up was prospectively planned and collected. Because the primary endpoint was formally not met, the survival results presented here are exploratory and hypothesis-generating analyses on secondary endpoints. All patients underwent central assessment of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 and sTILs before enrolment. PD-L1 testing was carried out using the SP-263 antibody as well as the predefined scoring system developed for durvalumab in other types of cancer, which are different from the scoring system and antibodies used for other CPIs.30 Follow-up of 43.7 months is mature for a TNBC cohort. Some patients received additional systemic therapy after surgery, for various reasons which could have inserted a potential bias. We do not report EFS as the definition of an inoperable progression as event is vague and iDFS or DDFS are more precise endpoints. Only serious AEs and secondary malignancies were collected after surgery when the study treatment was regularly ended. In conclusion, the addition of durvalumab to NACT improves survival despite a modest pCR effect. This iDFS, DDFS and OS improvement is seen despite no adjuvant CPI therapy and in patients with pCR as well as without pCR. In the light of these results the value of additional adjuvant CPI therapy needs to be further assessed given the potential long-lasting side-effects and financial burden of CPIs.
