# Effects of combined treatment of probiotics and metformin in management of type 2 diabetes: A systematic review and meta-analysis.

## Abstract

Background: Lifestyle changes and dietary intervention, including the use of probiotics, can modulate dysbiosis of gut microbiome and contribute to the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to assess the efficacy of metformin plus probiotics versus metformin alone on outcomes in patients with T2DM.

Methods: We searched MEDLINE and EMBASE from inception to February 2023 to identify all randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which compared the use of metformin plus probiotics versus metformin alone in adult patients with T2DM. Data were summarized as mean differences (MD) with 95 % confidence interval (CI) and pooled under the random effects model.

Findings: Fourteen RCTs (17 comparisons, 1009 patients) were included in this systematic review. Pooled results show a significant decrease in fasting glucose (FG) (MD = -0.64, 95 % CI = -1.06, -0.22) and HbA1c (MD = -0.29, 95 % CI = -0.47, -0.10) levels in patients with T2DM treated with metformin plus probiotics versus metformin alone. The addition of probiotics to metformin resulted in lower odds of gastrointestinal adverse events (Odds ratio = 0.18, 95 % CI = 0.09, 0.3.8; I^2 = 0 %).

Conclusions: The addition of probiotics to metformin therapy is associated with improvement in T2DM outcomes. However, high-quality and adequately reported RCTs are needed in the future to confirm our findings.

Keywords Metformin Probiotic Insulin resistance Fasting glucose Fasting insulin HbA1c Lipids Gastrointestinal intolerance 1 Introduction Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia, resulting from insulin resistance and inadequate insulin secretion. It is estimated that 643 million people will be diagnosed with diabetes by 2030 (11.3 % of the global population) [1]. Thus, the prevalence of T2DM is increasing rapidly, primarily due to dramatic changes towards sedentary lifestyle, risky dietary habits, aging, and rapid urbanization. Regions, including the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) as well as the North America and Caribbean (NAC), are faced with a global epidemic of T2DM with a prevalence of 18.1 % and 11.9 %, respectively, in adults of 20–79 years of age [1]. Within the USA, only 26 % of adults diagnosed with diabetes were found to have an adequate glycemic control [2–3]. In addition to genetic and lifestyle factors, changes in gut microbiota composition are reported to be associated with T2DM development [4–5]. A diet rich in saturated fat and sugar and low in fiber content, was reported to influence gut microbial diversity [6–7]. Furthermore, the consequent gut dysbiosis leads to metabolic dysregulation, inflammation and insulin resistance associated with T2DM [4]. In contrast, lifestyle interventions including optimized diet, increased physical activity, and treatment with the first-line oral antidiabetic drug metformin, have been shown to reduce T2DM incidence by inducing weight loss and enhancing glucose control. Although metformin’s mechanism of action is not completely understood, it is believed that the liver is its potential major target organ [8–9]. Recently, it was also indicated that metformin’s glucose-lowering effect resides in the gut [10], which appears to depend on its interaction with diverse gut microbiota [11]. Thus, the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions in mediating the glucose-lowering effects of metformin may be enhanced by targeting the dysbiosis of intestinal microbiota [12–13]. Interestingly, the meta-analysis performed by Zhang et al in 2016 suggested that probiotic consumption decreased fasting glucose and insulin levels, as well as the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) index [14], while another study suggested that treatment with probiotics may also influence the immune response in patients with diabetes by affecting their intestinal microbiota composition [15–16]. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated improvements in fasting glucose levels and insulin resistance in patients receiving combined treatment of metformin and probiotics [17]. However, the results from several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing the efficacy of metformin plus probiotics versus metformin alone have been controversial. While few studies demonstrated an improvement in glycemic outcomes with the use of probiotics in combination with metformin, the results from other RCTs showed no such enhancement in glucose control. Furthermore, there are two recent systematic reviews assessing the efficacy of probiotics plus glucose lowering agents in patients with T2DM [18–19]. The results from both systematic reviews are limited by heterogeneity of included studies for the intervention in terms of inclusion of all glucose lowering agents with varying efficacy and mechanisms of action. Furthermore, both systematic reviews also have conflicting results as well where probiotics use was associated with reduction in fasting glucose (FG) but not glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c]) [18–19]. Accordingly, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the efficacy of metformin plus probiotics versus metformin alone in the improvement of glycemic control and the occurrence of gastrointestinal (GI) adverse effects in patients with T2DM. That is, the aim of this study is to synthesize all available evidence to assess the benefits and risks associated with the use of metformin plus probiotics versus metformin alone in diabetes management. 2 Methods This systematic review was performed according to a pre-specified protocol and standard methods in Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [20] and is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21]. The protocol has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023398024). 2.1 Search strategy and selection criteria This systematic review and meta-analysis include any randomized control trial (RCT), which assessed the efficacy of probiotics plus metformin versus metformin alone in adult patients with Type 2 diabetes. RCTs in the pediatric population or observational study designs were not eligible for inclusion. There were no restrictions on inclusion according to the language of the publication, gender, or ethnicity. We systematically searched electronic databases (PubMed and EMBASE) on February 13, 2023, using search strategies available in the Supplement. Additionally, we hand-searched the references of included studies and relevant review articles for additional eligible studies. Two review authors (HM and FA) reviewed all titles, abstracts, and full-text reports independently to determine the eligibility of each reference for inclusion in the systematic review as per the inclusion criteria. Any disagreement in the inclusion was reviewed by the senior authors (SS, AK, TR) and resolved by consensus. 2.2 Data analysis Two review authors independently extracted data from all the included studies using a standardized data extraction form. Data were collected on study characteristics (study design, setting), participant characteristics (number of participants enrolled, age, gender, baseline body mass index, weight, baseline fasting glucose (FG), and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)), intervention characteristics (probiotic, dose, route, administration schedule, and associated therapies) and outcomes. The primary outcomes were FG (mmol/L) and HbA1c (%). The secondary outcomes were fasting insulin (FI, μIU/mL), HOMA-IR, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, mmol/L), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, mmol/L), total cholesterol (TC, mmol/L), and triglycerides (TG, mg/dL) as well as the occurrence of gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events. The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment tool for RCTs [20]. This tool includes an assessment of the method of randomization, allocation concealment, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and any other bias. The overall certainty of evidence was assessed and summarized using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) method. This method separates the quality of evidence based on the risk of bias, inconsistency of results, indirectness of evidence, imprecision, and reporting bias [22]. We summarized continuous outcome data as a mean difference (MD) along with a 95 % confidence interval (CI) for each study and pooled it using a random-effects model. We summarized dichotomous data as odds ratio (OR) along with a 95 % CI for each study and pooled it using a random-effects model. All analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4 [23]. Heterogeneity between pooled studies was assessed using the I [2] statistic. An I [2] value below 30 % represents a low level of heterogeneity, 30–60 % represents a moderate level of heterogeneity, and over 60 % represents a high level of heterogeneity. We planned to assess the publication bias using a funnel plot for outcomes with at least 10 RCTs [24–25]. However, none of the outcomes were reported in all ten included studies and therefore, we did not assess overall publication bias. 2.3 Role of funding source This study was supported by the Khalifa University research grant. 3 Results 3.1 Results of the search The search strategy identified a total of 340 citations. The overall certainty in the estimate was very low. 5 Discussion To our knowledge, this study is the largest body of synthesized evidence to date assessing the effects of combined treatment of probiotics with metformin in the management of T2DM. The findings from the systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs show that adding probiotics to metformin versus metformin alone in the treatment of T2DM is associated with significant improvement in glycemic control and insulin sensitivity, assessed via reduction in levels of FG, HbA1c, FI, and HOMA-IR. This is in line with the results of meta-analysis by Zhang et al [14], which suggested that probiotic consumption decreased FG, HbA1c, FI, as well as HOMA-IR. Our findings also demonstrated that the odds of GI adverse events were lower among patients who received metformin plus probiotics than among those who were treated with metformin only. These results complement other recent reports indicating the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions targeting intestinal microbiota [12–13] that might contribute to the glucose-lowering effects of metformin. Furthermore, one of the first clinical trials that analyzed the impact of adding a probiotic to metformin demonstrated that this combined treatment significantly alleviated gastrointestinal intolerance and decreased fasting glucose levels as compared to placebo plus metformin treatment [40]. Similarly, in another recent study, significant improvements in HbA1c levels and gastrointestinal intolerance were reported in patientswith prediabetes amd T2DM receiving combined treatment of probiotics with metformin as compared to metformin treatment alone [34]. Furthermore, our pooled results also show a significant reduction in levels of TC following a combined treatment of metformin with probiotics. However, the addition of probiotics to metformin did not have a significant impact on HDL-C, LDL-C, and TG levels. Similarly, the findings of a recent systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that probiotic consumption decreased levels of total cholesterol and LDL-C in nonobese healthy adults with hyperlipidemia, while no significant effect was observed at the HDL-C and TG levels [41]. In addition, several recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses also indicated positive effects of probiotic treatment on different components of the lipid profile in patients with prediabetes and T2DM [42–43], including lowering TC levels [19]. Thus, these results suggest the beneficial effects of probiotic treatment on dyslipidemia and it would be interesting to further explore these effects in patients with prediabetes and T2DM. Furthermore, it was suggested that treatment with probiotics may also have an effect on immune responses by affecting intestinal microbiota in certain diseases, such as diabetes [15–16]. Cani et al. were the first to report about a causal relationship between the development of T2DM, gut dysbiosis, and metabolic endotoxemia, which is associated with increased plasma lipopolysaccharide levels and low-grade inflammation [44]. It was also indicated that adding gut microbiota modulators, including probiotics, to metformin therapy could boost its antidiabetic effects, while decreasing its adverse reactions [45]. 5.1 Limitations While the findings are encouraging for the management of T2DM, our results also emphasize the need for improving the conduct and reporting of RCTs. The overall certainty of evidence ranged from very low to low primarily due to heterogeneity among pooled results, imprecision, and publication bias at the outcome level. Altogether, 14 RCTs involving 18 comparisons met the eligibility criteria. However, despite the management of T2DM being the main premise of these RCTs, not all RCT consistently reported T2DM management outcomes across all the included studies. Specifically, of the 14 included RCTs, 13 reported FG levels as the outcome, 12 reported HbA1c levels and 8 reported fasting insulin levels, indicating possible outcome publication bias. However, whether this is an artefact of conduct or reporting cannot be determined as we needed access to protocols for all studies to make a definitive judgment. Another important limitation can be that the findings may also be prone to random error. Nevertheless, synthesized results have higher sample size as compared to any individual RCT, thereby, the probability of random error, in theory, should be lower than in any individual RCT. Therefore, results based on the totality of evidence should be adequate to inform patients, physicians and policy makers to consider the inclusion of probiotics as an adjunctive to metformin regimen for the management of T2DM. In conclusion, findings from this systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated benefits of adding probiotics to metformin treatment in managing T2DM-associated traits, including fasting glucose, HbA1c, fasting insulin, and total cholesterol levels, as compared to metformin treatment alone. However, it would be pertinent to perform additional clinical studies/RTCs in order to further elucidate the potential additional effects of this combined treatment of probiotics and metformin in the prevention and treatment of Type 2 diabetes, including its beneficial effects on metformin-induced gastrointestinal intolerance, as well as to better understand the mechanisms behind this therapeutical interaction. 6 Research in context 6.1 Evidence before this study We searched MEDLINE and EMBASE on February 13, 2023, using the search terms listed in the appendix. We looked for randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews of randomized controlled trials evaluating the efficacy of adding probiotics to metformin therapy for treating patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). We identified two systematic reviews (SR). The first SR assessed the effectiveness and safety of probiotics for glycemic control (fasting glucose [FG] and glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c]) in adults with impaired glucose control, including prediabetes and T2DM. The findings from both SRs are confounded by heterogeneity of various glucose-lowering drugs and conflicting outcomes. The results from the first SR showed that adding probiotics reduced FG, but not HbA1c levels in short and long-term. The second SR assessed the effects of probiotics and various glucose-lowering agents, including thiazolidinediones (TZD), glucagon-like peptide 1- receptor agonist (GLP-1RA), dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP4i), sodium/glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), on glycemia, lipid profile, and blood pressure in patients with type 2 diabetes. That is, despite varying glucose lowering effects, all agents were pooled. The results indicate that probiotics reduced fasting blood glucose, but did not detect a significant effect of probiotics on HbA1c levels. 7 Added value of this study While a few studies demonstrated an improvement in glycemic outcomes with the use of probiotics in combination with metformin, results from other RCTs showed no such enhancement in glucose control. Based on our knowledge, our systematic review and meta-analysis for the first time summarize the available evidence related, in particular, to the use of metformin plus probiotics versus metformin alone, in improving glycemic control and the occurrence of gastrointestinal adverse effects in patients with T2DM. We find that using probiotics, in addition to metformin, improve glycemic control among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Specifically, patients treated with metformin plus probiotics attained a lower FG (Mean difference = −0.64, 95 % CI = −1.06, −0.22) and HbA1c (Mean difference = −0.29, 95 % CI = −0.47, −0.10) compared to patients treated with metformin alone. Additionally, patients treated with metformin plus probiotics had lower odds of gastrointestinal adverse events than those treated with metformin only (Odds ratio = 0.18, 95 % CI = 0.09, 0.3.8). 8 Implications of all the available evidence This systematic review and meta-analysis provide support for using probiotics in addition to metformin in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.
