# Retreatment, rechallenge, and escalation with subsequent immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies across cancers after initial failure.

## Abstract

Background: Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are used across many tumor types in perioperative and advanced settings. However, most patients discontinue treatment due to disease progression, adverse events, or other reasons. Clinical benefit of using ICIs following discontinuation is not well defined.

Methods: We analyzed the literature examining ICI treatment outcomes after progression or discontinuation in different tumor types. We extracted data from 51 studies, assessed the strength of the evidence, summarized treatment options, and identified gaps in our understanding.

Results: We proposed definitions for the different scenarios of subsequent treatment with ICIs. In melanoma, studies frequently reported complete response (CR), partial response (PR), and stable disease (SD) following subsequent ICI therapy. In renal cell carcinoma, discordant results have been reported following subsequent ICI treatment; some trials reported CR/PR cases, whereas others did not show any CR/PR. In non-small-cell lung cancer, we found frequent reports of PR or SD but not CR following subsequent ICI treatment, although studies had small patient cohorts. Subsequent ICI treatment showed efficacy in some patients with urothelial carcinoma, but the small cohort sizes limited the strength of the evidence. One cross-tumor study investigated subsequent ICI treatment after initial discontinuation and reported a few PR and SD without any CR.

Conclusions: Evidence supporting the efficacy of subsequent ICI treatment is strongest in melanoma, but the level of evidence remains low overall. Prospective studies and improved reporting of subsequent ICI therapy in existing trials investigating long-term outcomes, standardized predictive factors, and treatment modalities are warranted.

## Introduction

Introduction

The number of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and the number of cancer patients eligible to receive ICI therapy has substantially increased, leading to broader access to these treatments. 1 ICI therapies are indicated across a range of tumor types in patients with advanced disease, including melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and urothelial carcinoma (UC), which are discussed in this review, as well as classical Hodgkin lymphoma, esophageal cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, head and neck cancer, gastric cancer, cervical cancer, and Merkel cell carcinoma. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Novel ICI combinations have emerged and ICI treatment has been approved for use in early-stage disease amenable to radical surgery in the (neo)adjuvant setting. 2 , 4 Additionally, subcutaneous administration is now available for select ICIs. 8 , 9

In the metastatic setting, despite long-term survival benefits, many patients do not respond to ICIs [innate/primary resistance; stable disease (SD) for 6 months 10 ]. Other patients may discontinue ICI therapy due to toxicity or other decisions. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Consequently, there is a growing need to understand the suitability and clinical significance of subsequent ICI treatment.

Although subsequent ICI treatment is associated with clinical benefit, most supporting data are derived from real-world evidence, retrospective studies, studies with small cohorts, or studies without comparator arms, 15 , 16 , 17 with few randomized controlled trials. 18 This paucity of evidence, along with reimbursement limitations may lead to inconsistent clinical use of potentially beneficial subsequent ICI treatment. Current guidelines for ICI treatment beyond progression or after initial discontinuation are sparse, lack detail, and do not provide consensus. For example, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network Guidelines® (To view the most recent and complete version of the guideline, go online to NCCN.org . NCCN makes no warranties of any kind whatsoever regarding their content, use or application and disclaims any responsibility for their application or use in any way), 19 European Society for Medical Oncology, 20 and Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer 21 recommend subsequent ICI treatment for patients with melanoma who experience PD after an initial response to ICI. However, no guidance on specific patient populations who are most likely to benefit from this treatment is provided.

In this article, we examine existing published data regarding subsequent ICI treatment after progression or initial discontinuation to identify knowledge gaps and to define strategies to address these gaps. We also assess the strength of the evidence for ICI use beyond progression or after initial discontinuation, thereby providing a summary of potential treatment options based on the available published data.

Data from large, prospective studies investigating both retreatment/rechallenge and ICI escalation strategies are limited. Three investigator-initiated prospective studies ( n = 41, n = 35, and n = 111) that assessed ICI escalation reported efficacy (ORR, 0%-24%) in patients with advanced RCC who were initially treated with nivolumab monotherapy, either experienced PD or did not respond, and subsequently received nivolumab + ipilimumab. 52 , 54 , 55 These data suggest that nivolumab + ipilimumab may have limited activity in patients who had experienced SD/PD following initial nivolumab monotherapy. As the current standard of care for first-line treatment in patients with relapsed or stage IV RCC is anti-PD-1 + anti-CTLA-4 or anti-PD-1 + TKI, very few patients are likely to receive nivolumab or any other anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody monotherapy upfront. 48 Thus, the clinical utility of retreatment/rechallenge and ICI escalation strategies for patients with relapsed or stage IV RCC is unclear. Indeed, first-line use of ICI combination therapies is supported by studies demonstrating the efficacy of ICI escalation to nivolumab + ipilimumab in patients who progressed on nivolumab monotherapy. 52 , 54 , 56 More data are required to understand the optimal sequencing of ICI therapy and the appropriateness of ICI use after progression on initial ICI combination therapy.

ICI treatment was expanded for use in the adjuvant setting for patients with stage II/III RCC. 48 No trials investigated subsequent ICI treatment in the adjuvant setting for patients with stage II/III RCC who experienced PD during or after initial ICI treatment; future studies should examine this.

## Non-small-cell lung cancer

Non-small-cell lung cancer

Current data and knowledge gaps

The current standard of care for patients with early-stage disease without EGFR or ALK genomic aberrations eligible for systemic treatment includes anti-PD-(L)1 + chemotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting with the option to continue anti-PD-(L)1 therapy after resection. 59 For patients with PD-L1-positive advanced/metastatic disease, the standard of care is anti-PD-(L)1 ± chemotherapy or anti-CTLA-4 in the first-line setting (To view the most recent and complete version of the guideline, go online to NCCN.org . NCCN makes no warranties of any kind whatsoever regarding their content, use or application and disclaims any responsibility for their application or use in any way). 59

Studies that investigated retreatment, rechallenge, and treatment beyond progression using ICI therapies in previously untreated, locally advanced/metastatic NSCLC often reported PR (15.2%-33.3%), SD (50.0%-60.6%), or PD, but only two cases of CR in patients who had not progressed on initial ICI treatment ( Table 3 and Supplementary Table S4 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105833 ). 12 , 60 , 61 , 62 ORR was 2.6%-33.3% in studies investigating retreatment, 15 , 61 , 63 18.8%-22.5% in studies investigating rechallenge, 62 , 64 and 9.3% in a study investigating treatment beyond progression. 65 However, most prospective studies presented here were long-term follow-up reports on clinical trials, in which relatively few patients received subsequent ICI treatment after discontinuation (in five out of eight prospective studies 12 , 61 , 66 , 67 , 68 ) ( Table 2 ). No studies had placebo or chemotherapy control arms, and no studies investigated ICI escalation. Many studies did not report long-term outcomes data, such as OS and PFS. 12 , 61 , 63 , 66 Additionally, no studies investigated the use of subsequent ICI treatment in patients with primary resistance to initial ICI therapy. Large-scale prospective data are currently lacking, limiting the level of evidence to support the use of subsequent ICI treatment in patients with NSCLC. Current data and knowledge gaps

The treatment landscape of UC is rapidly changing. ICI treatment is now recommended for patients with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer and early-stage muscle-invasive bladder cancer (To view the most recent and complete version of the guideline, go online to NCCN.org . NCCN makes no warranties of any kind whatsoever regarding their content, use or application and disclaims any responsibility for their application or use in any way). 70 Nivolumab monotherapy can be considered as adjuvant treatment for patients with resected stage II/IIIA UC. 70 The preferred first-line treatment for patients with unresectable stage IIIB/IV UC is pembrolizumab + enfortumab vedotin, and nivolumab + chemotherapy can be considered as an alternative. 70

TITAN-TCC was a prospective study that investigated ICI escalation in two independent cohorts of patients who had previously received platinum-based chemotherapy. 71 Patients initially received nivolumab monotherapy. Patients who achieved CR/PR continued nivolumab monotherapy, whereas patients who experienced SD/PD received ICI escalation to nivolumab + ipilimumab. 71 Cohort 1 included patients in the first- or second-/third-line setting and cohort 2 included patients in the second-/third-line setting. 71 Response to initial nivolumab monotherapy and subsequent nivolumab + ipilimumab treatment is shown in Supplementary Table S4 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105833 . Although ORRs and 3-year OS rates suggest that nivolumab + ipilimumab may be useful for patients who experience PD on second- or third-line nivolumab (ORR, 23% for second-/third-line cohort 1 and 20% for cohort 2; 3-year OS rate, 19% for second-/third-line cohort 1 and 34% for cohort 2), substantial rates of primary progression and early death were observed in cohort 1, which led to discontinuation of the cohort. 71 Earlier analyses of both cohorts identified higher ORR in patients with PD-(L)1 expression ≥1% than in patients with PD-(L)1 expression <1%. 72 , 73

A recent phase III study reported significantly improved outcomes in patients with previously untreated unresectable locally advanced or metastatic UC following enfortumab vedotin + pembrolizumab treatment compared with chemotherapy. 74 Additionally, avelumab has recently demonstrated efficacy in the adjuvant setting. 75 In each of these treatment scenarios, there is a paucity of data regarding subsequent use of ICI therapies after initial discontinuation that future studies should seek to address. 70

Implications for practice

The evolving treatment landscape for UC, particularly in the first-line metastatic setting, has led to uncertainty regarding the optimal use of subsequent ICI treatment. Data presented here indicate that selected patients with UC who do not respond to nivolumab in the second-line setting could be treated with nivolumab + ipilimumab, particularly if they have PD-L1 expression ≥1%. Treatment beyond progression is not recommended due to the aggressive nature of metastatic UC.

## Conclusions

Conclusions

The data summarized here indicate that subsequent ICI treatment, such as retreatment, rechallenge, ICI switch, or ICI escalation, can provide clinical benefit for patients. Participation in clinical trials is a favored option for patients who discontinue initial ICI treatment due to disease progression, especially when an ICI-based combination therapy is being investigated.

In melanoma, consistent reports of response, including CR, exist for patients with resected or unresectable stage III/IV disease who received retreatment/rechallenge, 11 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 31 and studies suggest that ICI escalation strategies may result in even greater responses. 11 , 27 , 40 Nonetheless, response to subsequent ICI treatment is not consistent across tumor types. In NSCLC, reports of CR are limited, highlighting the need for further treatment modalities in this setting and underscoring the importance of clinical trial participation. Data on whether response to initial ICI therapy is indicative of response to subsequent ICI therapy also varies between tumor types: a study in NSCLC suggests response to initial ICI therapy is indicative of response to subsequent ICI treatment, 65 a study in RCC suggests that it is not, 51 and two studies in melanoma report opposing outcomes. 26 , 36 However, data here are extremely limited and differ depending on the specific type and the timing of subsequent ICI treatment used; therefore, this remains an unresolved issue.

Lack of clarity regarding variations in efficacy between different subsequent ICIs may prevent their optimal use. In this article, we have described five distinct modalities: retreatment, rechallenge, ICI switch, treatment beyond progression, and ICI escalation. Each of these strategies has been investigated to a different degree in individual tumor types. In melanoma, ICI escalation appears to be more effective than retreatment/rechallenge with ICI monotherapy in patients with metastatic or unresectable stage III/IV disease. 11 , 24 , 27 , 40 In advanced or metastatic RCC and advanced UC, ICI escalation appears to be effective in some patients who do not respond to initial ICI monotherapy. 52 , 54 , 56 , 72 , 73 By contrast, to our knowledge, ICI escalation has not been investigated in NSCLC. Future studies comparing different subsequent ICI treatment modalities could demonstrate the most effective strategy for specific tumor types and improve their clinical utility.

A consideration when evaluating the evidence from the studies presented here is the standard of care. The treatment landscape for each of the tumor types discussed has changed since the advent of immunotherapies, with ICI forming the backbone of the standard of care across RCC, melanoma, and many lung cancers. 19 , 48 , 59 Nivolumab (± ipilimumab) and pembrolizumab are approved for use in patients with metastatic melanoma, metastatic NSCLC, and advanced RCC, and nivolumab + relatlimab is approved for use in patients with metastatic melanoma. 2 , 76 Many patients will receive these combination treatments as their initial ICI therapy, which may reduce the clinical relevance of ICI escalation.

It is important to consider and rule out the possibility of pseudoprogression, a response pattern characterized by apparent PD after initiation of ICI treatment, followed by prolonged SD or PR/CR. 77 This could give a false impression of efficacy for subsequent ICI treatments if traditional Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) are used to determine progression rather than immune RECIST. Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer guidelines recommend a confirmatory scan ≥4 weeks after initial evidence of PD to rule out pseudoprogression. 10

No predictive factors exist to identify patients likely to respond to subsequent ICI treatment. A number of biomarkers associated with response have been described, including PD-L1 and lactate dehydrogenase, 24 , 26 , 52 , 53 but there are no validated biomarkers with which patients could be prospectively stratified as likely or unlikely to respond to subsequent ICI treatment. Indeed, there are conflicting reports regarding the association of PD-L1 expression with response to subsequent ICI treatment: Long et al. report that most responders had PD-L1-positive tumors, Olson et al. report that more responses were observed in PD-L1-negative tumors, and Choueiri et al. report that there is no association between PD-L1 expression and response. 24 , 26 , 53 However, there are data examining factors that are predictive of response to initial ICI treatment. 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 For example, a low neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has been associated with longer PFS and OS than a high NLR among patients with NSCLC treated with an initial course of anti-PD-1. 83 , 84 Interestingly, a recent retrospective analysis of patients with metastatic NSCLC who received ICI rechallenge found that a low NLR was associated with significantly longer PFS and OS compared with a high NLR, suggesting that NLR could be a useful indicator for response to subsequent ICI treatment. 85 Other biomarkers predictive of response to initial ICI treatment include tumor mutational burden, interferon-γ-related gene expression signatures, and high levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. 80 , 81 , 82 Limited data also suggest that the presence of immunosuppressive cell types in the tumor microenvironment, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages, may be associated with low response to initial ICI treatment. 86 , 87 These biomarkers of response to initial ICI therapy could be informative of response to subsequent ICI therapy, and prospective studies should seek to validate these biomarkers in the subsequent use setting. The development of treatment algorithms that detail the clinical scenarios under which patients should receive specific ICI treatments and the identification of factors that are predictive of response to subsequent ICI treatment could facilitate effective use of these strategies.

Few studies have compared subsequent ICI use with a control arm, and many of the studies described here had small cohorts and excluded patients who had received prior ICI treatment. 12 , 34 , 39 , 52 , 61 , 67 , 68 As first-line ICI therapy becomes common, future studies should include patients who have previously received ICI treatment, as has been done in a few clinical trials to date. 15 , 41 , 51 Future studies should be designed and powered to assess subsequent ICI treatment strategies in distinct patient populations against a meaningful control arm. Trials with control arms that enroll patients on the basis of predictive factors could provide evidence of the efficacy of subsequent ICI treatment in specific subpopulations.

This review focused on studies in which patients discontinued ICI therapy due to course completion or PD. However, patients may also discontinue ICI therapy due to AEs and subsequently receive further ICI treatment. In this setting, it is important to consider whether previous ICI treatment was well tolerated, the potential benefits and risks of subsequent ICI, and the potential impact of any additional medication used to mitigate AEs, such as immunosuppressive agents used to treat immune-mediated AEs. 88

Increased use of ICI therapy in early-stage disease underscores the need to investigate the impact of early ICI treatment on the efficacy of ICI use upon PD and in later lines of therapy.
