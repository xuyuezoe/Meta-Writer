# Prenatal exposure to maternal psychological distress and telomere length in childhood.

## Abstract

Telomere length (TL) is a biological marker of cellular aging, and shorter TL in adulthood is associated with increased morbidity and mortality risk. It is likely that these differences in TL are established long before adulthood, and there is growing evidence that TL can reflect prenatal experiences. Although maternal prenatal distress predicts newborn TL, it is unknown whether the relation between prenatal exposure to maternal distress and child TL persists through childhood. The purpose of the current longitudinal, prospective study is to examine the relation between prenatal exposure to maternal distress (perceived stress, depressive symptoms, pregnancy-related anxiety) and TL in childhood. Participants included 102 children (54 girls) and their mothers. Mothers' distress was assessed five times during pregnancy, at 12 weeks postpartum, and at the time of child telomere measurement between 6 and 16 years of age. Maternal distress during pregnancy predicted shorter offspring TL in childhood, even after accounting for postnatal exposure to maternal distress and other covariates. These findings indicate that maternal mental health predicts offspring TL biology later in childhood than previously observed. This study bolsters claims that telomere biology is subject to fetal programming and highlights the importance of supporting maternal mental health during pregnancy.

## INTRODUCTION

INTRODUCTION

Telomeres are the protective DNA–protein caps of chromosomes ( Moyzis et al., 1988 ), and shorter telomere length (TL) in immune cells among adults predicts elevated disease risk and earlier mortality ( Herrmann et al., 2018 ). Although TL in adulthood reflects a combination of the TL setpoint (i.e., TL at birth) and subsequent telomere shortening ( Armanios & Blackburn, 2012 ; Shalev, 2012 ), observational human studies suggest the TL setpoint at birth is a more influential contributor to differences in adult TL than subsequent shortening ( Benetos et al., 2013 ; Daniali et al., 2013 ; Factor-Litvak et al., 2016 ; Hjelmborg et al., 2015 ). Prospective research considering prenatal and postnatal factors is needed to determine the relative impact of the prenatal and the postnatal environments. Experimental animal models show that early life TL predicts longevity, consistent with the notion that differences in TL at birth have meaningful implications for life span health. For instance, finches with the longest life span had the longest TL during the nestling period ( Heidinger et al., 2012 ), and similar associations have been observed in mammals ( Fairlie et al., 2016 ). Further, differences in TL at birth reflect both environmental and genetic factors ( Hjelmborg et al., 2015 ). Experiences prior to birth likely contribute to differences in the TL setpoint and therefore subsequent adult TL. Thus, understanding the prenatal conditions related to differences in TL is critical for understanding long-term health and longevity in humans ( Entringer et al., 2018 ; Shalev et al., 2013 ).

Multiple aspects of the prenatal environment have been associated with differences in offspring TL. Maternal tobacco use, sleep disorders, health, and pregnancy complications (e.g., hypertension and gestational diabetes) and nutrition during pregnancy ( Cross et al., 2010 ; Entringer et al., 2015 ; Martens et al., 2016 ; Salihu, King, et al., 2015 ; Salihu, Pradhan, et al., 2015 ; Shalev et al., 2014 ; Tellechea et al., 2015 ; Theall et al., 2013 ; Werlang et al., 2019 ; Xu et al., 2014 ) all predict variation in the TL setpoint suggesting that telomere biology is impacted by the prenatal environment. Less is known about the impact of maternal psychological distress on offspring telomere biology. However, prenatal maternal distress is associated with child physical and mental health ( Davis & Sandman, 2012 ; Davis & Sandman, 2010 ; Davis et al., 2004 ; Davis et al., 2017 ; Glover et al., 2010 ; O’donnell et al., 2014 ; Posner et al., 2016 ; Van den Bergh et al., 2020 ) and these health outcomes have been shown to be related to TL biology ( Baskind et al., 2021 ; Humphreys et al., 2020 ; Lamprokostopoulou et al., 2019 ; Verstraete et al., 2018 ; Wojcicki et al., 2015 ). It is plausible that maternal prenatal distress could play a role in shaping offspring telomere biology. Consistent with this hypothesis, there is evidence that prenatal maternal stress is linked to TL in the newborn. Elevated pregnancy-related anxiety ( Entringer et al., 2013 ), depressive symptoms ( Bosquet Enlow et al., 2018 ), and stress during pregnancy ( Izano et al., 2020 ; Marchetto et al., 2016 ; Send et al., 2017 ; Suh et al., 2019 ; Verner et al., 2020 ) predict shorter newborn TL.

Although there is evidence that prenatal maternal distress is associated with the TL set point at birth, few prospective studies have examined whether prenatal maternal distress predicts TL beyond the newborn period. Retrospective research shows that recall of prenatal maternal life events is associated with offspring TL beyond the newborn period ( Entringer et al., 2011 ; Wang et al., 2017 ). Prospective studies have found that prenatal distress predicts TL at 1 year of age ( Suh et al., 2019 ) and at 3–5 years of age ( Carroll et al., 2020 ). These findings are consistent with research showing that exposure to stressors such as intimate partner violence and lower socioeconomic status during the prenatal period are associated with shorter offspring TL ( Chan et al., 2019 ; Martens et al., 2020 ). In contrast, two studies did not find associations between maternal distress during pregnancy and child TL. Specifically, prenatal depression symptoms measured once during pregnancy were not associated with TL at 4–5 years old ( Wojcicki et al., 2015 ), and report of stress during the last month pregnancy measured after birth was not associated with child TL at 11 years old ( Slykerman et al., 2019 ). A significant limitation of these prior studies examining prenatal maternal distress exposure and child TL is lack of consideration of potential confounding factors including exposure to trauma and other forms of adversity, postnatal maternal distress, and parental care that have been linked to TL ( Drury et al., 2014 ; Enokido et al., 2014 ; Mitchell et al., 2014 ; Shalev et al., 2013 ; Theall et al., 2013 ; Wojcicki et al., 2015 ). Thus, prospective longitudinal studies that assess maternal distress at multiple visits throughout pregnancy and consider the postnatal environment are needed to address these gaps in the literature and to determine if there are persisting consequences of prenatal maternal distress on child TL.

The purpose of the current longitudinal, prospective study is to investigate whether fetal exposure to maternal psychological distress (a composite measure of stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms) during pregnancy predicts TL in childhood beyond postnatal factors that may impact TL including maternal mental health, parental cohabitation status, socioeconomic status, and child life events. Because it is well established that male and female fetuses respond differently to the intrauterine environment ( De Bellis, 2001 ; Giesbrecht et al., 2017 ; Sandman et al., 2013 ) and there is evidence for sex-specific links between prenatal stress and offspring TL ( Bosquet Enlow et al., 2018 ; Bosquet Enlow et al., 2021 ), we also examine sex-specific differences in the association between prenatal distress and offspring TL.

## METHOD

METHOD

Study overview

Mother–child pairs were assessed at five prenatal visits (15.3 ± 1.2, 19.4 ± 1.2, 25.4 ± 1.1, 31.1 ± 1.1, and 36.7 ± 1.2 gestational weeks), an early postpartum assessment 3 months after delivery, and during childhood at the time of TL assessment. All methods and materials were approved by the Institutional Review Boards for Protection of Human Subjects. Mothers provided written, informed consent, and children age 7 or older provided assent.

Participants

Participants in the present investigation included 102 children (48 males, 54 females) and their mothers. Mothers and their children ( M = 12.01 years, SD = 2.71, 6.23–16.33) from a longitudinal cohort (described in Glynn et al., 2018 ) were asked to participate in a study involving collection of child blood spots for TL measurement. Adult English-speaking women with a singleton pregnancy were eligible for this study. Initial exclusion criteria were alcohol, tobacco, or drug use during pregnancy, presence of a condition associated with neuroendocrine dysfunction, or any uterine, cervical, or major congenital fetal anomalies. In childhood, participants were invited to participate. Of the 138 mother–child dyads recruited for the telomere study, 36 did not meet additional inclusion criteria. Participants were excluded if mothers did not complete the prenatal psychological distress measures ( n = 3), if the child already had a sibling participating in the study ( n = 9; sibling with higher quality DNA sample was selected), if the child was born before 34 weeks of gestation ( n = 2), and if the DNA collected did not meet quality standards ( n = 22; details below). The 36 participants who were excluded from the current study did not differ from the final sample on demographic (race/ethnicity, age, marital status, income-to-needs ratio, child sex), perinatal (gestational age at birth, 5-min Apgar, birth weight percentile), or obstetric factors (obstetric complications, gestational weight gain, maternal prepregnancy body mass index [BMI]) (all p s > .2). Among the participants excluded from the study, there was a smaller proportion of first-born children ( χ 2 = 2.9, p = .09) and a higher proportion of participants living with the child’s biological father ( χ 2 = 2.8, p = .10) than among those included in the study. Further information about the final study sample ( n = 102) can be found in Table 1 . Women in this sample were primarily non-Hispanic white (44%) or Hispanic/Latina (28%) and reported a household income of greater than $60,000/year and had educational experience beyond high school.

Measures

Prenatal maternal psychological distress

Maternal psychological distress was assessed at five time points during pregnancy using measures of pregnancy-related anxiety, depressive symptoms, and perceived stress.

Pregnancy-related anxiety was assessed using the 10-item pregnancy-related anxiety scale ( Glynn et al., 2008 ; Rini et al., 1999 ). Each item on this scale was scored on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 ( Not at all ) to 4 ( Very much ), with a final score ranging from 10 to 40. This scale is internally reliable ( Rini et al., 1999 ), with an alpha coefficient of .84 in the current study.

Maternal depressive symptoms were measured using the nine-item version of the Center for Epidemiological Studies—Depression Inventory (CES-D; Bennett & Einarson, 2004 ; Santor & Coyne, 1997 ). Response anchors for the CESD were on a scale from 0 ( Rarely or none of the time ) to 3 ( Most or all of the time ); final scores could range from 0 to 27. This scale reliably assesses depressive symptoms ( Santor & Coyne, 1997 ), with an alpha coefficient of .86 in the current sample.

Maternal perceived stress was assessed using 10-item version of Cohen’s Perceived Stress Scale (PSS; Cohen et al., 1983 ). Responses for each item in the PSS were on a scale from 0 ( Never ) to 4 ( Almost always ), and the final score could range from 0 to 40. This scale is reliable and appropriate for assessment among pregnant women ( Solivan et al., 2015 ). The alpha score for the PSS in the current study is .91.

Scores on each measure were averaged across the five pregnancy time points to create pregnancy-related anxiety ( M = 18.4, SD = 4.9), prenatal depression ( M = 6.6, SD = 4.2), and prenatal perceived stress ( M = 12.2, SD = 5.5) composite scores, which were positively correlated with one another (range r s: .49–.79, all p s<.001). Each composite score was then standardized, and all standardized scores were averaged to create a single prenatal maternal psychological distress score. This distress score was used as a primary predictor in analyses of prenatal maternal psychological distress as prior research has shown that the composite of anxiety, stress, and depression predicts cognitive and emotional development through infancy and childhood ( Glynn et al., 2018 ; Grande et al., 2021 ; Howland et al., 2020 ).

Postpartum and childhood maternal distress

Maternal depressive symptoms and perceived stress were measured 12 weeks postpartum ( M = 12.0, SD = 5.8) and again at the time of telomere assessment (“childhood”) using the CES-D (postpartum: M = 3.1, SD = 4.5; childhood: M = 5.5, SD = 4.7) and PSS (postpartum: M = 12.0, SD = 7.1; childhood: M = 12.0, SD = 5.6), respectively. In addition, the state anxiety subscale of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) ( Spielberger et al., 1970 ) was used to capture maternal anxiety at the postpartum ( M = 18.1, SD = 5.3) and childhood lab visits ( M = 18.0, SD = 5.0). Scores on each of these measures at the postpartum (range r s: .66–.69, all p s < .001) and the childhood (range r s: .56–.68, all p s < .001) visit were positively correlated. Postpartum and childhood maternal distress composites were calculated by standardizing scores on each distress indictor (CES-D, PSS, STAI) at the respective lab visit and calculating the average of these standardized scores.

Pregnancy factors and birth outcomes

Maternal height, prepregnancy weight, obstetric complications, and the child’s gestational age at birth, sex, birthweight percentile adjusted for gestational age at birth and sex, 5-min Apgar scores, and date of birth were extracted from medical records. Child biological sex was determined by physical characteristics at birth. Obstetric complications were determined based on a standard scoring used in numerous prior publications ( Davis & Sandman, 2012 ; Glynn et al., 2018 ; Hobel, 1982 ) and included gestational diabetes, oligohydramnios, pregnancy-induced hypertension, placenta previa, anemia, prenatal infection, vaginal bleeding, and preterm labor. The total number of obstetric complications diagnosed during pregnancy was used as the cumulative obstetric complications score.

Childhood factors

Child height and weight were collected, and BMI was calculated (kg/m 2 ). BMI percentile was calculated using the Centers for Disease Control BMI calculator for Child and Teen ( https://www.cdc.gov/healthyweight/bmi/calculator.html ). BMI and BMI percentile were highly correlated ( r = .84, p < .001). Child life events were assessed via maternal report on the Coddington Life Events Scale ( Coddington, 1972 ). Mothers reported as to whether their child had experienced each of 38 possible events from birth to the time ofchild TL assessment.

## RESULTS

RESULTS

Prenatal maternal psychological distress and child T/S

Multiple regression revealed that higher levels of maternal psychological distress during pregnancy predicted shorter child TL after adjusting for covariates ( β = −.333, t (102) = −2.44, p = .017, including maternal psychological distress postpartum and at the time of TL assessment) ( Table 3 ). When the three indicators of maternal distress were evaluated separately, elevated prenatal depression, prenatal perceived stress, and pregnancy-related anxiety each predicted shorter childhood TL after accounting for covariates, although depression fell below standard statistical significance (see Table 4 ). Unadjusted correlations with prenatal distress and child TL reach the conventional cutoff for statistical significance ( r (102) = .14, p = .16). Analyses with only child age, sex, and maternal postpartum distress as covariates yielded similar effect sizes and significance as the model including all covariates ( β = −.399, t (102) = −3.08, p = .003).

Moderating effect of child sex

Sex did not moderate the relation between prenatal maternal distress and child TL ( β = −.07, t (102) = −0.496, p = .62). Consistent with previous published literature ( Gardner et al., 2014 ; Wojcicki et al., 2016 ), females had longer relative TL than males ( β = .23, t (102) = 2.3, p = .021).

## DISCUSSION

DISCUSSION

The current prospective study provides new evidence of a relation between prenatal maternal distress and offspring TL into late childhood. Using comprehensive assessment of maternal mental health indicators at five prenatal visits, we show that maternal prenatal distress predicts shorter TL during childhood. These findings persist after covarying sociodemographic (e.g., INR) and obstetric factors as well as exposure to maternal distress during infancy and childhood and child experience of life events, suggesting that prenatal exposures may play a role in childhood TL. Data indicate that prenatal maternal distress may impact telomere biology, which has implications for long-term health and longevity.

The present research builds on prospective studies demonstrating that elevated maternal distress during the prenatal period predicted shorter TL at birth ( Entringer et al., 2011 ; Send et al., 2017 ; Suh et al., 2019 ; Verner et al., 2020 ). Findings also are consistent with retrospective studies that observed shorter TL among adults exposed to stressful life events during fetal development ( Wang et al., 2017 ) as well as recent research linking prenatal distress to TL during infancy and early childhood ( Carroll et al., 2020 ; Suh et al., 2019 ). The present study expands prior work suggesting that prenatal distress predicts TL at least through adolescence.

As expected, we found that females have longer TL than males ( Gardner et al., 2014 ). However, we did not observe any sex-specific effects of maternal distress on child TL. Evidence for sex differences in offspring TL related to prenatal maternal distress is inconsistent in the literature ( Bosquet Enlow et al., 2021 ). Future studies should test sex as a moderator to improve our understanding of the conditions under which prenatal exposures contribute to fetal programming of telomere biology.

There are several plausible mechanisms by which prenatal maternal distress could impact telomere biology during the fetal period, including stress hormones, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Mothers experiencing elevated prenatal distress during pregnancy show atypical cortisol and placental corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) production ( Kane et al., 2014 ; Peterson et al., 2020 ), and cortisol exposure may mediate the association between distress and newborn TL ( Bosquet Enlow et al., 2019 ). Increased inflammation ( D’Anna-Hernandez et al., 2012 ; Osborne et al., 2018 ) and higher oxidative stress ( Eick et al., 2018 ) also have been associated with elevated maternal distress during pregnancy, and there is new evidence that elevated inflammatory markers in pregnancy predict shorter newborn TL ( Lazarides et al., 2019 ). Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis function, pro-inflammatory processes, and oxidative distress are interrelated ( Roomruangwong et al., 2017 ; Shelton et al., 2015 ) and it is likely that each of these pathways plays a shared role in the biological embedding of prenatal distress on offspring telomere biology.

The results of this study should be interpreted in the context of several strengths and limitations. Prospective examination of the links between prenatal distress and TL through childhood in the context of postnatal factors including maternal distress in infancy and concurrent to TL measurement in childhood as well as childhood life events and household income is a strength. Future research could include both the TL set point at birth and in childhood to characterize more comprehensively the relation between prenatal distress and TL development. We examined multiple indicators of maternal distress during pregnancy to provide a comprehensive characterization of distress during pregnancy. Finally, we assessed child TL using blood spot that is highly correlated to venous blood ( Stout et al., 2017 ). The current study also has some limitations. As with any correlational study, the ability to make causal inferences or rule out all potential alternative explanations for the observed associations, including genetics, is limited. TL is partially heritable ( Dugdale & Richardson, 2018 ), and the current study was unable to include maternal TL to capture shared genetic variation. There is evidence from observational human studies and experimental animal research that the prenatal environment has a measurable effect on TL, beyond genetic factors ( Broer et al., 2013 ; Hjelmborg et al., 2015 ). Additional evidence for environmental effects comes from an experimental study in rats that found that exposing pregnant dams to prenatal stress caused shorter TL in her pups ( Blaze et al., 2017 ). Among humans, a quasi-experimental study observed shorter TL among adults exposed to an earthquake during prenatal development ( Wang et al., 2017 ). Participants in this investigation were predominantly non-Hispanic White and Hispanic/Latinx and future research should include greater racial/ethnic diversity. Additionally, there was a relatively wide age range among the children included in this study that may have contributed noise to analyses given that telomeres shorten with age. However, we show in Figure S1 that in this sample, as expected, there is a linear relation between age and child TL supporting the validity of the adjusted models covarying age. Future research should address the possibility that the association between prenatal stress and TL differs over development. Finally, the current study measured relative TL rather than absolute TL. Although this limits comparability across TL studies, the qPCR method is widely used in epidemiological studies, is less time- and resource-intensive than other methods, and has a strong association with absolute TL measured using telomere restricted fragmentation (TRF) through Southern blot ( Aviv et al., 2011 ).

In summary, we provide new evidence that maternal mental health during pregnancy may have a programming effect on biological aging of offspring, a predictor of later health and longevity. Importantly, we observed a relation between maternal distress and child TL among a community sample of women at low sociodemographic and obstetric complications, suggesting that even subclinical levels of maternal distress may be important for offspring TL. TL biology is associated with morbidity during childhood and adulthood as well as longevity and thus identifying and providing treatment for elevated distress during pregnancy may mitigate some of the potential long-term consequences of maternal distress on offspring health and development ( Davis et al., 2018 ). There is some evidence that maternal resilience is protective against the effects of prenatal stress on TL ( Verner et al., 2020 ). Further research is needed to directly assess how interventions may benefit maternal and child outcomes.
