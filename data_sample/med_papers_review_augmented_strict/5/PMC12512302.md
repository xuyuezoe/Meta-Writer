# Association between atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD): a cohort study based on NHANES 1999-2018.

## Abstract

Background: Lipid metabolism plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD). However, the effect of the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP)-a novel biomarker for assessing lipid metabolic abnormalities and atherosclerosis risk-on MASLD-related mortality remains unclear.

Methods: This study included 6,567 patients with MASLD from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (1999-2018), with mortality data linked to National Death Index records through 31 December 2019. Participants were categorised into quartiles based on AIP. The association between baseline AIP and all-cause mortality (ACM) or cardiovascular disease mortality (CVM) were investigated using multivariate Cox Models, with restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves evaluated to assess potential nonlinear associations. Subgroup and mediation analyses explored modifiers and mediator (HbA1c, neutrophils, hypertension), while sensitivity analyses tested the robustness .

Results: During a median follow-up period of 122 months, 1,323 all-cause and 447 CVD-related deaths occurred. After adjusting for confounders, higher AIP was significantly associated with an increased CVM risk (HR = 1.34, 95% CI = 1.01-1.79, P = 0.05). No significant association was observed between AIP and ACM (HR = 1.05, 95% CI = 0.89-1.24, P = 0.50). RCS analysis revealed a J-shaped relationship between AIP and ACM (threshold = 1.71), with a significant increase in ACM risk when AIP > 1.71 (HR = 1.48, 95% CI = 1.14-1.92, P = 0.003). Subgroup analyses showed a significant interaction between AIP and age (Pfor_interaction = 0.018); among individuals 1.71 may warrant early intervention. Baseline AIP may be an effective predictor of future ACM and CVM in individuals aged < 60. However, further research is required to validate these findings.

## Background

Background

According to the latest guidelines of the 2024 European Association for the Study of the Liver [ 1 ], metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD) has replaced the traditional term nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), emphasising the central role of metabolic dysfunction, such as insulin resistance and obesity, in the pathogenesis of liver disease. The prevalence of MASLD continues to increase globally [ 2 ], with the 2024 Global Burden of Disease Study indicating that nearly 38% of adults worldwide are affected by MASLD [ 3 ]. Recent studies have shown that patients with MASLD have an increased risk of hepatic complications and a significantly elevated risk of cardiovascular disease mortality (CVM) and all-cause mortality (ACM), with CVD now being the leading cause of death among patients with MASLD [ 2 , 4 , 5 ]. Thus, identifying predictive biomarkers of cardiovascular risk in patients with MASLD is crucial for improving clinical management.

Lipid metabolic disturbances and insulin resistance largely drive the increased cardiovascular burden in MASLD [ 5 ]. Previous studies have highlighted that dyslipidemia, which contributes to atherosclerosis, serves as a crucial mediator in the elevated risk of CVD [ 6 ]. Persistent hyperinsulinemia exacerbates atherosclerosis by enhancing inflammatory responses and oxidative stress [ 7 ], thereby amplifying the risk of CVD development. Emerging evidence shows that, compared with individuals free of fatty liver or metabolic abnormalities, MASLD patients exhibit substantially higher risks of ACM and CVM [ 8 ]. Pustjens et al. further showed that prevalent comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, and hypertriglyceridemia, significantly amplify this risk [ 9 ]. Given the metabolic disturbances inherent in MASLD, further research is urgently warranted.

The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), a novel biomarker that reflects dysregulated lipid metabolism and increased atherosclerotic risk, has garnered considerable attention [ 10 ]. Calculated as the logarithmic transformation of the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (Log10[TG/HDL-C]), AIP serves as a composite indicator of atherogenic dyslipidemia and has been robustly associated with increased CVD risk [ 11 – 13 ]. Emerging evidence indicates a significant linear relationship between elevated AIP and increased CVD risk in individuals with MASLD [ 14 ]. Furthermore, AIP has been consistently recognized as an independent predictor of ACM and CVM, with this association robustly confirmed across the general population as well as among patients with conditions including diabetes and hypertension [ 11 , 15 , 16 ]. Although AIP holds potential prognostic value, there is a paucity of studies specifically exploring its association with mortality risk in the MASLD population.

The well-established pathophysiological mechanisms that increase the risk of CVM in individuals with metabolic abnormalities include elevated chronic hyperglycemia, inflammatory responses, and blood pressure dynamics [ 4 – 20 ]. This suggests that the effect of AIP on CVD may, to some extent, be mediated by comorbid conditions. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have explored the potential mediating roles of prevalent conditions, such as diabetes, chronic low-grade inflammation, and blood pressure dynamics, in the relationship between AIP and CVM in patients with MASLD.

Therefore, this study, leveraging data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), aims to: (1) evaluate the association between AIP and ACM as well as CVM among patients with MASLD; and (2) investigate the potential mediating roles of HbA1c, neutrophil count, and hypertension in this relationship.

## Methods

Methods

Study design and participants

The NHANES is a nationwide study designed to assess the health status of the US population using a nationally representative sample. The original survey protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). The study protocol was designed and conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [ 21 ]. The NHANES data were collected in biennial cycles, encompassing 400–500 health indicators. We included participants aged ≥ 20 with MASLD from 10 survey cycles (1999–2018). The following participants were excluded: (1) those lacking TG or HDL-C data ( n = 5,940); (2) those with missing components for the US Multi-Ethnic Fatty Liver Index (USFLI) calculation ( n = 26,546); (3) excessive alcohol use ( n = 142); (4) MetALD(Metabolic dysfunction-associated alcohol-related liver disease)( n = 300); (5) individuals with a history of hepatitis B or C ( n = 16) or liver cancer ( n = 4); (6) pregnant women ( n = 69); (7) those with missing mortality data ( n = 7) or clinical information ( n = 61). Ultimately, this study included 6,567 participants who completed a series of questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests either at mobile examination centres or at home (Fig. 1 ).

## Baseline indicators measurement assessment

Baseline indicators measurement assessment

Demographic information, physical examination findings, laboratory blood test results, and medical history were collected from the participants.

Demographic data included age, sex, race, educational attainment, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and poverty-income ratio (PIR). Race was categorised into five groups: Mexican American, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black, other Hispanic, and others. Educational attainment was classified into three levels: below high school, high school or equivalent, and college or higher. PIR was stratified into three categories: 0–1.0, 1.0–3.50, and >3.0. Smoking status was assessed using the question ‘Have you ever smoked at least 100 cigarettes in your lifetime?’ Participants who responded ‘no’ were classified as ‘never smokers’. Further categorisation was based on the question ‘Do you currently smoke?’, which identified three groups of smokers: ‘former smokers, occasional smokers,’ and ‘daily smokers’. ## Calculation of the AIP

Calculation of the AIP

AIP was calculated as the logarithm of the ratio of TG to HDL-C, as expressed by the formula: AIP = log10[TG (mmol/L)/HDL-C (mmol/L)].

## Definition of MASLD

Definition of MASLD

Hepatic steatosis was diagnosed using the USFLI, with a cut-off value of USFLI ≥ 30 indicative of simple liver disease (SLD) [ 26 ]. USFLI=(e-0.8073×non-Hispanic black + 0.3458×Mexican American + 0.0093 ×age + 0.6151×log (GGT) + 0.0249×WC + 1.1792×log(insulin) + 0.8242×log(glucose) − 14.7812)/(1 + e-0.8073×non-Hispanic black + 0.3458×Mexican American + 0.0093×age + 0.6151×log(GGT) + 0.0249×WC + 1.1792×log(insulin) + 0.824×log(glucose) − 14.7812)×100. Participants who self-identified as non-Hispanic Black or Mexican Americans were coded as 1, while all others were coded as 0. MASLD is defined as the presence of SLD in an adult patient who satisfies at least one adult cardiometabolic criterion in the absence of other underlying etiological factors that contribute to hepatic steatosis or excessive alcohol consumption [ 1 ].

The specific adult cardiometabolic criteria are defined as follows:

(1) BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (23 kg/m2 for Asians) or WC > 94 cm in men, > 80 cm in women; (2) FBG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) or 2-h post-load blood glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L (140 mg/dL) or HbA1c ≥ 5.7% (39 mmol/L) or a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes or type 2 diabetes treatment; (3) Blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or specific antihypertensive treatment; (4) Plasma TG ≥ 1.70 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) or lipid-lowering therapy; (5) Plasma HDL-C ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL) in men, ≤ 1.3 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) in women, or lipid-lowering therapy.

## Mortality outcomes of the study population

Mortality outcomes of the study population

To assess the mortality status of the follow-up cohort, we used publicly available mortality data files from the NHANES as of 31 December 2019. This dataset was linked to the National Death Index of the NCHS through probabilistic matching algorithms. International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, was used to define cause-specific mortality. ACM encompasses deaths from any cause. CVM refers to deaths caused by major CVD and cerebrovascular diseases (codes: I00 – I09, I11, I13, I20 – I51, and I60 – I69). Follow-up was considered complete upon the patient’s death or 31 December 2019.

Statistical analysis

Given the complex sampling design of NHANES, all analyses incorporated appropriate sample weights (representing approximately 22.6 million US adults ). Participants were grouped into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on AIP scores to evaluate the distribution of risk. Unlike continuous variable analysis, the quartile approach divides the data into balanced groups, highlighting effect size differences and making the results more intuitive and interpretable. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, while categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics across AIP quartiles were compared using one-way analysis of variance or χ2 tests, as appropriate. The cumulative ACM and CVM rates for each AIP quartile were ascertained throughout the follow-up period.

Missing covariate data were addressed using multiple imputations based on chained equations. Covariates were selected using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). To assess the association between AIP and the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality in patients with MASLD, we used multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age, race, and sex; Model 3: further adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, education level, TC, physical activity and PIR. The nonlinear relationship between AIP , ACM,

and CVM mortality risks was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS). If the relationship was nonlinear, threshold values were estimated by testing all possible values and selecting one that was most likely to be the threshold. Piecewise Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to explore the association between the AIP and the risks of ACM and CVM on either side of the inflection point.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the relationship between AIP and ACM and CVM across different age groups (< 60 years or ≥ 60 years), sex (male or female), smoking status (never smoked, former smoker, current smoker), alcohol consumption (never drank or light to moderate drinking), BMI (< 25 or ≥ 25), FIB-4 (low risk, intermediate risk or high risk) and diabetes status (yes or no). Additionally, mediation analysis using the Bootstrap method (‘Mediation’ package) was used to examine the potential mediating roles of HbA1c, neutrophils, and hypertension in the relationship between AIP and the risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. To ensure the robustness of the results, three sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) The robustness of the primary findings was further evaluated through a sensitivity analysis utilizing the fatty liver index (FLI). (2) to minimise the influence of reverse causality, we excluded participants who died within 2 years of follow-up in the sensitivity analysis; and (3) In the analysis, adjustments were made by accounting for the use of lipid-lowering and antihypertensive medications as potential confounders. (4) Survival analysis was performed using the Fine-Gray competing risks model, with event outcomes categorized as 0 = survival, 1 = CVM (primary event), and 2 = non-cardiovascular disease mortality (competing event). This approach accommodates competing risks and allows for the evaluation of independent factors influencing CVM, thereby mitigating the biases associated with the Cox model and yielding more precise risk estimates. The Fine-Gray model effectively disentangles the risks associated with different causes of death, thereby ensuring the robustness and reliability of the findings in the context of multiple competing mortality causes. (5) To assess the effectiveness of multiple imputation, we analyzed the distribution of baseline characteristics before and after imputation. Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.3), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Results

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants according to AIP quartiles

A total of 55,081 participants aged ≥ 20 years initially enrolled in this study, of whom 6,567 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1 ). During a median follow-up period of 122 months, 1,323 all-cause deaths and 447 CVD-related deaths were recorded. AIP was normally distributed in this cohort ( Figure S1 ). Among them, 3,618 were male (55.09%) and 2,949 were female (44.91%), with a mean age of 57 years. The baseline characteristics of the participants stratified by AIP quartile are provided in Table 1 . Compared with participants in the lowest quartile, those in the highest AIP quartile (Q4) were younger (median (quartile interval): 53 (40, 65)), with a higher proportion of males (67.30%) and a greater representation of Mexican Americans (22.23%). Furthermore, the prevalence of mild alcohol consumption (66.08%) and current smoking (19.61%) was higher in the higher AIP quartile than in the other groups. Significant differences in biochemical markers were observed between the groups. Participants in the highest AIP quartile displayed notable variations in TC, FBG, GGT, ALT, AST, DBP, neutrophils, FIB-4 and HbA1c levels compared to those in the first quartile (all P < 0.001). However, SBP and physical activity levels did not differ significantly.

## Discussion

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association of AIP with ACM and CVM in patients with MASLD. Our findings revealed that high AIP levels were associated with an increased risk of CVM. Compared with the lowest quartile, the highest quartile showed a significant 34% increase in the risk of CVD death. Furthermore, after adjusting for potential confounding factors, we identified a J-shaped relationship between AIP and ACM (threshold value = 1.71), with a notable increase in ACM risk when AIP ≥ 1.71. Mediation analysis indicated that HbA1c, neutrophil count, and hypertension mediated the association between AIP and ACM and CVM. HbA1c levels had the highest mediation proportions, accounting for 72.17% and 42.98%, respectively. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the results.

Several studies have reported inconsistent associations between the AIP and ACM and CVM in the general population. For instance, a cohort study of adults in the US identified a J-shaped, nonlinear association between AIP and ACM, with a significant increase in risk observed only beyond a certain threshold (AIP >0.0905) [ 11 ]. In contrast, another study reported that elevated AIP was significantly associated with diabetes-related mortality but not with ACM or CVM [ 16 ]. These discrepancies may stem from variations in population characteristics, follow-up duration, and the extent of covariate adjustment. In our investigation of MASLD patients, we identified a J-shaped association between AIP and ACM, whereas its correlation with CVM appeared weak. Elevated AIP values signify more severe lipid dysregulation and IR, potentially accounting for the observed threshold effect [ 27 ]. We further assessed the prognostic impact of physical activity in MASLD, which may partly explain this association [ 28 ]. Yet, even at lower AIP levels, mortality risk remained elevated, with the modestly higher risk in the lowest quartile (Q1) deserving particular attention. Low AIP often reflects malnutrition or metabolic derangements, thereby predisposing to adverse outcomes [ 29 ]. Wang et al. showed that the unfavorable long-term prognosis in patients with low non–HDL-C (< 2.2 mmol/L) was largely attributable to the effects of malnutrition [ 30 ]. Moreover, insufficient lipid supply, indicated by low AIP, may destabilize cell membranes and limit steroid hormone synthesis, thereby exacerbating hepatic steatosis, promoting atherosclerosis, and ultimately heightening mortality risk. Duiyimuhan et al. [ 15 ] observed that both low and high AIP levels were associated with increased risks of ACM and CVM. They proposed that low AIP may elevate risk through malnutrition or the “triglyceride paradox” [ 31 ], whereas elevated AIP may do so by accelerating atherosclerotic progression. Thus, heterogeneity in metabolic status and cardiovascular risk profiles across populations may underlie the prognostic variability of AIP, warranting further in-depth investigation.

Our study further revealed heterogeneity in the prognostic value of AIP across subgroups. In particular, a positive association between AIP and ACM was observed among individuals aged 25 kg/m2, underscoring the role of obesity as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Collectively, these findings underscore the need to consider individual characteristics such as age, sex, and BMI when evaluating the prognostic significance of AIP in MASLD, and highlight the potential value of targeted prevention strategies in high-risk subgroups.

Chronic inflammation is a key driver of MASLD and may precede hepatic steatosis [ 36 ]. Persistent low-grade inflammation and impaired hepatic metabolism promote progression to MASH and fibrosis [ 37 , 38 ], while also modulating lipid metabolism [ 39 ]. Similar lipid alterations across chronic inflammatory diseases, as reported by Feingold and colleagues, may heighten atherosclerosis risk [ 40 ]. Cytokines such as TNF, IL-1, and IL-6 disrupt lipid metabolism [ 41 ], and inflammatory cells are central to plaque formation [ 42 ]. Thus, as a marker of atherogenic dyslipidemia, AIP may interact with inflammation to amplify mortality risk in MASLD. In addition, MASLD is often accompanied by diabetes and hypertension. Both have been identified as mediators of cardiovascular outcomes in obesity [ 43 ], with hyperglycemia and hypertension inducing endothelial dysfunction through distinct pathways, accelerating atherosclerosis, and increasing adverse cardiovascular events. Our findings further suggest that dyslipidemia may raise cardiovascular risk through persistent hyperglycemia.

Although our study confirmed an association between AIP and mortality, the underlying biological mechanisms driving this relationship remain incompletely elucidated. Atherosclerosis may constitute a key factor contributing to the elevated CVM observed in patients with MASLD. Multiple studies have consistently reported that MASLD patients exhibit significantly higher coronary artery calcium scores and increased carotid intima-media thickness compared with the general population, demonstrating a dose–response relationship with cardiovascular death [ 44 ]. Dysregulated lipid metabolism promotes atherosclerosis, which in turn underlies the major causes of cardiovascular events, including stroke and acute coronary syndromes. Among patients with type 2 diabetes, those with elevated AIP exhibit markedly poorer prognoses than those with lower AIP, likely due to hyperglycemia and insulin resistance increasing the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [ 45 ]. A similar pattern has been observed in the general population [ 46 ]. Thus, AIP may serve as an independent predictor of cardiovascular risk. Furthermore, De Matteis and colleagues have shown that high AIP levels can aid in diagnosing severe hepatic steatosis [ 47 ]. Timely assessment and intervention based on AIP may potentially slow the progression of MASLD-related complications. Nevertheless, further research is warranted to more fully clarify and substantiate the association between AIP and mortality.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, the exclusion of a substantial number of participants due to missing USFLI or other essential variables may introduce selection bias, reducing the representativeness of the study population and thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the reliance on USFLI, a biochemical marker-based definition of MASLD, introduces the possibility of misclassification and does not account for potential confounders such as iron overload, genetic factors, and autoimmune hepatitis. Second, the determination of cardiovascular mortality based on self-reported data may lead to misclassification bias, as it lacks clinical validation. Furthermore, the AIP was derived from a single measurement of TG and HDL-C, without considering the longitudinal variability of lipids or the confounding effects of lipid-lowering therapies and metabolic fluctuations during the follow-up period. Lastly, despite comprehensive adjustment for covariates over a period spanning more than two decades in this observational study, residual confounding remains a significant limitation, impeding causal inference. Nevertheless, the association between AIP and both ACM and CVM remains robust, even after applying rigorous analytical methodologies.

## Conclusions

Conclusions

Our study indicates that baseline AIP exhibits a J-shaped relationship with ACM (cut-off = 1.71) in the U.S. MASLD population, with individuals having AIP > 1.71 experiencing a 48% higher risk of death. Moreover, the simple and cost-effective AIP index holds promise as a predictive marker for future ACM or CVM, particularly in individuals under 60 years of age. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings.
