# Burnout Among Chinese Anesthesiologists After the COVID-19 Pandemic Peak: A National Survey.

## Abstract

Background: Evidence has shown that large-scale pandemics can have prolonged psychological impacts on health care professionals. The current study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of burnout after the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) epidemic peak and to explore the prolonged impact of COVID-19 on burnout among Chinese anesthesiologists.

Methods: From August 2021 to October 2021, a nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted. Burnout was measured using the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Service Survey. Basic demographic information, exposure to COVID-19, and perceived institutional support were documented. Validated tools measuring mental health status, including anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and resilience, were also used to provide additional information on psychological distress.

Results: Of the 8850 anesthesiologists from the 218 institutions who were invited to participate, 6331 (74.93%) completed the surveys and were included in the analysis. A total of 52.7% (95% confidence interval [CI], 51.5-53.9) met the criteria for burnout. Depression, anxiety, and PTSD were positively associated with burnout. After pooled multivariate analysis adjusting for potential confounding factors, among the COVID-19 exposure parameters, redeployment outside normal professional boundaries remained associated with an increased risk of burnout (odds ratio, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.72-0.92; P = .039). Higher perceived institutional support and resilience could act as protective factors against burnout.

Conclusions: The impact of the COVID-19 epidemic on the psychological well-being of anesthesiologists still exists more than 1 year after the outbreak. Building better institutional support and cultivating stronger resilience may be helpful future intervention measures.

## Main text

KEY POINTS

Question: What is the current burnout status of anesthesiologists after more than 1 year of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic?

Findings: The burnout rate remains high.

Meaning: Improving anesthesiologists’ professional well-being requires substantial further work.

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outbreak was declared a public health emergency of international concern on January 30, 2020, by the World Health Organizaton (WHO). 1 Since then, COVID-19 has rapidly spread across the world. As of November 20, 2022, there had been over 637 million documented cases of COVID-19 and more than 6 million deaths. 2

Health care professionals across the globe have been faced with unprecedented challenges, both physically and psychologically. 3 A systematic review and meta-analysis identified a high prevalence of depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among health care workers during the COVID-19 pandemic. 4 , 5

Especially during the peak of COVID-19, fear for personal safety, inadequate training on infection prevention and control, shortages of personal protective equipment (PPE), fatigue, and emotional burden all led to negative psychological impacts among health care professionals, including anesthesiologists. 6

Anesthesiologists are at greater risk of being infected when performing aerosol-generating procedures such as endotracheal intubation. 6

Burnout was already an alarming issue among anesthesiologists before COVID-19. Evidence from the pre–COVID-19 era demonstrated a high burnout rate (69%) among Chinese anesthesiologists. 7

Burnout is not a problem of people but of the environment in which they work. 8 The structure and functioning of the workplace shape how people interact with one another and how they perform their jobs. A reduced sense of control over their own practice, undermined connections with patients and colleagues, and trouble with work–life integration all can lead to uncontrolled stress and eventually burnout. It has been previously indicated that global pandemics, such as the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-1 (SARS-CoV-1 outbreak, can lead to a chronic period of elevated stress even long after the pandemic has been controlled. 9 , 10

Now, more than 1 year since the initial COVID-19 outbreak, the burnout status of anesthesiologists is worth revisiting.

Whether aspects of COVID-19 exposure, such as caring for COVID-19–infected patients and having routine practice disrupted, will leave an impact among anesthesiologists remains unknown.

Therefore, we analyzed national representative survey data of anesthesiologists to explore current burnout status and to determine

if there was an association between COVID-19 exposure and burnout;

the level of coexisting mental health status, including depression, anxiety, PTSD, and resilience; and

if there existed an association between perceived institutional support during COVID-19 and burnout.

## METHODS

METHODS

Patient Selection Sampling Strategy

This study was approved by the university’s institutional review board (IRB #S-K1637), and written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. The trial was registered before patient enrolment at clinicaltrials.gov ( NCT04967820 , principal investigator: Yuguang Huang, date of registration: July 20, 2021).

To select a national representative sample of anesthesiologists, we conducted this survey under the auspices of the Chinese Society of Anesthesiology (CSA), which is the official academic society of the Chinese anesthesiologist organization affiliated with the China Medical Association. All 86 current national committee members of CSA from 81 institutions were invited to participate. A convenience sample of anesthesiologists covering 27 provinces, cities, and autonomous regions—including Shanghai, Yunnan, Inner Mongolia, Peking, Jilin, Sichuan, Tianjin, Ningxia, Anhui, Shandong, Guangdong, Xinjiang, Jiangsu, Hebei, Henan, Zhejiang, Hubei, Hunan, Gansu, Fujian, Xizang, Guizhou, Liaoning, Chongqing, Shaanxi, Qinghai, and Heilongjiang—were invited to participate. Considering that all 86 national committee members came from tertiary care hospitals, we asked each national committee member to nominate 1 to 2 nontertiary care hospitals in their geographical region to generate a more nationally representative sample. The tertiary:nontertiary ratio was set to 1:2 according to recently published data on the current status of anesthesiology departments in China. 11 To ensure a high response rate and to minimize bias, an institution coordinator was designated for each participating hospital. The role of the local coordinator was to distribute the questionnaire to all the working anesthesiologists and to provide feedback during the study. The survey was conducted from August 2021 to October 2021.

Questionnaire Design

The questionnaire contained 3 parts (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Supplemental Material 1, http://links.lww.com/AA/E132 ). The first part addressed sociodemographic and occupational characteristics, including many possible risk factors for burnout, such as age, academic position, workload, income, and marital status.

The second part explored COVID-19 exposure and perceived institutional support in terms of providing COVID-19–related knowledge and emotional and equipment support. Respondents were asked whether themselves, their relatives/friends, or known coworkers had been infected with COVID-19; whether they had cared for COVID-19 patients in person; and whether they had been redeployed to an unfamiliar position because of COVID-19. Furthermore, during the initial outbreak of COVID-19 in China, Wuhan being the source of outbreak, the delay in recognition of this disease and lack of effective quarantine measures caused a massive shortage of medical resources. Health care personnel around the country including anesthesiologists were dispatched to assist in Wuhan, the center of the COVID-19 outbreak, when little was known about the disease. We think that being in the forefront of the battle in Wuhan during the peak of the outbreak, with limited resources and increasing fear, may have seriously affected the incidence of burnout. Therefore, we included being in Wuhan during the peak of the outbreak as a parameter in our questionnaire.

The third part explored 5 aspects of the psychological well-being of respondents using validated tools, which we detail in the subsequent sections.

Burnout

Burnout was evaluated with a validated Chinese version of the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Service Survey (MBIHSS) 12 which includes a total of 22 items to evaluate 3 dimensions of burnout: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment. A 7-point Likert-type scale on each item was used, which was the same tool used in a previous study for the purpose of data comparison. 7 Scores were then categorized as low level (emotional exhaustion = 0–16, depersonalization = 0–6, personal accomplishment ≥39), moderate level (emotional exhaustion = 17–26, depersonalization = 7–12, personal accomplishment = 32–38), or high level (emotional exhaustion ≥27, depersonalization ≥13, personal accomplishment = 0–31). Burnout was defined as a high level on the emotional exhaustion subscale or a high level on the depersonalization subscale. 7

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder

The Impact of Events Scale-Revised (IES-R) has been proven to be suitable for the Chinese population, 13 and it was incorporated to evaluate the psychological impact of COVID-19. It is a 5-point Likert scale for which the total scores can be categorized into 4 different levels: subclinical (0–8), mild (9–25), moderate (26–43), and severe (44–88). Scoring above the threshold of 33 was considered diagnostic of PTSD.

Depression

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess depressive symptoms. The total score can be categorized into 5 severity levels: normal (0–4), mild depression (5–9), moderate depression (10–14), moderate to severe depression (15–19), and severe depression (20–27). 14

Anxiety

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) was used to screen for anxiety disorder. Respondents were asked to rate each item on a Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”); the items were summed for an overall score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores reflecting more severe GAD symptoms (no [0–4], mild [5–9], moderate [10–14], and severe anxiety [≥15]). The Chinese version of the GAD-7 (C-GAD-7) has been demonstrated to have acceptable reliability and validity. 15 A scale cut-off of 10 and above to represent GAD was chosen for the current study. 16

Resilience

The Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) was used to assess resilience. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always). Higher scores correspond to greater resilience. The CD-RISC-10 demonstrated good reliability and validity in a Chinese population. 17 Due to the lack of a recognized cut-off point, resilience was incorporated into the analysis model as a continuous variable.

Survey Administration

The survey was distributed through WeChat, the most widely used social platform in China. Each participating institution designated an institutional coordinator who was responsible for (1) reporting the number of anesthesiologists currently working and (2) disseminating the survey and encouraging all working anesthesiologists in their institution to participate.

Statistical Analysis

The difference between categorical variables of respondents with burnout and without burnout was evaluated χ 2 test. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to explain the associations after controlling for covariates. Anxiety, depression, PTSD were incorporated in the regression model as a continuous variable. The burnout rate was 69% based on a previous national survey in Chinese anesthesiologists. At least 1315 participants are needed to be recruited to provide an estimate of burnout rate with a 5% upper to lower bounds width of 95% confidence interval. We considered 2-sided P values of <.05 statistically significant. All of the analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL).

## RESULTS

RESULTS

Response Rate and Participant Characteristics

Among a total of 8850 actively working anesthesiologists from the 218 institutions that participated in this survey, 6631 (74.93%) completed the survey. The survey respondents were similar to the overall population for most demographic characteristics, including age, gender, and specialty, according to a recent national census survey. 11

## DISCUSSION

DISCUSSION

The psychological status of health care professionals has been increasingly recognized to profoundly impact workforce turnover 18 and the quality of patient care. 19 Burnout, characterized by a state of mental exhaustion, depersonalization, and a decreased sense of personal accomplishment, is found to be associated with suboptimal self-perceived patient care. 20 The COVID-19 pandemic has heightened interest in health care professionals’ mental health status. Large-scale epidemics with high mortality rates are known to cause prolonged negative psychological impacts among front-line personnel.

The current study demonstrated that anesthesiologists across mainland China reported a burnout rate of 52.7% (n = 6631) almost 2 years after the initial outbreak of COVID-19.

A total of 6.9% of respondents reported having personally treated patients infected with COVID-19, and 3.3% worked on the front-line in Wuhan during the initial outbreak. Both forms of exposure were not identified to be associated with burnout. Having known a colleague/friend/relative tested positive for COVID-19 is also not positively associated with current burnout. However, being deployed to unfamiliar posts due to COVID-19 is positively associated with burnout.

With the accumulation of knowledge regarding COVID-19 and the implementation of counteractive measures, initial stressors have also changed. Fear of contamination and infection of others may not be urgent, while disruptions in existing routines and everyday practice increase in importance to be the most influential aspect of COVID-19 exposure. Research indicated that anxiety felt by hospital staff is independent of whether the worker is directly involved in caring for or in any way coming into contact with COVID-19 patients. 8 The hospital workers felt anxiety because they were facing a new situation which caused changes in their work and daily routine. 8 Also, research done in surgical trainees who had been redeployed to intensive care units during COVID-19 indicated that over 50% of the respondents felt the redeployment had a negative impact on their mental health. 9

Coexisting mental health conditions are also a concern for combating burnout. In the current study, depression, anxiety, and PTSD were positively associated with burnout. This correlates with previous research showing that at the more severe end of the spectrum, burnout overlaps considerably with depression. 21

Identifying and treating depression and reducing anxiety may reduce the risk of burnout. Continuous and proper psychiatric intervention is needed. 22

Resilience has been suggested as a potential modifiable target to protect against negative psychological status. 23

Resilience represents the capacity of a person to successfully adapt to change, resist the negative impact of stressors, and avoid the occurrence of significant dysfunctions. It represents the ability to return to the previous, healthy condition after trauma or illness. The current survey demonstrated a negative association between resilience and burnout, similar to previous studies.

However, whether cultivating resilience and mindfulness in health care providers can assist in preventing psychological distress burnout needs further research.

Over time, a shift from the initial fear and uncertainty about the virus toward “COVID fatigue” was observed. 24 Disruptions in existing routines, including managing patients with more restrictions than in the past, can lead to emotional exhaustion and depersonalization.

Burnout harms not only patient safety but also workforce turnover. A survey of more than 1100 US faculty members found that 55% had seriously considered changing careers or retiring early because of the pandemic. 25 This topic has already been realized as an urgent issue in various specialties, and a series of suggestions have been proposed regarding how mental health can be protected and optimized.

Good institutional support during COVID-19 was shown to be a protective factor against burnout in the current study. It has been indicated that organizational leaders can have a large, positive impact on their employees during crises. 26

It is the team leader’s responsibility to provide clear communication, access to adequate personal protection and adequate rest. 27 Furthermore, to protect health care providers, initiatives at the national professional society level can also help, including (1) acknowledging and regularly measuring the extent of burnout, (2) establishing professional wellness programs such as resilience courses, and (3) educating and advocating through social media as well as plenary sessions at national meetings.

It is known that working under considerable strain can lead to medical errors and clinician burnout. A commission on global surgery recommends 20 specialist surgical workforce providers per 100,000 population to achieve safe, affordable surgical, and anesthesia care for all. 28 According to the most updated data, the density of physician anesthesia providers per 1,000,000 population is 5.12 in China. 29

We are undoubtedly understaffed. Advocating for an increasing anesthesia workforce and gaining governmental support to build a more sustainable anesthesia workforce team can potentially ultimately benefit patients.

It is comforting to see that COVID-19 did not significantly negatively affect anesthesiologists’ psychological well-being. While gratifying, it should raise alarms for administrators and politicians that our burnout rate remains high. Ensuring sustained psychological well-being for anesthesiologists and other health care providers should be a priority for all who care about our health care system.

Strengths and Limitations

Research targeting burnout has been difficult to compare and generalize due to inconsistencies in the instruments and criteria used to define outcomes. 21 , 27

Substantial differences in rates of burnout and satisfaction with work–life balance were observed by specialty. 30 To ensure consistency, this current national survey study implemented the most widely used burnout assessment tool, the MBIHSS. The same tool was used at baseline before the crisis to better compare the temporal changes. It should be noted that the cross-sectional nature of this study may not be applicable for observing causal relationships between the variables. Furthermore, the pre–COVID-19 burnout survey and the current survey, even using the same burnout assessment tool, are not from the same set of subjects; therefore, change in burnout rate over time cannot be tested.

## CONCLUSIONS

CONCLUSIONS

Now, almost 2 years after the initial peak, different aspects of COVID-19 exposure have been shown to have little effect on burnout in anesthesiologists.

Overall, although attention has been given to the psychological well-being of health workers, effective intervention is limited. 31

Governmental policy in favor of mitigating burnout should influence the design of interventions to protect health workers from psychological impacts, and such interventions need to have their efficacy evaluated and effects confirmed by future research.
