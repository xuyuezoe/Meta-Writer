# Adverse pregnancy outcomes and long-term risk of chronic kidney disease in women: national cohort and co-sibling study.

## Abstract

Background: Women with adverse pregnancy outcomes may have higher subsequent risk of chronic kidney disease, but the long-term independent risks and potential causality are unclear.

Objective: This study aimed to determine long-term risks of chronic kidney disease associated with 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes in a large population-based cohort, and to assess for familial confounding using co-sibling analyses.

Study design: A national cohort study was conducted of all 2,201,279 women with a singleton delivery in Sweden from 1973 to 2015, followed up for chronic kidney disease identified from nationwide diagnoses through 2018. Cox regression was used to compute hazard ratios for chronic kidney disease associated with preterm delivery, small for gestational age, preeclampsia, other hypertensive disorders, and gestational diabetes, adjusting for other adverse pregnancy outcomes and maternal factors. Co-sibling analyses assessed for potential confounding by shared familial (genetic or environmental) factors.

Results: In 56 million person-years of follow-up, 11,572 (0.5%) women were diagnosed with chronic kidney disease (median age, 61 years). All 5 adverse pregnancy outcomes were independently associated with increased chronic kidney disease risk. Within 10 years following delivery, adjusted hazard ratios associated with specific adverse pregnancy outcomes were: 7.12 for other hypertensive disorders (95% confidence interval, 5.88-8.62), 4.38 for preeclampsia (3.72-5.16), 3.50 for preterm delivery (2.95-4.15), 3.15 for gestational diabetes (2.53-3.92), and 1.22 for small for gestational age (1.02-1.44). All hazard ratios remained significantly elevated even 30 to 46 years after delivery (gestational diabetes, 3.32 [95% confidence interval, 2.96-3.72]; other hypertensive disorders, 2.44 [1.91-3.11]; preeclampsia, 2.03 [1.90-2.16]; preterm delivery, 1.56 [1.44-1.68]; and small for gestational age, 1.24 [1.16-1.31]). These findings were only partially (0%-45%) explained by shared familial factors. Women with multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes had further increases in risk.

Conclusion: In this large national cohort, women who experienced any of 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes had increased risk for chronic kidney disease up to 46 years later. Women with adverse pregnancy outcomes need early preventive actions and long-term monitoring to reduce risk of chronic kidney disease.

## INTRODUCTION

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide 1 and has a higher reported prevalence in women than men. 2 , 3 Women who experience adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm delivery, small for gestational age, preeclampsia, other hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, or gestational diabetes, have been reported to have higher risks of subsequent renal complications. 4 – 18 However, the long-term independent associations between adverse pregnancy outcomes and CKD risks, and their potential causality, remain unclear. Approximately 85% of women in high-income countries bear at least one child, 19 , 20 and up to 1/3 of all pregnancies are affected by at least one adverse pregnancy outcome, 21 thus nearly 30% of all women experience an adverse pregnancy outcome during their reproductive years. A better understanding of their long-term risks of CKD is needed to guide risk stratification, early preventive actions, and follow-up care to preserve their renal function across the life course.

Adverse pregnancy outcomes share certain pathogenetic features, including abnormal placentation, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, 22 that may cause persistent microvascular changes leading to renal complications. Most prior studies have examined a single adverse pregnancy outcome in relation to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) or, less commonly, CKD. However, few studies have examined multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes that were prospectively ascertained in the same cohort to understand their relative impacts on long-term risks. It is also unclear whether previously reported associations are explained by adverse pregnancy outcomes unmasking preexistent risk or eliciting new risk of kidney disease. Family-based designs that control for unmeasured shared familial (genetic or environmental) factors are needed to help further elucidate potential causality.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a national cohort study of over 2 million women in Sweden. Our goals were to: (1) examine risks for CKD associated with 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes (preterm delivery, small for gestational age, preeclampsia, other hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes) using prospectively ascertained data in the largest population-based cohort to date; (2) assess changes in such risks across the life course with up to 46 years of follow-up; and (3) assess for potential confounding by unmeasured shared genetic and environmental factors in families using co-sibling analyses. We hypothesized that women who experienced any of these 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes would have long-term increased risks of CKD, and that such risks would be only partially explained by shared familial factors.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population

The Swedish Medical Birth Register contains prenatal and birth information for nearly all deliveries in Sweden since 1973. Using this registry, we identified 2,201,638 women who had a singleton delivery during 1973–2015. To improve internal comparability, only singleton deliveries were included in the analyses, given the higher prevalence of adverse pregnancy outcomes and their different underlying causes in multiple gestation pregnancies. We excluded 359 ( 3 months postpartum. CKD was identified using ICD codes in the Swedish Hospital and Outpatient Registers ( ICD -9 code 585 during 1987–1996 and ICD -10 code N18 during 1997–2015). The Swedish Hospital Register contains all primary and secondary hospital discharge diagnoses from six populous counties in southern Sweden starting in 1964 and with nationwide coverage starting in 1987. 26 The Swedish Outpatient Register contains all diagnoses from specialty clinics nationwide starting in 2001. CKD had no specific ICD codes prior to 1987; to assess the effects on our results, sensitivity analyses (described below) were performed after restricting to deliveries in 1987 or later. In addition, ESKD ( ICD -10 codes N18.0, Z49, Z94.0, Z99.2) was examined as a secondary outcome. High positive predictive values have been reported for most chronic disorders in these registries, 26 but to our knowledge no validation studies have specifically examined CKD and ESKD.

Covariates

Other maternal characteristics that may be associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and CKD were identified using the Swedish Medical Birth Register and national census and diagnosis data, which were linked using a pseudonymous serial number. Maternal age was adjusted for in all analyses as the Cox model time scale (as described below). Covariates included the following maternal factors, with all time-varying factors updated for each pregnancy: calendar year of delivery (continuous and categorical in 5-year groups), parity (1, 2, 3, 4, ≥5), education level (≤9, 10–12, >12 years), employment status (yes/no) and income (quartiles) in the year prior to delivery, country of origin (Sweden/other), and other CKD risk factors 27 which included body mass index (BMI; continuous and categorical [ 99% complete for all other variables. Missing data were multiply imputed with 20 imputations using all other covariates and CKD as predictors. As alternatives to multiple imputation, sensitivity analyses were performed that (1) restricted to women with complete data (N=1,201,615), or (2) coded missing data for each variable as a separate category.

Statistical Analysis

Cox proportional hazards regression was used to compute hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for subsequent risk of CKD associated with specific adverse pregnancy outcomes. These associations were examined across the maximum possible follow-up (up to 46 years) and within narrower intervals of follow-up (<10, 10–19, 20–29, 30–46 years) among women still living in Sweden without a prior diagnosis of CKD at the beginning of the respective interval. All adverse pregnancy outcomes were modeled as time-dependent variables based on “ever” experiencing the respective outcome. For example, if a woman’s first delivery had no adverse pregnancy outcomes and her second was preterm with preeclampsia, she entered the preterm and preeclampsia exposure categories at the date of her second delivery. If a woman’s first and second deliveries were both preterm, she entered the preterm delivery exposure category at the date of her first delivery. 23 , 28 – 32

Maternal age was used as the Cox model time axis with age at each delivery as “time zero” (i.e., each singleton delivery was included as a separate observation, with exposures updated for each delivery). Women were right-censored at death as identified in the Swedish Death Register (n=85,352; 4%) or at emigration as determined by absence of a Swedish residential address in census data (n=92,658; 4%). For each adverse pregnancy outcome, two adjusted Cox models were performed that (1) adjusted for maternal sociodemographic factors, parity, and traditional CKD risk factors, and (2) further adjusted for all other adverse pregnancy outcomes. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by examining log-log survival plots, and no substantial departures were found. Incidence rate differences and 95% CIs, attributable fraction in the exposed (AF e ), and population attributable fraction (PAF) were computed for each adverse pregnancy outcome. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed that was restricted to each woman’s first delivery instead of time-dependent modeling of all deliveries.

Co-sibling analyses were performed to assess for potential confounding by unmeasured familial (genetic and environmental) factors that may be shared determinants of adverse pregnancy outcomes and CKD. Shared environmental factors in families may include lifestyle factors such as diet and physical activity, or ambient exposures such as passive smoking and air pollution. These analyses included all 1,191,043 (54%) women with at least one sister who had a singleton delivery (regardless of adverse pregnancy outcome status), and the same analysis was performed as the main analyses except for stratifying on sets of sisters. In the stratified Cox models, each stratum included a unique set of sisters identified by their mother’s and father’s pseudonymous serial numbers, modeled with their own baseline hazard function that reflects their shared genetic and environmental factors. Thus, these analyses examined associations between adverse pregnancy outcomes and CKD within each family, controlling for shared familial exposures. 23 , 28 – 32 In addition, these analyses were further adjusted for the same covariates as in the main analyses.

## RESULTS

RESULTS

A total of 667,774 (30.4%) women experienced at least 1 adverse pregnancy outcome, and 181,783 (8.3%) experienced at least 2 adverse pregnancy outcomes (not necessarily in the same pregnancy). The most common adverse pregnancy outcomes were small for gestational age (affecting 14.3% of women across all deliveries) and preterm delivery (8.8% of women). Table 1 shows characteristics of women with specific adverse pregnancy outcomes. Women with preterm or small for gestational age delivery were more likely to be younger at first delivery, have low education level or income, and/or to smoke. Women with preeclampsia were more likely to have low education level or income, high BMI, and/or to smoke. Women with other hypertensive disorders or gestational diabetes were more likely to be older at first delivery, have higher income or BMI, and/or to be a nonsmoker.

In 56 million person-years of follow-up, 11,572 (0.5%) women were diagnosed with CKD and 3,428 (0.2%) with ESKD. The median age at first delivery was 27 (interquartile range [IQR], 23 to 30), at CKD diagnosis was 61 (IQR, 51 to 69), at ESKD diagnosis was 53 (IQR, 45 to 62), and at end of follow-up was 53 (IQR, 43 to 64) years. The median follow-up time in women who did not die was 27 (IQR, 14 to 39) years. Absolute incidence rates for CKD by specific adverse pregnancy outcomes and follow-up time are reported in Table 2 , and cumulative incidences are shown in Figure S1 in the Supplementary Appendix .

Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes and Risk of CKD

Across the entire follow-up (up to 46 years after delivery), all 5 adverse pregnancy outcomes were independently associated with increased risk of CKD. After adjusting for all other adverse pregnancy outcomes and other covariates, the HRs for CKD associated with specific adverse pregnancy outcomes were: other hypertensive disorders, 4.00 (95% CI, 3.64–4.41); gestational diabetes, 3.85 (3.58–4.15); preeclampsia, 2.35 (2.24–2.46); preterm delivery, 2.00 (1.90–2.11); and small for gestational age, 1.25 (1.19–1.31). The fully adjusted HRs (as above) were moderately lower than those adjusted for maternal factors but not other adverse pregnancy outcomes ( Table 2 , Reduced model). When infant birth weight was more narrowly stratified, <5th percentile for gestational age was associated with a slightly higher risk of CKD in the mothers (adjusted HR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.22–1.39) than was <10th percentile (1.25; 1.19–1.31), relative to ≥10th percentile.

HRs for CKD associated with preterm delivery, preeclampsia, or other hypertensive disorders were highest in the first 10 years after delivery then subsequently declined ( Table 2 ). In contrast, HRs for CKD associated with gestational diabetes peaked at 20–29 years after delivery (adjusted HR, 5.31; 95% CI, 4.63–6.10), and those associated with small for gestational age peaked at 10–19 years after delivery (1.44; 1.27–1.64).

After longer follow-up time, the adjusted HRs for CKD declined but remained significantly elevated even 30–46 years after delivery: gestational diabetes, 3.32 (95% CI, 2.96–3.72); other hypertensive disorders, 3.32 (2.96–3.72); preeclampsia, 2.03 (1.90–2.16); preterm delivery, 1.56 (1.44–1.68); and small for gestational age, 1.24 (1.16–1.31). Figure 1 shows adjusted HRs for CKD by time since delivery for specific adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Incidence rate differences (i.e., excess CKD cases) associated with each adverse pregnancy outcome increased with longer follow-up to older ages. Across the entire follow-up period, other hypertensive disorders and gestational diabetes were associated with the most excess CKD cases (49 and 43 per 100,000 person-years, respectively; Table 2 ). Preterm delivery and preeclampsia accounted for the largest percentages (15% and 13%, respectively) of CKD cases in this population of women, compared with 6% for small for gestational age and <3% for other hypertensive disorders or gestational diabetes ( Table 2 , PAFs).

Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes and Risk of ESKD

The results for ESKD had a similar pattern as CKD except that most HRs were higher ( Table S2 in Supplementary Appendix ). Across the entire follow-up period, adjusting for all other adverse pregnancy outcomes and other covariates, the HRs for ESKD associated with specific adverse pregnancy outcomes were: gestational diabetes, 5.41 (95% CI, 4.82–6.07); other hypertensive disorders, 5.34 (4.60–6.18); preterm delivery, 2.90 (2.65–3.17); preeclampsia, 2.55 (2.34–2.78); and small for gestational age, 1.16 (1.06–1.26). With longer follow-up time, the adjusted HRs for ESKD declined but most remained significantly elevated even 30–46 years after delivery: gestational diabetes, 4.56 (95% CI, 3.61–5.75); other hypertensive disorders, 2.69 (1.56–4.62); preeclampsia, 2.06 (1.78–2.39); preterm delivery, 1.86 (1.56–2.22); and small for gestational age, 1.02 (0.88–1.18).

Co-Sibling Analyses

Co-sibling analyses to control for unmeasured shared familial (genetic and environmental) factors resulted in increases in some risk estimates and partial attenuation of others ( Table 3 ). Across the entire follow-up (up to 46 years), the adjusted HR for CKD associated with preterm delivery was 2.00 (95% CI, 1.90–2.11) in the primary analysis vs. 2.33 (1.95–2.77) in the co-sibling analysis, with small for gestational age was 1.25 (1.19–1.31) vs. 1.22 (1.06–1.41), with preeclampsia was 2.35 (2.24–2.46) vs. 2.53 (2.13–3.00), with other hypertensive disorders was 4.00 (3.64–4.41) vs. 5.04 (3.42–7.42), and with gestational diabetes was 3.85 (3.58–4.15) vs. 3.65 (2.65–5.03).

Secondary Analyses

Both spontaneous and medically indicated preterm delivery were associated with increased relative rates of CKD compared with full-term delivery (adjusted HR, spontaneous preterm: 1.93; 95% CI, 1.63–2.29; P <0.001; medically indicated preterm: 4.34; 3.88–4.86; P <0.001; Table S3 in Supplementary Appendix ), but medically indicated delivery was the stronger risk factor ( P <0.001 for difference in HRs).

Women who experienced multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes had further increases in risk ( Table S4 in Supplementary Appendix ). For example, at <10 years after delivery, the adjusted HRs for CKD associated with 1, 2, or ≥3 adverse pregnancy outcomes were 2.64 (95% CI, 2.24–3.11), 9.53 (7.95–11.42), and 17.79 (14.19–22.29), respectively, compared with women who never experienced an adverse pregnancy outcome. At 30–46 years after delivery, the corresponding HRs were 1.52 (95% CI, 1.44–1.61), 2.29 (2.10–2.49), and 3.67 (3.28–4.11).

Five of 10 possible two-way interactions between adverse pregnancy outcomes were positive on the additive and/or multiplicative scale ( Table S5 in Supplementary Appendix ). Preterm delivery had significantly positive additive interactions with preeclampsia, other hypertensive disorders, and gestational diabetes (i.e., their combined effect on CKD risk exceeded the sum of their separate effects, P <0.001 for each). These findings indicate that preterm delivery accounted for more CKD cases among women who also had preeclampsia, other hypertensive disorders, or gestational diabetes than among those who did not. In addition, gestational diabetes had positive additive interactions with preeclampsia or other hypertensive disorders ( P <0.001 for each). These interactions are shown in Figure 2 and in further detail in Tables S6 - S10 in the Supplementary Appendix .

When restricting to women with a first delivery in 1987 or later, most HRs for CKD were similar to those from the main analyses, except that the association between small for gestational age delivery and CKD was no longer significant ( Results S1 in Supplementary Appendix ). In sensitivity analyses that assessed alternative approaches for missing data or restricted to each woman’s first delivery, all results were similar to the main findings and the conclusions were unchanged ( Results S1 ).

## COMMENT

COMMENT

In this large national cohort, women who experienced any of 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes had increased risk for subsequent development of CKD. In the 10 years following delivery, the relative rates of CKD were elevated 7-fold in women with other hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, 4-fold in those with preeclampsia, 3.5-fold in those with preterm delivery, 3-fold in those with gestational diabetes, and 1.2-fold in those with small for gestational age delivery, after adjusting for all other adverse pregnancy outcomes and maternal factors. Most relative rates declined with longer follow-up but remained significantly elevated (1.2- to 3.3-fold) even 30–46 years after delivery. Co-sibling analyses suggested that these findings were only partially explained by genetic or environmental factors that may be shared determinants of adverse pregnancy outcomes and CKD within families.

To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date of multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes in relation to long-term CKD risks, and the first to assess for potential familial confounding. A meta-analysis of 23 studies 4 with >5 million total women (but only 5 studies that examined more than one adverse pregnancy outcome) suggested that women with preterm delivery had a 2-fold risk of ESKD, 5 – 7 and a subsequent study reported a 1.4-fold risk of CKD in such women. 8 Small for gestational age and/or low birth weight had discrepant prior findings that ranged from no association to a 2-fold risk of ESKD. 4 , 9 Preeclampsia and gestational hypertension, respectively, were associated with 2- and 1.5-fold risks of CKD, and 5- and 3.5-fold risks of ESKD. 4 , 5 , 7 , 10 – 15 Preeclampsia also was previously associated with a 3.7-fold odds of renal hospitalization. 34 Previous findings for gestational diabetes have been inconsistent, ranging from no association 4 , 16 , 17 to a 2-fold odds of any renal morbidity 35 and a 4.5-fold risk of ESKD. 18 However, prior studies had substantial heterogeneity 4 due to differences in outcome ascertainment, sample sizes, and/or follow-up times.

The present study extends prior evidence by determining CKD risks associated with 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes that were prospectively ascertained in a large national population, thus enabling well-powered assessment of their independent associations. The findings suggest that all 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes are independently associated with increased risks of CKD up to 46 years later. Other hypertensive disorders and gestational diabetes were associated with the most excess cases of CKD (i.e., incidence rate differences), whereas preterm delivery and preeclampsia were associated with the largest proportions of CKD cases in this population (i.e., population attributable fraction). CKD risk also was increased (nearly 2-fold) after spontaneous preterm delivery.

Women who experienced multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes had further increases in CKD risk, with some synergistic effects noted. Significant additive interactions indicated that preterm delivery accounted for significantly more CKD cases among women who also had preeclampsia, other hypertensive disorders, or gestational diabetes. Gestational diabetes accounted for significantly more CKD cases among women who also had preeclampsia or other hypertensive disorders. Early preventive interventions may be even more critical in women with these combinations of adverse pregnancy outcomes. 23

In co-sibling analyses, most associations persisted after controlling for familial (genetic and environmental) exposures that may be shared determinants of adverse pregnancy outcomes and CKD. Adverse pregnancy outcomes may be, at least partly, a signal for preexisting risk for CKD, but our findings are also consistent with potential causal relationships. Adverse pregnancy outcomes share key features that suggest shared pathogenesis, including abnormal placentation, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction. 22 Preterm delivery, for example, has been associated with endothelial-specific inflammation that was undetectable prior to pregnancy. 36 Both preeclampsia 37 , 38 and gestational diabetes 39 , 40 also have been associated with long-term endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness. Microvascular changes that emerge during pregnancy may progress or fail to resolve postpartum, 22 increasing the risk of future renal complications. Preventive interventions to interrupt these processes soon after an adverse pregnancy outcome may be key to preserving long-term renal function.

The present study’s findings have important clinical implications. All major adverse pregnancy outcomes should now be recognized as long-term risk factors for CKD. They should be routinely tracked in electronic health records to facilitate transition of patients from obstetric to primary care clinics where they need long-term clinical monitoring. Adverse pregnancy outcomes may provide an early window for identifying high-risk women long before the development of CKD, thus enabling earlier preventive actions. 41 Such actions should include counseling on avoidance of potentially nephrotoxic exposures (e.g., non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications) and control of other known risk factors for kidney disease progression, including obesity, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, anemia, and smoking. 42 Diabetes and hypertension are major risk factors for CKD worldwide, 2 and effective glucose and blood pressure control is critical for slowing disease progression. 42 , 43 Access to high-quality preconception and prenatal care is also a high priority to help reduce the prevalence of adverse pregnancy outcomes and their health sequelae.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of the present study is its large national cohort design with up to 46 years of follow-up. The availability of highly complete nationwide birth and medical registry data, both inpatient and outpatient, helped minimize potential selection and ascertainment biases. The large sample size enabled high statistical power for simultaneous assessment of 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes and combinations of these outcomes. The results were controlled for multiple other maternal factors, as well as unmeasured shared familial factors using co-sibling analyses.

This study also had certain limitations. First, detailed clinical records were unavailable to validate CKD diagnoses. High positive predictive values have been reported for most chronic disorders in the Swedish registries. 26 However, administrative data also have been reported to under-ascertain CKD. 44 The CKD prevalence observed in this study based on ICD codes (0.5%) was intermediate to previously reported estimates based on serum creatinine measurements in Swedish outpatients aged 18–44 years (0.3%) or 45–64 years (1.9%). 45 Second, outpatient diagnoses were available starting in 2001, resulting in some underreporting of CKD especially in earlier years. Consequently, the absolute risks of CKD associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes may potentially be higher than those reported. Third, this study was limited to Sweden and will need replication in other countries when feasible, including racially diverse populations to explore for potential heterogeneity of findings. The public health impacts may be higher in minority populations in the US that have more restricted access to postpartum care and higher rates of adverse pregnancy outcomes and CKD. 2 , 3 , 22

## CONCLUSIONS

CONCLUSIONS

In this large national cohort, women who experienced any of 5 major adverse pregnancy outcomes (preterm delivery, small for gestational age, preeclampsia, other hypertensive disorders, or gestational diabetes) had long-term increased risks for CKD. For each adverse pregnancy outcome, the relative rates of CKD remained persistently elevated up to 46 years later. All major adverse pregnancy outcomes should now be recognized as long-term independent risk factors for CKD. Women with adverse pregnancy outcomes need early preventive actions and long-term monitoring to help reduce risk of CKD.
