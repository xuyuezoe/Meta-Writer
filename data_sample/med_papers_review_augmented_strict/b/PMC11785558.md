# Polyphenols synergistic drugs to ameliorate non-alcoholic fatty liver disease via signal pathway and gut microbiota: A review.

## Abstract

Background: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a common chronic liver disease with an increasing incidence worldwide. Single drug therapy may have toxic side effects and disrupt gut microbiota balance. Polyphenols are widely used in disease intervention due to their distinctive nutritional properties and medicinal value, which a potential gut microbiota modulator. However, there is a lack of comprehensive review to explore the efficacy and mechanism of combined therapy with drugs and polyphenols for NAFLD.

Aim of review: Based on this, this review firstly discusses the link between NAFLD and gut microbiota, and outlines the effects of polyphenols and drugs on gut microbiota. Secondly, it examined recent advances in the treatment and intervention of NAFLD with drugs and polyphenols and the therapeutic effect of the combination of the two. Finally, we highlight the underlying mechanisms of polyphenol combined drug therapy in NAFLD. This is mainly in terms of signaling pathways (NF-κB, AMPK, Nrf2, JAK/STAT, PPAR, SREBP-1c, PI3K/Akt and TLR) and gut microbiota. Furthermore, some emerging mechanisms such as microRNA potential biomarker therapies may provide therapeutic avenues for NAFLD.

Key scientific concepts of review: Drawing inspiration from combination drug strategies, the use of active substances in combination with drugs for NAFLD intervention holds transformative and prospective potential, both improve NAFLD and restore gut microbiota balance while reducing the required drug dosage. This review systematically discusses the bidirectional interactions between gut microbiota and NAFLD, and summarizes the potential mechanisms of polyphenol synergistic drugs in the treatment of NAFLD by modulating signaling pathways and gut microbiota. Future researches should develop multi-omics technology to identify patients who benefit from polyphenols combination drugs and devising individualized treatment plans to enhance its therapeutic effect.

## Introduction

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a type of metabolic syndrome that is primarily defined as a clinicopathological syndrome caused by excessive fat accumulation in the liver cells as a result of alcohol consumption and other known liver-damaging factors. Recent decades have shown a rise in the prevalence of NAFLD, reflecting the rising global rates of obesity, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension and diabetes [1] . Due to its high incidence, difficult diagnosis, complex pathogenesis and lack of specific treatment strategies, the precise treatment of NAFLD is a major global challenge [2] . Research has identified a close interrelationship between the liver and the gut, which not only controls hepatic gut health and disease but also affects signaling between the gut and the host [3] . The gut microbiome belongs to a complex ecosystem, with a staggering variety of diverse microorganisms recorded in the host gut. It is estimated that there are more than 1014 gut microbiotas, making them 10 times more numerous than the body's cells [4] . The gut microbiota is intricately linked to overall health and plays an extremely vital role in the absorption and metabolism of nutrients, human growth and development, host immune regulation, nerve signal regulation, endotoxin removal, drug metabolism and disease treatment. Intrinsic or extrinsic factors may affect the gut microbiome, and a disrupted gut microbiota can have detrimental effects on the host's intestinal metabolic balance as well as cause inflammation in the body, leading to adverse reactions ( Fig. 1 ). Gut microbiota disorders often induce the occurrence of NAFLD, in which the ratio of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes plays a dominant role. Furthermore, the gut microbiome is interconnected with the immune system to induce cholestatic liver disease [5] ; Disturbances in gut microbiota promotes the development of fatty liver and exacerbate symptoms in various cirrhosis diseases, changing the ratio of inflammatory factors. Therefore, as an “invisible organ”, gut microbiota play an irreplaceable role in immune regulation and inflammatory response [6] . The interactions and linkages between the disease microenvironment and the gut microbiota are relatively complex and variable, and the occurrence of inflammation and disease frequently necessitate drug and other clinical treatments, with NAFLD being no exception [7] . The current means of treating NAFLD with pharmacotherapy include the use of insulin-sensitizing agents, antioxidants, lipid-lowering agents and other drugs [8] . Although these drugs all show different effects in treating NAFLD, long-term medication may disturb the microecological balance of gut microbiota. Therefore, research will increasingly focus on a number of bioactive compounds (polyphenols, flavonoids, etc.) to intervene in diseases or on the combination of polyphenols and drugs to treat diseases [9] . Polyphenols are natural compounds found in abundance in plants, including flavonoids, tannins, ellagitannins and phenolic acids, which are beneficial to the body and help fight disease [160] . As people's awareness of polyphenols' ability to resist inflammation and improve disease continues to increase, the demand for polyphenols in the food and biomedical fields has gradually increased [161] . Polyphenols market size is expected to grow from USD 1.02 billion in 2023 to USD 1.56 billion by 2028, at a CAGR of 8.72 %. The health effects of polyphenols on the body are mainly related to their biological structure, efficiency and bioavailability, which can be easily influenced by factors such as extraction techniques, processing conditions, enzyme treatments, and intestinal microbial composition [162] , [163] . Differential bioavailability of polyphenols was shown as phenolic acids > isoflavones > flavonols > catechins > flavanones, and proanthocyanidins > anthocyanin glycosides [164] . The metabolism of polyphenols by gut microbiota can increase their biological effects, and reasonable intake measures will improve their in vivo utilization [165] . Certain polyphenolic compounds undergo transformation into bioactive metabolites through host-derived enzymes upon entering the gastrointestinal tract. Others, which are not absorbed by the small intestine, may undergo fermentation and transformation by the gut microbiota. This dual process contributes not only to disease treatment but also to the maintenance of a balanced intestinal microecology [10] . Additionally, gut microbiota can metabolise polyphenols parent compounds into value-added compounds, producing high levels of short chain fatty acids, increasing the diversity of gut microbiota and enhancing its oral bioavailability [11] . The mechanism of polyphenolic compounds regulating gut microbiota to improve immunity and synergistically treating diseases with drugs will be the hotspot of future clinical research.

The occurrence of NAFLD will cause gut microbiota disorders and changes in intestinal permeability. The accumulation of a large number of harmful microorganisms will lead to an imbalance in the proportion of inflammatory factors, mainly manifested by the production of pro-inflammatory factors and the reduction of anti-inflammatory factors ( Fig. 2 ). In this review, we systematically summarize how the gut microbiota mediates the occurrence of NAFLD and the relationship, as well as the role of polyphenols and drugs in improving the condition. Simultaneously, the clinical mechanism of polyphenols and drugs coupled in the treatment of NAFLD, by balancing and increasing the diversity of gut microbes, was explored at the gene and molecular level. The correlation between gut microbiota and NAFLD, and some bottlenecks in research on the combination of polyphenols and drugs to treat and improve the gut microbiome, are summarised through previously published literature, and current challenges and future development trends are discussed. The review also provides the latest research progress on the clinical mechanisms through which metabolic diseases are likely to be treated by enhancing gut microbiota through the combination of active substances and drugs in the future. ## Methodology

Methodology

For article retrieval and screening methods, we follow the systematic analysis method. Literature search criteria: Data were collected through academic world include PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Mainly retrieved relevant papers published in the past 10 years (2013–2023). CiteSpace software (5.7. R5, 64 bit) was used for literature collation and summarization. Given that keywords are the core content of the paper, which are helpful to mining and discovering research hotspots in a specific period. In the present study, co-occurrence analysis of keywords (Gut microbiota; Polyphenols; Drugs; Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease; Mechanism) was performed to obtain the research trend in the field. Through literature screening, information extraction and quality evaluation, about 160 papers were finally collected.

Correlation between gut microbiota and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

Studies of the mechanism of NAFLD have predominantly focused on genes and metabolism, but no research has been capable of demonstrating the pathogenesis. With the expansion and development of metagenomics and microbiology in recent years, the innovation of sequencing technologies such as the 16S rRNA gene variable region, 18S rRNA gene variable region, shotgun genome and high-throughput DNA sequencing, many research have found positive or negative correlations between the occurrence of NAFLD and the gut microbiota [12] , [13] , [14] , [15] . It has become a hotspot of clinical research in recent years that is predicted to continue in the future.

There is an enormous number of gut microbes, and they can be considered to be a major organ of the human body, or even “another self”. Therefore, it is quite possible that future studies will reach the goal of co-treatment of multiple diseases by modulating gut microbiota. Patients with NAFLD have lower levels of the beneficial bacteria Lactobacillus , Bifidobacterium , Akkermansia and Clostridium in their guts, while the levels of harmful microorganisms Sarterella and Streptococcus were significantly increased. Additionally, the number of butyrate-producing bacteria decreases, pathogenic bacteria increase, and the intestinal microecology is disturbed. Generally, dyslipidaemia can cause the imbalance of gut microbiota, which leads to an imbalance in the abundance of Actinobacteria , Bacteroides , and Proteobacteria in the gut, eventually leading to lipid metabolism disorders [16] . NAFLD patients had greater numbers of gram-negative bacteria in their gut, with Bacteroidetes increasing by 20 % and Firmicutes declining by 24 % [17] . In addition, Klebsiella (HiAlc Kpn) is largely associated with NAFLD, and large amounts of Klebsiella can be isolated from the faeces of patients with NAFLD. Interestingly, when germ-free mice were given the Klebsiella microbiota, the mice developed NAFLD [18] . Erysipelotrichia and Gammaproteobacteria were also linked to liver fat accumulation in different individuals, and Erysipelotrichia abundance was positively correlated with the occurrence of NAFLD while Gammaproteobacteria abundance was linked to the reverse [19] . Studies of the treatment of NAFLD by gut microbes have mainly focused on Bacteroides , Escherichia coli , Clostridium sporogenes , Escherichia , Ruminococcus , Anaerosporobacter , Faecalibacterium , Allisonella , Parabacteroides , R. gnavu , C. boltea , Streptococcus parasanguini , Klebsiella pneumoniae , Faecalibacterium prausnitzii , Alistipes putredinis and Eubacterium eligens [20] , [21] .

The gut microbiota can aggravate or improve NAFLD through different pathways and mechanisms. For example, gut microbiota influences the efficiency of energy and fat absorption by regulating enzymes and regulatory factors related to lipid metabolism. An imbalanced gut microbiota can disrupt the synthesis and conversion of bile acids, thereby affecting the digestion and absorption of lipids in the body [22] . Furthermore, gut microbiota disruption causes damage to the intestinal mucosa, affects intestinal permeability, and stimulates the formation of a variety of bacteria, bacterial metabolites, and environmental toxins. Gut microbiota imbalance will allowing harmful substances such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) to leak into the liver, triggering inflammation and oxidative stress. Some Gram-negative gut microbes can release LPS as part of their normal metabolic activities. However, following intestinal bacterial translocation, the metabolite LPS enters the blood and liver via the venous system, prompting the liver to release inflammatory factors and promoting the development of NAFLD [23] . Because of the unique involvement of gut microbiota in NAFLD, it can be treated by changing and regulating gut microbiota. Overall, there is a strong link between the gut microbiota and NAFLD, and it can also be used to monitor and treat other disorders.

## Combination of polyphenols and drugs to treat NAFLD

Combination of polyphenols and drugs to treat NAFLD

Combination of polyphenols and anti-obesity lipid-lowering drugs

Drugs-polyphenols interactions in the context of NAFLD are an active area of research and while promising ( Table 3 ). Naruszewicz first reported that the combination of polyphenols with statins in the treatment of coronary heart disease significantly reduced serum LDL concentrations and inflammation after six weeks [63] . The effect of drug combination with polyphenols to treat NAFLD maybe connected to the inhibiting effect of cytochrome CYP3A4 and the transcription protein-mediated drug transport capacity [64] . Simvastatin at 15 μg/ml can reduce the cholesterol biosynthesis rate of macrophages by 42 %, and the combination of pomegranate juice and statins increases the inhibition rate to 59 %; When 240 mL grapefruit juice and 10 mg/d felodipine were combined, the bioavailability of the drug felodipine was increased due to the suppression of CYP3A4 in the intestine, which reduced the metabolism [65] . This interaction may be related to the inhibition of the CYP3A4 enzyme and CYP450 isoenzyme, which are both involved in drug metabolism [66] . Although rat experiments have shown that orange juice and apple juice may inhibit organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP) and therefore reduce the dosage of oral fexofenadine, many animal experiments have demonstrated that the interaction between orange juice and the drug has no significant effect [67] . In another study, the combination of high-antioxidant flavonoids and statins was found to be effective in the treatment of arterial atherosclerosis, reducing LDL, IL-6, and C-reaction protein (CRP) and, as a result, lowering the incidence of NAFLD. In rats with NAFLD, flavanol was found to raise blood simvastatin levels by decreasing the activity of hepatic OATP1B2 [68] . The combined use of simvastatin (40 mg/day) and flavanols (85 mg/day) in treating NAFLD can also improve dyslipidemia in hyperlipidemia mice, and this interaction may be mediated by OATP1B12. Additionally, flavonoids can stimulate the gene expression levels of OATP1B1 and OATP1B3 [69] . Finally, the combination of flavonoids and statins can affect the lipoprotein status, and daily intake of flavanol-rich chocolate has been shown to dramatically lower the ratio of LDL-C to total cholesterol in type 2 diabetics, which reduces the risk of cardiovascular disease [70] . ## Potential mechanisms for combination therapy for NAFLD

Potential mechanisms for combination therapy for NAFLD

Research into the pathogenesis of NAFLD has been piqued curiosity. However, due to the complexity of the mechanism, there are no clear conclusions about how it causes the disease. Among them, the most classic is the “two-hit theory”, this theory focuses on lipid metabolism disorder and insulin resistance, which contribute to the buildup of fat in the liver. In addition, the transition from the “two-hit” to the “multiple-hit” theory is also a research hotspot, mainly including oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and aberrant cytokines cause inflammation, which in turn encourages liver fibrosis and cirrhosis by impeding the liver's capacity to break down fatty acids ( Fig. 4 ). The main factors associated with the development of NAFLD include lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, oxidative stress and inflammatory response. These factors are frequently linked to several signalling pathways, including NF-κB, AMPK, JAK/STAT, PPARs, SREBP-1c, PI3K/Akt and TLRs. Additionally, some emerging mechanisms such as microRNA potential biomarker therapy and altering the gut microbiota may offer a therapeutic avenue for NAFLD. Therefore, blocking or modulating the expression of these pathways is a powerful strategy to prevent and treat NAFLD. Inhibition of NF-κB pathway and inflammatory response

Polyphenols fight inflammation by essentially blocking the NF-κB pathway and inhibiting IκB kinase (IKK) complex degradation [89] . Polyphenols such as flavonoids, apple polyphenols, kaempferol, curcumin, Morin and quercetin inhibit the phosphorylation of the insulin receptor and NF-κB pathway, and reduce insulin resistance and the pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6 [90] , [91] . Through a comprehensive review of transcriptomics and metabolomics demonstrated that polyphenolic extract significantly regulated the genes and metabolic pathways implicated in the production of NAFLD and downregulated pro-inflammatory pathways (cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway, NF-κB signalling pathway, NOD-like receptor pathway, TNF signalling pathway), up-regulated the anti-inflammatory pathway PPAR, and then regulated the expression of related genes (up-regulated Inhbb, down-regulated Tnfrsf21, Ifit1, Mapk15, and Gadd45g) [92] .

Activation of PPAR receptors increases β-fatty acid oxidation

PPAR is an important transcription factor that plays a key role in regulating physiological processes such as lipid metabolism and insulin resistance. The activity of this molecule is closely related to the development and differentiation of adipocytes and the formation of lipids in the body. It exists in three isoforms, PPAR-α, PPAR-β and PPAR-γ, each with different tissue distribution patterns and distinct functions. Studies has been been shown that activation of PPAR-α helps to alleviate adipocyte degeneration, and polyphenols show an even effect. For example, kaempferol, naringenin and glabridin stimulate the gene expression of PPAR-α, which can alleviate high-fat diet-induced obesity and reduce fat accumulation. Treatment with polyphenols is more advantageous for activating PPARs than other drugs, e.g. glitazone [93] . A mouse experiment revealed that polyphenols in loquat may increase PPAR-γ expression while decreasing NF-κB expression, and therefore limit the production of pro-inflammatory factors MCP-1 and TNF-α, indicating that polyphenols could prevent inflammation by blocking the NF-κB pathway [94] . Oral administration of 1 % tannic acid for 12 weeks reduced SREBP-1c and PPAR-γ levels in obese mice [95] ; Feeding proanthocyanidin B2 for 10 weeks in a high-fat model reduces the levels of PPAR-γ, C/EBPα and SBYP1-c [96] ; Cyanidin-3-glucoside at 1 mg/mL increased the expression of PPAR-α [97] ; Both 1 and 3 mL/kg bw/d kiwifruit seed extracts were shown to increase PPAR-α levels [98] ; A 50 mg/kg bw/d kelp enriched extract increased PPAR-α expression [98] ; Polyphenol-rich chrysanthemum extract inhibited fat accumulation and upregulated PPAR-α expression [99] . Carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT1), a downstream lipid oxidation target gene, is the rate-limiting enzyme in the lipid oxidation process and is regulated by the upstream factor PPAR [100] . In high-fat diet-induced NAFLD mice, tea polyphenols increased the mRNA and protein expressions of LPL, PPAR-α, CYP7A1, and CPT1, while they decreased the expressions of PPAR-γ and C/EBP-α in the liver. Furthermore, tea polyphenols increased the expression of occludin and ZO-1, while decreasing the CD36 and TNF-α in the small intestines of NAFLD mice, reducing liver fat formation, and preventing the development of NAFLD [101] .

This shows that the nuclear hormone receptor PPAR acts as a ligand-activated receptor that regulates various cellular metabolic processes. It can defend against inflammation by blocking the NF-κB pathway, while TNF-α contact with its receptors can activate NF-κB transcription factors. PPAR-α is involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism, specifically the oxidation of fatty acids. It regulates the expression of genes involved in the transport, uptake, and oxidation of fatty acids, and the production of ketone bodies. Enhanced fatty acid oxidation and lower blood triglyceride levels are linked with PPAR-α activation, while enhanced fatty acid oxidation and glucose uptake are connected with PPAR-β activation. Additionally, it controls how adipocytes and muscle cells differentiate and proliferate. PPAR-β is also involved in the modulation of energy metabolism and insulin sensitivity; PPAR-γ is involved in the differentiation of preadipocytes into mature adipocytes and the storage of triglycerides in adipose tissue. Activation of PPAR-γ leads to increased insulin sensitivity, decreased plasma glucose levels, and improved lipid metabolism. Furthermore, transcription factor C/EBP-α directly affects adipocyte proliferation, has a synergistic effect with PPAR-γ, and their expression has a positive feedback effect [102] .

Activation of AMPK and downregulation of SREBP-1c inhibits adipogenesis

AMPK is a major regulator of cellular homeostasis and is linked to insulin resistance, fatty acid production, and metabolism. Its activation can effectively limit fat accumulation and it is therefore a viable target for NAFLD treatment. SREBP-1c is a metabolic regulatory target of NAFLD, and insulin signalling stimulates fat and triglyceride synthesis by up-regulating SREBP-1c. AMPK activation can slow the course of adipocyte degeneration by suppressing the expression of SREBP-1c, consequently reducing the synthesis of cholesterol and triglycerides in the liver and effectively avoiding the development of NAFLD. ## Conclusion and prospect

Conclusion and prospect

There is currently no effective treatment available for NAFLD. Therefore, it is essential to continuously investigate the pathophysiology of the disease and discover targeted therapeutic strategies based on its pathogenesis. This review summarizes that polyphenols and drugs mainly interact with genetic pathways and gut microbiota to alleviate and treat NAFLD ( Fig. 5 ). The following may be effective ways to improve and treat NAFLD: The genetic pathways include inhibition of the activation of the NF-κB pathway, inhibition of the inflammatory response, up-regulation of PPAR-α to increase β-FAO, activation of AMPK and down-regulation of SREBP-1c to inhibit adipogenesis, stimulation of the Nrf2 pathway to enhance antioxidant defence, lipid autophagocytosis and autophagy, and potential biomarker therapy. These pathways reduce fat formation and oxidative damage while also inhibiting inflammatory factor synthesis. The gut microbiota influences NAFLD by increasing helpful bacteria and decreasing harmful bacteria via the gut-liver axis. However, some of the challenges faced with polyphenol combination drugs to treat NAFLD include: the low bioavailability of polyphenols, which may limit their effectiveness in treating NAFLD; determining the optimal dose of polyphenol combinations for the treatment of NAFLD can be challenging, and there is currently no consensus on the most effective dose or formulation; the safety and interactions are debatable. This challenging area of research may involve several issues: 1) Interactions: different polyphenols and drugs may interact with each other, affecting their absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion in the body. This may lead to adverse reactions or reduced therapeutic efficacy. 2) Drug metabolism: Polyphenols can affect the metabolism and clearance rate of drugs by affecting the activity of drug metabolizing enzymes, such as cytochrome P450 enzymes. This may lead to fluctuations in drug concentration in the body, increasing toxicity or reducing efficacy. 3) Adverse reactions: polyphenols and certain drugs may lead to allergic reactions or other adverse reactions, and attention needs to be paid to the patient's tolerance of these substances in combination therapy. 4) Dosage issues: Dosage selection of polyphenols and drugs is critical to therapeutic efficacy and safety, and inappropriate dosage may lead to adverse reactions or diminish therapeutic efficacy. 5) Long-term safety: safety data on the long-term use of polyphenols and drugs may be limited, especially in the context of treating NAFLD. Three potential solutions are proposed to address these challenges. Firstly, strategies to improve the bioavailability of polyphenols using nanotechnology, microencapsulation, and the development of prodrugs that can be converted to active polyphenols in vivo could be pursued. Secondly, the development of standardized dosing regimens for polyphenol combinations, which would help ensure that patients receive the optimal dose for their specific condition. Finally, the evaluation of the safety of polyphenol combinations for the treatment of NAFLD, using drugs that have been shown to have low side effects. In the future, multi-omics technology is needed to identify patients who may benefit most from polyphenol combination drugs, and develop individualized treatment plans for NAFLD patients to enhance their therapeutic effects. In addition, further studies are needed to determine the long-term efficacy of polyphenols combined with drugs in the treatment of NAFLD, which will help to improve the long-term treatment strategies for NAFLD patients. Future research will focus on the combination of polyphenols and drugs in the treatment of NAFLD, and how their interaction mediates transcription factors and gut microbiota.
