# Peripheral blood inflammatory biomarkers dynamics reflect treatment response and predict prognosis in non-small cell lung cancer patients with neoadjuvant immunotherapy.

## Abstract

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy has significantly changed the therapeutic approach for treating patients with surgically resectable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Here, peripheral blood inflammation-based biomarkers as well as previously less focused eosinophil fraction, modified Glasgow prognostic score (mGPS), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI) were systematically included to comprehensively analyze their potential in predicting neoadjuvant immunotherapy efficacy and prognosis. We enrolled 189 patients (94 in training and 95 in validation cohorts) with stage I-III B surgically resectable NSCLC treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy from the National Cancer Center of China. Baseline and post-treatment eosinophils fraction, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), PNI, mGPS, and their changes were calculated and analyzed for correlation with neoadjuvant immunotherapy efficacy and prognosis. In patients in the major pathological response (MPR) group, the post-treatment eosinophil fraction was significantly high, and NLR, PLR, SII, and MLR were significantly lower compared to the non-MPR group in both the training and validation cohorts. The receiver operating characteristic curve showed that post-treatment, eosinophil fraction and SII and their changing were two of the most important factors. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses showed that post-treatment eosinophil fraction, SII, mGPS, and ΔSII could independently predict MPR in patients treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Survival analysis showed a significant correlation between high post-treatment NLR, PLR, SII, mGPS, and their changes in ΔNLR and ΔSII elevation with poor overall survival and event-free survival of patients. Our results suggest that inflammatory biomarkers could predict the patient's response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy and prognosis.

## INTRODUCTION

INTRODUCTION

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) 2020 ranked lung cancer (LC) among the most diagnosed cancers. It accounts for around 18% of deaths due to cancer. 1 The most prevalent pathological subtype of LC is non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). More than 60% of newly diagnosed NSCLC patients have local or distant metastases, 2 , 3 and 30%–55% of patients who undergo curative surgery relapse and eventually succumb to the disease. 4 Following targeted therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), represented by programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) and its ligand PD‐L1, have revitalized immune anti‐tumor therapy and are gradually becoming the standard treatment strategy for NSCLC patients, with approximately 20% of patients demonstrating good response rates to ICIs. 5 Previously, immunotherapy was used as palliative care in patients who underwent surgery or in patients with advanced unresectable NSCLC. However, recent studies have demonstrated high major pathological response (MPR) rates and longer survival duration of patients with non‐metastatic surgically resectable treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 However, only a few patients benefit from immunotherapy. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and PD‐L1 are static biomarkers that are widely used for predicting the response of patients to immunotherapy. However, these biomarkers fail to accurately predict the patient's response to treatment. Moreover, they cannot predict the patient's prognosis. 10 , 11 Therefore, developing more convenient and effective biomarkers to predict the patient's response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy and their prognosis is necessary.

Mounting evidence has shown the impact of the immune system and inflammatory factors on the proliferation, differentiation, and metastasis of cancer cells, 12 and ICIs can stimulate tumor antigen release and antigen‐presenting cell (APC) function thereby allowing immune cells to exert anti‐tumor effects and improve patient prognosis. 13 , 14 Previous studies have shown that the neutrophil‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR) could effectively predict the prognosis of patients with solid tumors, such as breast and colorectal cancers and esophageal squamous cell and hepatocellular carcinoma. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Moreover, the NLR and the systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII) could predict a patient's response to immunotherapy and survival in various advanced solid tumors. 19 , 20 , 21 In regard to the molecular mechanism, high peripheral neutrophil counts indicate inflammation, which impairs immune functions, and lymphocyte levels indicate healthy cytotoxic T‐cell response. 22 Hwang et al. (2022) showed that eosinophils could predict the clinical outcomes of patients with advanced NSCLC undergoing immunotherapy. 23 Moreover, the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) based on blood biochemistry could predict the prognosis of patients with pulmonary neuroendocrine tumors, colorectal cancer, and esophageal squamous carcinoma. 24 , 25 , 26 The modified Glasgow prognostic score (mGPS) based on serum C‐reactive protein (CRP) and albumin is a potential marker for disease prognosis and immunotherapy. 27 Therefore, blood‐based markers could predict the disease progression in certain cancers in an accurate manner. However, these markers in neoadjuvant immunotherapy have not been investigated. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have used eosinophil, NLR, PLR, SII, and MLR as indicators to determine the response of patients with NSCLC to neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Li et al. (2023) demonstrated that SII could effectively predict the response of patients with NSCLC to neoadjuvant immunotherapy. 28 However, this study did not include eosinophils, PNI, mGPS, and the relative changes in inflammatory markers and failed to establish the correlation between the markers and patients’ prognoses. Including pre‐and post‐treatment inflammatory biomarkers and prognostic nutritional indices to comprehensively explore the correlation between the response of patients with NSCLC to neoadjuvant immunotherapy and their prognosis is necessary.

Therefore, in this study, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical data and peripheral blood indicators collected from 189 patients with NSCLC who underwent neoadjuvant immunotherapy. First, these patients were divided into a training cohort (TC, n = 94) and a validation cohort (VC, n = 95). Next, pre‐ and post‐treatment eosinophil fraction, NLR, PLR, SII, MLR, PNI, and mGPS were calculated for all patients. Further, we determined the potential of these biomarkers to predict the efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Finally, we analyzed the correlation between these markers and the event‐free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) of patients. These will help identify effective biomarkers for screening patients with NSCLC who would benefit from neoadjuvant immunotherapy.

## METHODS AND MATERIALS

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Study design and patients

We enrolled 189 patients with NSCLC undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy at the National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Beijing, China, between March 2019 and October 2022. The medical records of these patients were analyzed. All patients with stage I–IIIB NSCLC who underwent surgical resection after neoadjuvant immunotherapy were divided into TC ( n = 94) and VC ( n = 95). Patients in both cohorts were fully evaluated as surgically resectable by positron emission computed tomography (PET‐CT) preoperatively and received one cycle of anti‐PD1 inhibitor + platinum‐based chemotherapy every 3 weeks preoperatively. Patients underwent surgery after 3–4 weeks of drug withdrawal. The key inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (i) patients with stage I–IIIB (limited to N2) NSCLC diagnosed histologically and using PET‐CT; (ii) patients with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group status score between 0 and 1 and between 18 and 75 years; (iii) patients not receiving systemic chemotherapy, radiation, surgery, or targeted therapy; and (iv) patients not harboring EGFR, ALK, and other driver gene mutations and with normal organ functions. The primary exclusion criteria were inoperable tumor post‐treatment or due to clinical accident, immunodeficiency, currently undergoing treatment with systemic immunosuppressive therapy, and active autoimmune or infectious diseases. A flowchart for the study is presented in Figure 1 . All procedures performed in the study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the NCC/Cancer Hospital Ethics Committee. All patients provided written informed consent.

The flowchart of this study. ECOG, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status; PET‐CT, positron emission tomography–computed tomography.

Assessments

Routine H&E staining was applied by the pathologist to identify the percentage of residual surviving tumor from the primary tumor. Responders and non‐responders were identified based on the pathological response rate after surgical excision. Patients with residual tumor ≤10% after surgical resection were classified as the response group, and the remaining patients were classified as the non‐response group. In addition, clinical staging and radiological responses were evaluated based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1. 29

Analysis of peripheral blood laboratory data

The eosinophil fraction, absolute neutrophil counts (ANC), absolute lymphocyte counts (ALC), absolute platelet and monocyte counts, and albumin levels (g/L) of patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy were recorded pre‐ and post‐4–6 weeks of treatment. NLR was calculated as the ratio of ANC and ALC, PLR as platelet counts to ALC ratio, and SII as the absolute platelet count multiplied by NLR. MLR was calculated as the absolute monocyte count/ALC; albumin level (g/L) + 5 × ALC was defined as PNI. Additionally, the relative changes in eosinophil fraction, NLR, PLR, SII, MLR, and PNI pre‐ and post‐treatment were defined as the ratio between the corresponding time points and the markers pretreatment, referred to as Δeosinophil, ΔNLR, ΔPLR, ΔSII, ΔMLR, and ΔPNI, respectively. In addition, based on previous studies, 30 patients were assigned mGPS = 0 if normal serum C‐reactive protein (CRP, ≤10 mg/L) and albumin levels (≥35 g/L) were observed. Patients were assigned mGPS = 1 if elevated CRP without hypoalbuminemia (CRP > 10 mg/L and albumin ≥35 g/L) was diagnosed. If CRP >10 mg/L and albumin <35 g/L, mGPS = 2. In this study, mGPS = 0 was defined as the low mGPS group, and mGPS = 1, 2 was defined as the high mGPS group.

Survival

According to the study protocol, NSCLC patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy underwent computed tomography scans of the chest and abdominopelvic cavity every 8–12 weeks and were assessed for disease progression based on treatment. Primary inclusion in the survival analysis was the patients’ 3‐year OS and 3‐year EFS. OS was defined as the date of the start of neoadjuvant immunotherapy treatment for the patient to the event of death from any cause or observational cessation. EFS was defined as the time of treatment with neoadjuvant immunotherapy to the occurrence of any event, including disease progression, discontinuation of treatment from any cause, or death.

Statistical analyses

We employed SPSS 27.0 software (New York, USA) to statistically analyze the clinical data of the patients. We generated heatmaps, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and survival curves using the R (version 3.6.3) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, USA) software. The categorical and continuous data of patients in the MPR and non‐MPR groups were compared using Fisher's exact test and the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U ‐test, respectively. Spearman's correlation coefficient was assessed between continuous variables and immunotherapy MPR. ROC curves were plotted to determine the area under the curve (AUC) values. The highest Youden's index was used to calculate the optimal cut‐off values of eosinophil, NLR, SII, PLR, MLR, PNI, and mGPS. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the factors predicting MPR. We calculated and compared the OS and EFS of patients using the Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis and log‐rank tests, respectively. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to determine the factors predicting the prognosis of patients in the neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohort. Following univariate analysis, clinicopathologic characteristics (staging, histology, gender, age, and other covariates) were included in the model together to conduct multivariate regression analysis according to clinical plausibility, choosing the forward method, selecting the optimal variables, and fitting the optimal model based on the input p < 0.05 and the output p < 0.1. Further, according to the results of multicollinearity, there is no covariance between the influencing factors in this study, and the results of multivariate regression analysis meet the statistical requirements (Table S1 ).

## RESULTS

RESULTS

Characteristics of patients

We enrolled 189 patients with NSCLC undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy. These patients were classified into TC ( n = 94) and VC ( n = 95) randomly. All patients in these cohorts were further divided into MPR and non‐MPR groups based on postoperative MPR status (Table 1 ).

The results showed no significant difference in the BMI and age of patients between TC and VC. Furthermore, 80.8% of patients in TC and 87.4% of patients in VC were men. However, a significant difference in the history of smoking was observed in VC. The pathological type of 74.5% of patients in TC and 72.6% in VC treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy was squamous cell carcinoma. The analysis showed a significantly high MPR rate in patients with squamous cell carcinoma compared to adenocarcinoma. No significant difference in the location of tumor growth was observed between both cohorts. The clinical stages of most patients were II‐IIIA. In TC, 41 (43.6%) patients achieved MPR, of which 34 (82.9% of all MPR in TC) had radiologic PR/CR status. Similar results were observed in patients in the VC. These results were significant in both cohorts ( p < 0.01). Postoperative pathological staging was significantly shrunk in both cohorts. Additionally, 27.7% of patients in the TC and 16.8% in the VC achieved complete pathological response (Table 1 ).

Based on MPR and non‐MPR, the distribution of eosinophil fraction, NLR, PLR, SII, MLR, and PNI pre‐ and post‐treatment were similar in patients in both cohorts (Figure 2 ). In patients in TC, the results revealed no difference in the pre‐treatment eosinophil fraction between the MPR and non‐MPR groups; however, the post‐treatment eosinophil fraction was significantly higher in patients in the MPR group (median 5.80 vs. 1.30, p 0.05) was observed in patients in the MPR group compared to the non‐MPR group. However, no significant increase in the post‐treatment period PNI was observed (Figure 2A ). Similarly, in patients in VC, the post‐treatment eosinophil fraction was significantly higher, and the post‐treatment NLR, PLR, SII, and MLR were significantly lower in the MPR group compared to the non‐MPR group. However, no significant difference in PNI was observed in patients (Figure 2B ).

Next, we determined the correlation between pre‐ and post‐treatment changes in inflammatory markers and PNI and MPR in patients. In TC, 63.6% of patients with increased eosinophil fraction (fold change <1) achieved MPR, and only 15.4% of the remaining patients achieved MPR ( p < 0.001). In contrast, the patients with reduced NLR (28.6 vs. 60.0, p = 0.002), SII (28.6 vs. 60.0, p = 0.002), and MLR (34.8 vs. 64.3, p = 0.008) had a higher probability of MPR. However, the results revealed no significant differences in PLR and PNI. Moreover, a significant difference in PLR existed in patients in VC (Table S2 ). Figure 3 shows the heatmap of the distribution characteristics between Δeosinophil, ΔNLR, ΔPLR, ΔSII, ΔMLR, ΔPNI, and MPR. We can see that most patients with elevated eosinophils clustered in the MPR group, and elevated NLR, PLR, SII and MLR tended to cluster in the non‐MPR group. The results revealed that TC and VC showed similar frequencies. Since mGPS is categorical data, it was not included in the above analysis.

Ability of inflammatory markers to predict major pathological response and optimal cut‐off value

Based on the differential distribution of inflammatory markers in the neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohort, we constructed ROC curves to determine if the pre‐ and post‐treatment changes in biomarkers could predict MPR. In TC, the pretreatment inflammatory biomarkers and PNI could not predict MPR in patients undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy (the AUC values between 0.486 and 0.596, Figure 4A ). The post‐treatment ROC curves showed that the AUC values for both eosinophil fraction and SII were 0.853/0.798 (95% CI 0.802–0.908/0.728–0.857), NLR was 0.727 (95% CI 0.664–0.808), PLR was 0.734 (95% CI 0.633–0.779), MLR was 0.672 (95% CI 0.612–0.761), and PNI was 0.552 (95% CI 0.456–0.624, Figure 4B ). Additionally, the AUC values of pre‐ and post‐treatment Δeosinophil were 0.819 (95% CI 0.802–0.908), ΔNLR was 0.723 (95% CI 0.664–0.808), ΔPLR was 0.664 (95% CI 0.633–0.779), ΔSII was 0.757 (95% CI 0.728–0.857), ΔMLR was 0.675 (95% CI 0.612–0.761), and ΔPNI was 0.522 (95% CI 0.456–0.624, Figure 4C ). A similar trend was observed in patients in VC. These results indicated an increase in the ability of PLR, MLR, NLR, SII, and eosinophils in predicting MPR post‐treatment. Similarly, the ability of Δeosinophil, ΔNLR, ΔPLR, ΔSII, and ΔPLR in predicting MPR in patients was strong; however, the predictive ability of ΔPNI was weak (Figure 4D–F ). In addition, mGPS is a categorical variable, so it is not included in the ROC curve.

Next, to stratify patients based on MPR, the optimal cut‐off values of inflammatory biomarkers and PNI were calculated using ROC curves and the Youden index in TC. The optimal cut‐off values of pre‐treatment inflammatory biomarkers and PNI were not very specific or sensitive. The optimal cut‐off values for eosinophil fraction, NLR, PLR, SII, MLR, and PNI post‐treatment were 3.35, 2.43, 157.29, 673.72, 0.35, and 54.5, respectively. The sensitivity and specificity for eosinophil fraction were 0.75 and 0.83 and for SII were 0.86 and 0.65, respectively. The optimal cut‐off values for Δeosinophil, ΔNLR, ΔPLR, ΔSII, ΔPLR, and ΔPNI were 1.33, 1.12, 0.93, 0.80, 1.23, and 1.05, respectively (Table S3 ).

As per the optimal cut‐off value, we classified patients in TC into high and low groups. Next, the factors predicting MPR were determined using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that pathologic type, post‐treatment eosinophil fraction, NLR, PLR, SII, MLR, mGPS, Δeosinophil, ΔNLR, ΔPLR, ΔSII, and ΔMLR were predictors of MPR in TC and VC (Table 2 ). Furthermore, multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that post‐treatment eosinophil fraction, SII, mGPS, and ΔSII remained independent predictors of the TC cohort. In VC, pathologic type, post‐treatment SII, mGPS, and Δ SII, Δ PLR were independent predictors of MPR (Table S4 ).

## DISCUSSION

DISCUSSION

Mounting evidence has shown that chronic inflammation and evasion of immune surveillance are characteristics of cancer cells. Inflammation associated with cancer primarily affects the disease progression and survival of patients with multiple types of solid tumors. 31 , 32 The advent of immunotherapy has changed the therapeutic strategy of patients with NSCLC. It is used for treating patients with advanced metastatic or locally advanced resectable tumors. A phase Ib clinical trial showed that the MPR of patients with resectable NSCLC receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy was 40.5%. 33 Several clinical trials demonstrated that the MPR of patients with resectable NSCLC receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy + chemotherapy was significantly better compared to conventional chemotherapy. In addition to the improved pathological response rate, CheckMate‐816 showed that patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy had a better survival time compared to chemotherapy alone. 34 , 35 , 36 Currently, static predictive biomarkers based on treated tissues, such as PD‐L1 and TMB, fail to accurately predict the antitumor immune responses in patients treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Therefore, identifying more convenient and dynamic biomarkers for predicting the MPR rates of patients treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy and survival is necessary.

Here, we calculated inflammatory markers based on dynamic changes in eosinophils, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, monocytes, albumin levels, and CRP in the peripheral blood of patients with pre‐ and post‐neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Next, we explored the correlation between these markers and the patient's prognosis and efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy. We included 189 patients with NSCLC who underwent neoadjuvant immunotherapy at a single center. The MPR rates of patients post‐surgery in TC and VCs were 43.6% and 44.2%, respectively. In the TC, no significant difference in the pre‐treatment eosinophil fraction was observed between MPR and non‐MPR groups; however, a significant increase in the post‐treatment eosinophil fraction was observed in patients in the MPR group. Patients with elevated eosinophil fraction post‐treatment had higher MPR rates. However, our analysis revealed no correlation between high eosinophil fraction and the OS and EFS of patients in both cohorts. Few studies on inflammatory markers have analyzed eosinophils. Previous studies have shown that high eosinophil levels in the blood and tumors could predict the patient's response to ICI therapy. 37 Mast cells, natural killer T cells, lymphocytes, and cancer cells can secrete interleukin‐5. It is involved in the growth and differentiation of eosinophils and promotes eosinophil infiltration in tumors. 38 The toxic effects of eosinophils upon IFN‐γ activation can counteract cancer cells via the mechanism of promoting the recruitment of CD8+ T cells through chemokines, thus contributing to tumor regression. 39 , 40 In addition, post‐treatment NLR, PLR, SII, and MLR were significantly lower in the MPR group. The ROC curves showed that the AUC values of post‐treatment eosinophil fraction, NLR, and SII had good efficacy in both cohorts. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses showed that post‐treatment eosinophil fraction, SII, mGPS, and ΔSII could independently predict MPR in patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy. To explore the impact of pre‐ and post‐treatment changes in inflammatory markers on patients, we constructed ROC curves based on the changes in inflammatory markers. The results revealed that the AUC values of Δeosinophil (%) and ΔSII ranked top two in both cohorts, and ΔSII could independently predict MPR. ICI affects the immunotherapy efficacy by blocking T‐cell function and altering the relative ratio of circulating lymphocytes. Neutrophils secrete several chemokines and cytokines that attenuate the immune activity of natural killer cells and lymphocytes. 41 , 42 Together, this impacts the tumor growth and the patient's response to therapy.

Additionally, survival analysis revealed that high post‐treatment NLR, PLR, SII, and mGPS were significantly associated with poorer OS and EFS in both cohorts. PLR and NLR are the most widely studied inflammation biomarkers associated with therapeutic failure and death of patients in various cancers. 43 , 44 , 45 mGPS has shown early success in predicting outcomes in patients with renal cell carcinoma, bladder cancer, and advanced NSCLC. 27 , 46 , 47 Our results showed that SII, a combination of NLR and PLR, had better predictive ability. Moreover, for the first time, we combined the changes in inflammatory biomarkers and patient outcomes. The results showed a significant correlation between high ΔNLR and ΔSII and poor OS and EFS in patients in both cohorts. Neutrophils and lymphocytes could be surrogate markers for the status of inflammation and to monitor immune responses, which are closely associated with the cytotoxic effects of the treatment and tumor progression. 48 Studies have shown that PNI based on albumin levels predicts the survival of patients with solid cancers, such as colorectal, esophageal, and NSCLC. 26 , 49 However, our results showed that PNI had no significant advantage in patients undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Several studies have reported the potential of other inflammatory markers, such as NLR and SII, to predict the prognosis and response of patients with solid tumors to immunotherapy. 50 , 51 However, most patients had advanced unresectable tumors and a single observational index. Unlike our study, several biomarkers were not included in these studies.

However, our study has a few limitations. First, our retrospective single‐center study could be affected by bias and confounding factors. Second, the follow‐up duration of patients in the neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohort was short, and the sample size was small so might not represent the entire population. Third, standardized cut‐off values for inflammation biomarkers in peripheral blood are still unavailable. The cut‐off values we calculated may not be applicable to all patients and should be validated in a large sample size excluding additional confounding factors. Finally, the underlying mechanisms of inflammatory biomarkers in peripheral blood for predicting the efficacy and outcome of neoadjuvant immunotherapy remain unclear. Hence, in vivo and in vitro experiments are required.

In conclusion, this study systematically included NLR, PLR, SII, and MLR, calculated based on peripheral blood inflammatory indexes, and eosinophil fraction, which has received less attention in previous studies, and added PNI and mGPS calculated based on albumin levels, to comprehensively analyze the potential of inflammatory biomarkers in peripheral blood to predict neoadjuvant immune efficacy and prognosis in NSCLC. Our results demonstrated that post‐treatment eosinophil fraction, SII, mGPS, and ΔSII could independently predict MPR in patients treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy in both cohorts. Furthermore, high post‐treatment NLR, PLR, SII, mGPS, ΔNLR, and ΔSII were significantly correlated with poor OS and EFS of patients. We aim to construct a comprehensive predictive model by integrating these non‐invasive and easily accessible peripheral blood biomarkers to design new strategies for treating NSCLC patients undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy.
