# Investigating the biomechanics of falls in older adults in long-term care using a video camera: a scoping review.

## Abstract

Background: Falls are a worrying and growing phenomenon worldwide that especially affects the elderly. With the development of technology, one way of studying the real-life falls that occur in healthcare settings is by using video cameras.

Aims: To (a) map the patterns of the research on real-life falls among older adults in healthcare settings as assessed with digital video camera supports; and (b) highlight the advances, the evidence produced, and the gaps still present regarding the biomechanics of falls as assessed technologically.

Methods: A scoping review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. CINAHL, PubMed and Google Scholar were searched. All studies regarding falls investigated with video cameras among older individuals in healthcare settings published from 1st January 1990 to 1st January 2022 were eligible. Findings were summarised according to the Patterns, Advances, Gaps, Evidence and Recommendations framework for Scoping Reviews.

Results: In total, 1943 studies were retrieved, and 16 met the inclusion criteria. Studies were mostly conducted in Canada. They described the real-life falls that occurred mainly in common and living areas of long-term facilities among older individuals, mainly females. Thirteen studies investigated falls through biomechanics, while three provided advances in the reliability of the measures as collected with video cameras. Studies reported that the biomechanics of a fall, reflecting the direction of the fall and protective responses, increase or decrease the likelihood of serious impact. In addition, the direction of the landing after a fall has been determined as having a significant impact on the severity and outcome of the fall.

Conclusion: The use of video cameras to investigate the biomechanics of falls is a well-established research area that offer interesting insight regarding (a) how to prevent falls and their injuries and (b) the direction of the research in the field of falls.

## Background

Background

Falls may affect the functional independence of older adults [ 1 ] and an increasing number of falls with age are associated with increased morbidity, long-term care and mortality [ 2 , 3 ]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis conducted in 2020 defined the worldwide prevalence of falls in the older adults at 26.5% (95% confidence interval [CI] 23.4–29.8%) [ 4 ], and in the United States of America alone 50 billion dollars in medical costs annually are spent in aspects older-adult falls-related [ 1 ]; moreover, only in 2020, falls were the cause of 58.1% of unintentional injuries, leading to more than 36,000 deaths [ 5 ].

Falls have recently been projected to become an increasing public health burden worldwide [ 6 ]. At the individual level, they may affect quality of life due to the consequences of injuries and the self-imposed restrictions on movement triggered by the fear of falling again [ 7 ]. The first fall doubles the likelihood of falling a second time [ 8 ].

Several multidimensional intrinsic and extrinsic factors increasing the risk of falls have been documented to date, including balance issues, visual impairments and the use of medications (e.g. sedatives, antidepressants), as well as hazards in the environment [ 1 , 6 ]. However, despite the large body of knowledge produced in this field making falls a well-investigated phenomenon [ 9 ], their prevention is still an important public health goal [ 6 ] and a research priority given that the evidence is still insufficient to inform health-planning decision-making [ 10 , 11 ]. Nurses perceive their limited capacity to prevent falls effectively [ 12 ], resulting in increased stress, anxiety, guilt, concern for liability and self-doubt about the quality of care provided [ 13 ].

One strategy for increasing the capacity to prevent falls effectively has been recommended through investigating more data on fall biomechanics [ 14 , 15 ]. Specifically, the priority is to identify fall patterns during the descent and impact phase from the beginning of the fall to its end [ 16 ], the impact velocity [ 17 ], risk of injury, the time duration of the fall, and the ability to initiate a protective response [ 18 ]. In fact, the fall configuration, the response/movements enacted by patients to protect themselves, the falling speed and the time elapsing between landing and touching the ground, may all affect the consequences of falls [ 19 , 20 ].

Several studies performed to date are based on self-reported data [ 21 ], witnessed falls [ 22 ] or post-fall data collection where accuracy may be affected by the emotional implications of falls, by the cognitive issues of the patients and the recall bias [ 23 ]. To overcome these issues, falls have started being investigated in simulated environments with video cameras [ 24 , 25 ], but data on artificial falls have limited meaning regarding unintentional and unexpected falls that may occur in daily life [ 24 , 25 ]. The spread of technology has led to the use of video cameras not only for surveillance but also for clinical purposes, developing an innovative method to investigate the biomechanics of falls as they occur in real life [ 26 ]. However, no summary of studies to date has summarised the main progresses accumulated in the field, the gaps still present, and the preventive measures recommendations for healthcare professionals [ 27 ]. Therefore, this study was intended at mapping the knowledge produced to date in the field.

## Aims

Aims

This study aimed (a) to map the patterns of the research on real-life falls among older adults in healthcare settings as assessed with digital video camera support and (b) to highlight the advances, the evidence and the gaps regarding the biomechanics of falls as evaluated technologically.

## Methods

Methods

Design

A scoping review was performed according to the conceptual framework of Arksey and O’Malley [ 28 ] and further developed by Levac et al. [ 29 ]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR) guidance [ 30 ] was followed to design the scoping review (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the Patterns, Advances, Gaps, Evidence and Recommendations (PAGER) framework [ 27 ] was used to report the results.

Search question

The following research questions were addressed: What are the patterns of the research on real-life falls among older adults in the healthcare settings as assessed with digital video camera supports? and What advances, evidence, gaps and recommendations can be summarised regarding falls as investigated technologically?

Central to our investigation was the concept of the biomechanics of falls, such as all fall configurations, protective responses, velocity, duration and impact [ 16 ].

Search strategy and inclusion/exclusion criteria

Electronic databases including the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), PubMed and Google Scholar, were searched up to 1st January 2022 (Supplementary Table 2). Initially, two researchers (AT and AB) performed an unsystematic pilot literature review. No studies on this topic before 1990 [ 31 ] have been published, whereas some keywords were detected, allowing the establishment and refinement of the search string. Therefore, the following terms were considered keywords: ‘accidental falls’, ‘mechanisms of falls’, ‘dynamics of falls’, ‘biomechanics of falls’, ‘fall characteristics’ and ‘circumstances of falls’. Moreover, four additional terms were combined with the previous words: ‘inpatients’, ‘older adults’, ‘video’ and ‘video capture’. The review involved three researchers (AT, AB and AP), and the search strategy was improved by team discussion. In addition, disagreements among researchers on the study selection process were solved by consensus and discussion. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarised in Table 1 . Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Search outcomes

All studies retrieved were recorded. After excluding duplicates, the remaining studies were screened by title, abstract and keywords against the inclusion criteria. Two researchers (AT and AB) screened independently, and in the case of disagreement, further discussion occurred to reach a consensus by involving a third researcher (AP). The same process was performed regarding all eligible studies identified that were carefully read in their full text. The assessment regarding their final inclusion was performed independently and then agreed upon.

Data extraction

The following variables were identified and extracted as relevant to the review questions: (a) author(s), year of publication, country and affiliation(s); (b) aim(s), study design and ethical issues addressed; (c) the setting and study period; (d) the sample and its main characteristics; (e) the data collection tools (including the main characteristics of the digital video camera support); (f) the data collected at the fall, individual and environment/facility levels; (g) the data on the validity of fall measures investigated, when available; and the findings, which were divided into two categories: (h) key messages and (i) specific findings. First, the data extraction grid was developed and then tested in two studies by two researchers (AT and AP), after which no further changes were required. Then, data extraction was performed independently by two researchers (AT and AB), and a third researcher (AP) was consulted in the case of disagreements.

Data synthesis

During the process of collating, summarising and reporting the study findings [ 28 ], data were categorised according to the PAGER framework [ 27 ] around: (a) the research patterns in this field of investigation, describing their main features (e.g., specific aims, outcomes, data collection tool, settings); (b) the advances and the evidence produced to date in the dynamics of falls in the initiation, descendent and impact phases [ 16 ] as measured with digital video camera support; and (c) the main gaps and recommendations informing the future research and the practice, respectively. All researchers (AT, AB, GR, SC) were involved, along with two researchers (AG, AP) in resolving disagreements.

## Discussion

Discussion

We reviewed studies using video cameras to investigate the biomechanics of falls in real life, map study patterns, and highlight the advances, the evidence, and the gaps, following the PAGER framework [ 27 ].

Study patterns

A total of 16 studies were conducted up to the beginning of 2022, and nearly all were performed by an extraordinarily productive Canadian research team, which has been working in this field since 2010 [ 36 ] in close contact with two facilities with many video cameras positioned in the common areas. This research line has accumulated progressive evidence, working over 11 years on the same data gradually expanded both for the database accessed [ 44 ] and the aims of the work, e.g. from cause [ 36 ] to characterise falls among patients affected by Parkinson’s and non-Parkinson’s [ 40 ], suggesting how a complex research problem can be developed over time under different perspectives with substantial stable methodologies. On the other hand, only two isolated studies in Belgium and the United States have pursued the same attempts to introduce interesting methodological innovations, such as the analysis of videos recorded in private spaces [ 23 ] and video footage analysis from a video camera system that alerts nurses in case of a probable fall [ 33 ] instead of starting from incident reports filled in routinely.

This field involves primarily multidisciplinary basic discipline research teams and, on a few occasions, clinical teams, whose responsibility it was mainly to fill in the incident fall reports, enabling the identification of appropriate video footage [ 20 ].

Overall, the video cameras were installed for safety purposes by ensuring non-intrusive surveillance where video footage was extracted after the fall event and only related to it. The use of video cameras has consistently raised concerns due to their intrusiveness, as a form of restraint for the prevention of security issues and as a potential violation of privacy [ 46 ]. However, all studies were based on a solid ethical foundation in which residents gave informed consent upon admission to the facility to be observed for safety purposes in common and not private spaces, except for one study in which the residents were offered the ability to turn off the video camera when deemed necessary [ 23 ]. However, investigators never detected the cameras switched off except for some accidental episodes [ 23 ]. Images were transformed into silhouettes to protect privacy [ 33 ], a useful strategy to prevent ethical issues.

Beyond the classification reported in the studies, nearly all appear to be population-based studies, followed over time, in which multiple aims are achieved, ranging from (a) epidemiological (dimensioning the falls by reporting incidence and prevalence data) to (b) investigating their biomechanics and (c) the underlying methodology adopted to increase the validity of data collected with video footages.

Studies have been multi-centre, engaging mainly two Canadian long-term facilities for many years, transforming them into places where falls are investigated over time in their natural settings. From a laboratory-type research phase, as represented in the first studies, the two facilities have become natural places to study falls, where recording technologies will likely become ubiquitous and normalised in their presence for both residents and staff [ 47 ].

The eligible population was identified from the total number of residents living in the facilities and the falls that occurred to them, as well as from very select groups (e.g. three residents [ 23 ]). In describing the flow of the participants and/or falls included, some have reported that the flow diagram [ 17 ], which indicated the reason for exclusion (e.g. falls occurred too far from the video camera), has led to a methodological approach that has been encouraged to be reported in future studies to increase transparency. However, except for the O’Connor study [ 33 ], all video footage has been scrutinised according to the incident report filled in by the staff, and therefore studies available have extended the acknowledged falls because of witnesses or reports, leaving those not recorded a still-hidden world.

Some authors collected clinical data at the individual level, such as medications (e.g. Vlaeyen et al. [ 23 ]), while a few also collected data at the facility level, such as the size of the beds (e.g. Yang et al. [ 20 ]). However, nearly all studies used the FVAQ to assess each fall episode in detail and they estimated the velocity and time interval between imbalance and impact through video digitization methods or by counting the number of frames between important events. This homogeneity seems exemplary, considering that different tools are used in other research lines, challenging each attempt to compare and accumulate the evidence produced. Vlaeyen et al. [ 23 ] also used the broader framework of Noury et al. [ 45 ] to evaluate and classify fall detectors, where falls were analysed in the pre-fall, critical, post-fall and recovery phases. Although the FVAQ classification and that of Noury and colleagues [ 45 ] respond to different aims, it might be interesting to consider their elements as the basis of the incident reports being filled in by nurses to increase their homogeneity across settings and countries and their capacity to produce valid information [ 20 ] even in contexts where there are no video cameras available.

Evidence and advancements

Study findings inform how to prevent the initial phase of falls, instructing and exercising patients on how to transfer or shift the body weight correctly [ 36 ], how to adopt safe landing strategies during a fall [ 48 ] and how to secure external support aides during ambulation by providing automatic systems, locking wheelchairs and/or ensuring fixed objects that residents may grasp and then balance their weight [ 49 ]. In fact, falls occurred twice as often in the rising phase as in the stabilisation phase of the transfer, but falls during the stabilisation phase were more likely to cause injury [ 49 ].

Training should also be focused on the descendant fall phase regarding effective reactive responses – for example, optimising neck stiffness to maintain the head upright [ 35 ] or enacting a backward rotation [ 37 ] to prevent head impacts, shaping the educational intervention according to the patient’s risk (Parkinson’s vs non-Parkinson’s disease) [ 40 ]. Older individuals may find turning the body easier than coordinating the other limbs due to low muscle control, weakened motor-response ability and reflex perception [ 37 , 50 ]. The success of protective movements is associated with the energy with which the fall occurred, the environmental constraints, the speed of response and the clinical and physical condition [ 51 ]. Even if it seems difficult, older adults in long-term facilities can execute stepping and grasping reactions and maintain a grip on objects during falls [ 49 ]. Exercises that include balance, strength, dual-task and cognitive-motor training may be needed to promote increased capacity in performing stepping and reaching reactions [ 52 , 53 ].

## Limitations

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. First, although designed according to a systematic approach by including all languages, some studies may have been missed. Moreover, all studies were included without evaluating their methodological quality: although this is consistent with the methodologies available in scoping reviews [ 28 ]. In addition, all the long-term care studies developed in British Columbia (Canada) involved analysing the same data set, albeit with different research questions and larger samples as the years progressed. Therefore, the generalisability of the evidence mapped may have been influenced.

Second, no critical analysis or comparison was conducted across the technologies used by researchers: the rapid developments expected in the digital video camera support will improve the quality of the studies in this field. For example, in some studies, falls were not easily viewable due to shadows [ 33 ] because of the distance between the camera and the resident, or because of the occlusion of the body section [ 36 ], and the number of seconds collected: Robinovitch et al. [ 36 ] suggested 60 s but O’Connor et al. [ 33 ] collected only 10.

Third, the search process and the analysis of the studies required long time to be completed accurately after the data search ended in the beginning of the 2022: future research may cover the time gaps by mapping the studies produced in the last year. However, the methods used in this field and the findings mapped may inform and give insights to other lines of investigation as that exploring the association between the amount of nursing resources and occurrence of falls expanding the research capacity to fully understanding the phenomenon [ 59 ].

## Conclusions

Conclusions

This scoping review provided a map of studies using video cameras to investigate the biomechanics of real-life falls among older adults in long-term care. The intent was to summarise the evidence available in this field but also the gaps and the recommendations to inform which preventive mechanisms can be implemented to reduce the high occurrence of falls among older individuals cared for in health care settings and how to address the future research in the field.

The use of video footage of falls mainly occurring in common areas is recent, involves multidisciplinary research teams, the largest one being based in Canada. The study processes conducted are complex, lasting up to 11 years, triggered by falls recorded in the incident reports and include the analysis of the video footage, using substantially the homogeneous methods.

Studies documented that the biomechanics of a fall, reflecting the direction of the fall and protective responses, increase or decrease the likelihood of serious impact. In addition, the direction of the landing after a fall has been determined as having a significant impact on the severity and outcome of the fall. Therefore, they may inform healthcare professionals and patients regarding the prevention, e.g., how to prevent the initial phase of falls, instructing and exercising patients on how to transfer or shift the body weight correctly; how to adopt safe landing strategies during a fall and how to secure external support aides during ambulation by providing automatic systems, locking wheelchairs and/or ensuring fixed objects that residents may grasp and then balance their weight.

Video cameras are important for deepening our knowledge of real-life falls and moving forward in research in this area by assuming a complementary role to that conducted by other traditional research methodologies due to their capacity to provide useful information. Future studies should consider the several challenges faced by this research field as the time required to analyse video; moreover, the ethical implications and the poor adherence of patients to research projects, as well as the continuous developments in the technological field may improve the accuracy of the finding but, on the other hand, may increase the complexity of the observational studies.
