# Increased Risk of Cardiovascular Events After Coronary Interventions in Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A Nationwide Matched Cohort Study.

## Abstract

Background: Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) have been associated with an increased long-term risk of coronary artery disease due to chronic systemic inflammation.

Aim: To evaluate the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after coronary interventions.

Methods: In this nationwide cohort study of adults undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) (2012-2022), patients with IBD were propensity score-matched 1:10 to comparators without IBD. The primary outcome was MACE, a composite of acute myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalisation for heart failure, or mortality. Secondary outcomes included each MACE component, 30-day all-cause readmission, revascularisation and in-hospital outcomes including intensive care unit admission and length of hospital stay. We calculated hazard ratios (HRs) and incidence rates (IRs) using Cox proportional hazards modelling.

Results: We included 987 patients with IBD and 9571 matched comparators. After a median follow-up of 3.5 years, MACE occurred in 488 patients with IBD (49.4%, IR: 96.5/10,000 person-years [PY]) and in 3857 matched comparators (40.3%, IR: 68.9/10,000 PY); HR 1.37 (95% CI, 1.24-1.52). This equates to one additional MACE for every 36 patients with IBD over 10 years. The risk of each MACE component was increased, except for stroke. There were no differences between IBD subtypes or coronary intervention (PCI vs. CABG). Risks were highest in older individuals and elective interventions.

Conclusions: Patients with IBD were at 37% higher risk of MACE after coronary intervention, indicating a need for intensified cardiovascular risk reduction in these high-risk individuals.

## Introduction

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), including Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), are chronic immune‐mediated inflammatory conditions that affect the gastrointestinal tract [ 1 , 2 ]. A relapsing–remitting course, variable disease severity and differing responses to treatment characterise them. The disease significantly impacts patients' health‐related quality of life and work productivity [ 3 ].

Chronic inflammation, a hallmark of IBD, is a significant risk factor for the pathogenesis and progression of atherosclerosis [ 4 ]. Several studies have reported an increased long‐term risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in patients with IBD [ 5 , 6 , 7 ]. In light of the increasing evidence linking inflammation and atherosclerosis, several anti‐inflammatory treatments have recently been investigated for the primary and secondary prevention of CVD with positive results [ 8 , 9 , 10 ].

While patients with IBD are at an increased risk of developing CVD due to persistent chronic inflammation, it is unknown whether these individuals are also at an increased risk of recurrent events after coronary interventions. Indeed, chronic low‐grade inflammation during this vulnerable period may be a clinically significant risk factor for recurrent events and may be relevant for optimising post‐interventional care.

Therefore, this nationwide matched cohort study aimed to assess whether IBD is associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and other adverse clinical outcomes after coronary interventions, including both percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).

## Methods

Methods

This nationwide cohort study leveraged fully pseudo‐anonymised population‐based administrative claims data from the Swiss Federal Statistical Office (Bundesamt für Statistik, Neuchâtel, Switzerland). The database encompasses all Swiss inpatient discharge records from acute care and specialty hospitals, excluding post‐acute care units, regardless of payer, thereby creating a complete sample of inpatient discharges in Switzerland. Patient‐level data on demographics, healthcare utilisation, hospital type, discharge diagnoses, clinical procedures and in‐hospital outcomes including lengthofhospital stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) admission, intubation and in‐hospital mortality were available for all hospitalised patients in Switzerland. Each individual was uniquely identified using an anonymous identifier, allowing for follow‐up and tracking of rehospitalisations. Medical diagnoses were coded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, German Modification (ICD‐10 GM) and procedures were coded using Swiss classification of operations codes [ 11 ]. This study was conducted according to principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki [ 12 ] and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Ethics

The institutional review board of Northwestern Switzerland approved this study and waived informed patient consent due to the use of deidentified data.

Study Population and Exposure Definition

All adults who underwent either PCI or CABG in Switzerland between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2022, were eligible for inclusion. Individuals undergoing PCI were identified by the discharge procedure code 00.66. X (X denotes wildcard characters) and individuals undergoing CABG were identified using the procedure codes 36.1X or 36.2 (Table S1 ). Both acute and elective indications of coronary interventions were included in the study. Individuals with IBD were identified using ICD‐10‐GM codes K50 and K51, as detailed in Table S2 . Patients younger than 20 years and cases from non‐somatic hospitals were excluded from the study. To ensure adequate at‐risk exposure time of at least 6 months for each individual, we excluded patients undergoing a coronary intervention within the first 6 months of the study period. Patients with IBD were then propensity score‐matched 1:10 with individuals from the general population without IBD (see flowchart Figure S1 ).

Follow‐up began on the day after hospital discharge as the exact date of intervention was not available. Follow‐up continued until the occurrence of any study outcome, death, or the end of the study period on 31 December 2022.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was 4‐point MACE, a composite of acute myocardial infarction, stroke (ischemic and haemorrhagic), hospitalisation for heart failure and all‐cause death. Secondary outcomes included each individual component of MACE, all‐cause revascularisation rate (defined as any recurrent PCI or CABG after the index coronary intervention), 30‐day all‐cause readmission rate, and in‐hospital outcomes including ICU admission rate, length of ICU stay, intubation rate, duration of intubation and LOS. For patients transferred between acute care hospitals, hospital stays were combined into a single episode of care, with outcomes attributed to the initial hospitalisation. Any rehospitalisation within 30 days of discharge was classified as a readmission for the all‐cause readmission rate endpoint. The analysis of readmissions included both emergency and planned hospital admissions.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarised baseline characteristics. Means and standard deviations (SD) were reported for continuous variables, while numbers, frequencies and percentages were given for categorical variables. Standardised mean differences (SMD) assessed group differences between the two groups; meaningful imbalances were defined as an SMD equal to or greater than 0.1 [ 13 ]. There was no missing data for patient characteristics and study outcomes.

To address the heterogeneity of the patient cohort, a propensity score‐matched analysis was performed, derived from a logistic regression model including all relevant and documented comorbidities up to and including the index date to achieve balanced patient characteristics between individuals with IBD and matched comparators from the general population.

This method provides a robust analysis of observational data and minimises measurable confounding. Based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, eligible IBD patients were 1:10 propensity score–matched with general population comparators using the nearest neighbour method with replacement. The distribution of propensity scores before and after matching was examined graphically (Figure S2 ).

To assess the robustness of our results, we conducted stratified and sensitivity analyses to investigate clinical outcomes of PCI or CABG based on the indication for the index coronary intervention (acute vs. elective), the subtype of IBD (CD vs. UC), sex (male vs. female), age groups (20–49, 50–69 and ≥ 70 years) and pre‐existing heart failure, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia or hypertension.

Effect sizes and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using linear, logistic, or Cox proportional hazards regressions as appropriate. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed and confirmed by examining Schoenfeld residuals related to time. Two‐sided p ‐values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA software (version 18.0).

## Results

Results

After propensity score‐matching, 987 adults with IBD and 9571 matched general population comparators were included (flowchart in Figure S1 ). Among patients with IBD, 399 (40%) had CD and 588 (60%) had UC (Figure S1 ). Patients with IBD were predominantly male (73%) and the majority were over 50 years of age (6% were aged 20–49 years, 45% 50–69 years and 49% ≥ 70 years). The majority of patients underwent PCI (82%) at the index hospitalisation, and the main indication was acute (55%). The number of diseased vessels included 1‐vessel disease (70%), 2‐vessel disease (12%) and 3‐vessel disease (18%), and left main stem disease was seen in 3.5% of patients with IBD. Cardiovascular Outcomes After PCI or CABG

During a median follow‐up of 3.5 years (range 0–10.5 years) we recorded 488 MACE in patients with IBD (49.4%, IR: 96.5/10,000 PY) and 3857 in matched comparators (40.3%, IR: 68.9/10,000PY) (Figure 1 , Table 2 ). Compared to propensity score‐matched comparators, patients with IBD had a 37% higher relative risk of any of the 4‐point MACE events (HR: 1.37 [95% CI 1.24–1.52]) (Table 2 ), corresponding to an excess absolute risk of 0.8% at 1 year, 1.9% at 2 years and 2.6% at 4 years. This translates to one additional MACE outcome for every 36 patients with IBD followed over 10 years. The risk was uniformly increased for all individual MACE components, except for stroke (Table 2 ), but the highest absolute risk was observed for hospitalisation for heart failure (IR: 50.6 vs. 33.3 per 10,000 PY; HR: 1.49 [95% CI 1.31–1.70]) and all‐cause mortality (IR: 44.8 vs. 32.7 per 10,000 PY; HR: 1.40 [95% CI 1.22–1.59]). In addition, individuals with IBD had higher hazards of coronary revascularisation (HR: 1.16 [95% CI 1.01–1.34]) (Table 2 ).

In stratified analyses by subtype of IBD, the relative risk estimates of MACE were similar for both subtypes (CD: HR: 1.43 [95% CI 1.22–1.66] vs. UC: HR: 1.34 [95% CI 1.18–1.52]). No differences were seen between sex or the two types of coronary interventions (PCI vs. CABG, p‐

in teraction > 0.05). However, the hazards of MACE were higher in individuals with elective (HR 1.54 [95% CI 1.33–1.78]) vs. acute coronary intervention (HR 1.24 [95% CI 1.08–1.43], p‐

interaction = 0.04). The hazards of MACE were also increased in patients aged 50 years and older, but not in those under 50 years ( p‐

interaction = 0.03) (Figure 2 ). Additionally, the risk of MACE was higher in patients with IBD without a prior diagnosis of heart failure ( p‐

interaction = 0.001) before PCI or CABG compared to their respective comparators. There were no differences across hospital categories, start of follow‐up, or comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension and dyslipidaemia (Figure 2 ).

## Discussion

Discussion

In this nationwide Swiss propensity score‐matched cohort study of approximately 1000 adults with IBD who underwent PCI or CABG, we found that patients with IBD had a 37% higher relative risk of MACE compared to matched population comparators. This equates to 1 additional MACE for every 36 patients with IBD followed over 10 years. While the risk was found to be highest in those of a higher age, who had undergone elective intervention and who did not have a previous diagnosis of heart failure, no major differences were found with respect to sex and prevalent metabolic syndrome.

While several studies have reported a long‐term increased risk of arrhythmia [ 6 ], heart failure [ 7 ], stroke [ 14 ] and myocardial infarction (MI) [ 5 ] in patients with IBD, surprisingly few studies have comprehensively investigated outcomes after interventions prompted by coronary artery disease [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ]. Previous studies have reported contradictory findings on the outcomes after coronary interventions [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ]. To our knowledge, this is the first population‐based propensity score‐matched study to investigate short‐ and long‐term risks of MACE and in‐hospital outcomes after both PCI and CABG in patients with IBD.

We found a 1.37‐fold increased relative risk of MACE after PCI or CABG in patients with IBD compared to matched comparators without IBD. No difference between the risk of MACE after PCI and CABG was observed. Among individual MACE components, the risks of death, hospitalisation for heart failure and MI were elevated, but not the risk of stroke. Our findings align with those of an Israeli retrospective single‐centre study, investigating a composite endpoint of mortality, acute coronary syndrome, or admission due to acute heart failure in 30 days and 12 months after PCI in a cohort including patients with IBD. After adjusting for several covariates, the authors found a 40% increased relative risk (HR 1.4 [1.06–1.85]) of the composite endpoint during follow‐up in patients with IBD. The risks of acute heart failure (HR 1.37) and acute coronary syndrome (HR 2.22) were significantly increased after PCI, but mortality was not.

In a register‐based matched cohort study, the rate of MACE within 1 year after MI was reported to be higher in patients with IBD compared to the matched comparators without IBD; however, likely due to the limited sample size ( n = 245), the result did not reach statistical significance [ 19 ]. Similar to our MACE outcomes, these were investigated in three US health care database cohort studies [ 15 , 16 , 17 ]. One study [ 15 ] reported a decreased risk of in‐hospital post‐PCI complications, while two showed lower [ 16 ] or similar [ 17 ] in‐hospital mortality rates in patients with IBD compared to individuals without IBD. Notably, these three studies only explored short‐term outcomes after PCI and did not, as in our research, include long‐term follow‐up data. Furthermore, while we performed a sophisticated propensity score‐matched analysis, these studies did not match patients with IBD to non‐IBD comparators or comprehensively adjust for essential covariates linked to adverse CVD outcomes such as metabolic comorbidities. Furthermore, no previous study analysed MACE and in‐hospital outcomes after PCI and CABG.

We did not observe an increased risk of MACE in the age group 20–49 years, whereas the risk was significantly increased in those aged ≥ 50 years. The risk of CVD is generally higher in men than in women; however, we observed no difference in the primary outcome between the sexes. We found a significant difference in risk according to indication (acute vs. elective) of the coronary intervention, with higher risks among individuals undergoing elective coronary intervention. This might suggest that the post‐interventional care following elective interventions may not be sufficient to prevent recurrent events and revascularisations. Interestingly, the increased risk of MACE in patients with IBD was not seen in individuals with heart failure, as this condition is already associated with chronic low‐grade systemic inflammation [ 20 ] and the presence of an additional chronic inflammatory bowel disease did not significantly alter their risk of recurrent major adverse cardiovascular events.

We posit that the explanation for the association observed in our study is multi‐factorial, including factors related to inflammation in IBD, IBD patient characteristics and possibly also to the IBD treatment. Systemic inflammation has long been linked to coronary artery disease as a driver of the atherosclerotic process [ 4 , 21 ]. This association has also been suggested for several gastrointestinal diseases, including IBD [ 22 , 23 , 24 ]. In IBD, hypercoagulability and increased plaque vulnerability caused by inflammation and disease flares may potentially also contribute to the risk of adverse CVD events. Interestingly, a nationwide register‐based Danish study reported an association between IBD flares or disease activity and an increased 30‐day all‐cause mortality after first‐time MI, as well as higher rates of recurrent MI [ 25 ].

Several important considerations arise from our findings. First, it is crucial to contextualise the elevated risks shown in our study. As evidenced in several studies, IBD itself and IBD therapy carry a risk of adverse CVD outcomes [ 24 ]. Therefore, coronary intervention treatment decisions should consider the comprehensive risk–benefit profiles, including comorbidities, treatment, available coronary intervention techniques and stent options. Second, understanding the difference between absolute and relative risk is essential. We report relative risks that increased by between 16% and 49% for MACE and in‐hospital hard endpoints. This suggests a considerable risk attributed to coronary interventions. However, the absolute risk difference compared to the general population comparators was 27.6 events per 10,000 PY, translating to 1 extra MACE for every 36 patients with IBD followed over 10 years. Third, the highest risk was seen in the older age groups with IBD. In addition to the underlying inflammatory burden of IBD, we speculate that frailty could be an important contributor to the risk increase in the older age group. Frailty is more prevalent among patients with IBD than in the general population [ 26 ] and may indeed influence the risk of MACE outcomes after coronary interventions. Moreover, frailty has been shown to increase the risk of postoperative mortality and morbidity following surgery [ 27 ]. Consequently, before coronary interventions, assessment beyond traditional risk factors for adverse outcomes after PCI and CABG is warranted in patients with IBD, particularly in the older population. Finally, our results indicate a need for risk stratification of patients with IBD before PCI and CABG and intensified monitoring and treatment after the intervention. More ambitious low‐density lipoprotein levels than in current guidelines [ 28 ] and prolonged tailored antiplatelet therapy could be considered after PCI and CABG to prevent recurrent atherosclerotic events, especially after elective intervention. In addition, physicians caring for patients with IBD should be cautious when considering shorter duration of dual antiplatelet therapy, as well as in those with higher bleeding risk. Recent guidelines for the management of chronic coronary syndromes [ 28 ] advocate the use of cardiac anti‐inflammatory treatment, such as colchicine, which would be a possible adjunct treatment for patients with IBD and chronic low‐grade systemic inflammation.
