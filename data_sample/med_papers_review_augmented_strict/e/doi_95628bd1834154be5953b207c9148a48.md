# Improving asthma control and quality of life via a smartphone self-management app: A randomized controlled trial.

## Abstract

Background: Mobile phone applications (apps) show promise for enhancing asthma self-management, but their effectiveness varies. This study examined the effect of a smartphone asthma app on asthma control and quality of life.

Methods: Using block randomization, 60 patients with asthma were allocated to an intervention group (n = 30) or control group (n = 30) for this single-blind randomized controlled trial. At baseline, both groups completed the Asthma Control Test (ACT) and Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire-Marks (AQLQ-M). The intervention group used a smartphone-based asthma self-management app plus their regular treatment, while the control group received only usual care. Follow-up ACT and AQLQ-M assessments occurred at 3 and 6 months. SPSS version 26 was used for analysis, including descriptive statistics, non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney U), and analysis of variance with repeated measurements.

Results: Both groups showed improved asthma control and quality of life at 3 and 6 months compared to baseline. However, after 6 months the intervention group had significantly greater improvement than controls (p < 0.05). Repeated measures ANOVA revealed divergent changes in ACT and AQLQ-M scores over time, with the intervention group demonstrating greater enhancement of asthma control and quality of life (p < 0.001).

Conclusion: This study demonstrated that use of a smartphone-based asthma self-management app improved asthma control and quality of life after 6 months compared to usual care alone. These findings indicate that guideline-based asthma apps can positively impact outcomes.

Abstract Background Mobile phone applications (apps) show promise for enhancing asthma self-management, but their effectiveness varies. This study examined the effect of a smartphone asthma app on asthma control and quality of life. Methods Using block randomization, 60 patients with asthma were allocated to an intervention group (n = 30) or control group (n = 30) for this single-blind randomized controlled trial. At baseline, both groups completed the Asthma Control Test (ACT) and Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire-Marks (AQLQ-M). The intervention group used a smartphone-based asthma self-management app plus their regular treatment, while the control group received only usual care. Follow-up ACT and AQLQ-M assessments occurred at 3 and 6 months. SPSS version 26 was used for analysis, including descriptive statistics, non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney U), and analysis of variance with repeated measurements. Results Both groups showed improved asthma control and quality of life at 3 and 6 months compared to baseline. However, after 6 months the intervention group had significantly greater improvement than controls (p 2 times/week but not daily), moderate persistent asthma (daily symptoms), and severe persistent asthma (continual symptoms, limited physical activity) based on Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines [1], age ≥18 years, at least a primary education, ownership of an Android smartphone. Exclusion criteria were patients with other chronic respiratory diseases (except asthma), inability to adequately utilize a smartphone, and previous use of asthma mobile apps. Patient recruitment and enrollment for this trial began on August 1, 2021 and concluded on September 15, 2021. The final study visit with 6-month follow-up assessments for the last enrolled participants took place on March 23, 2022. The required sample size was calculated based on mean and standard deviation changes in ACT scores reported in a similar study [20]: 47.1 ± 20 for controls and 72.5 ± 22 for intervention. Assuming 95 % confidence and 95 % power, the minimum per group was 9. However, this study utilized the central limit theorem (n > 30) and examined two groups of 30 asthma patients each to strengthen statistical analysis. 2.2 Randomization and recruitment Using block randomization, patients were allocated to an intervention group (n = 30) or control group (n = 30). The control group received usual care alone, while the intervention group also used a smartphone-based app along with regular treatment. Randomization involved first generating four blocks with codes A and B (6 blocks total). Then 15 blocks were randomly selected with replacement using a random number table for group assignment. To reduce the risk of observer bias, the researcher who conducted the data analysis was blinded. The subjects could not be made unaware of the intervention due to its nature. 2.3 Intervention description The intervention group utilized a smartphone app, RespRight (version 2.2), previously developed for and validated on the Android platform for usability by patients, IT experts, and asthma and allergy specialists [21]. RespRight was designed as a self-management app for patients with asthma based on nationalized localized guidelines. The app aimed to improve treatment adherence and self-management through features including recording peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) values and asthma symptoms (optional daily entry), educational content about asthma management, self-assessment tools for symptom control, reminder alerts for taking medications, and ability to communicate with the physician. In both the intervention and control groups, patients were instructed to record daily PEFR values. The smartphone app enabled the intervention group to log PEFR and provided feedback messages on changes in entered values, while the control group recorded measurements manually without app support. However, the app itself did not provide graphs for tracking peak flow data over time. There was no automated integration between the peak flow device and app. 2.4 Data collection and patient follow-up At enrollment, the clinic secretary loaded the app onto intervention patients' smartphones when they visited the Asthma and Allergy Clinic. The physician provided in-person instruction on using the app, which supported therapy at each visit. At baseline (BL), all patients received face-to-face assessment and education on proper inhaler technique and individual action plans. Follow-up visits occurred at 3 and 6 months (M3 and M6) for examination and treatment. At each visit, patients completed the Asthma Control Test (ACT) and Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire-Marks (AQLQ-M) to assess outcomes. 2.5 Outcome measurement tools The primary outcome of asthma control was assessed using the 5-item ACT questionnaire. Each item measures respiratory status over the past 4 weeks on a 5-point scale, with 1 indicating poor control and 5 indicating well control. Total scores range from 5 (uncontrolled) to 25 (controlled). The ACT demonstrated good validity (r = 0.86) and internal consistency (α = 0.89) in an Iranian study [22]. The secondary outcome of quality of life was assessed using the 20-item AQLQ-M. This questionnaire evaluates multiple domains related to quality of life, including physical, emotional, social, and functional health aspects [23]. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert-type scale, with response options ranging from 1 (“not at all impaired”) to 5 (“very severely impaired”). Thus, lower scores represent better quality of life. The minimum score is 20 while the maximum is 100. An overall quality of life score can be obtained by summing the scores across the four measured domains. The AQLQ-M was validated for content and internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.86) in an Iranian asthma population based on expert review and prior research [24]. To identify clinically meaningful changes in asthma control and quality of life, minimum clinically important difference (MCID) values were calculated for the ACT and AQLQ-M scores. The MCID represents the smallest change in an outcome measure that is perceived as beneficial and can lead to adjustments in medical treatment [25]. Based on Norman et al.'s method [26], the MCID was defined as half a standard deviation (SD) from baseline values. 2.6 Statistical analysis SPSS version 26 was used for analysis. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic, clinical, and study variable data. Within-group changes from baseline to 3 and 6 months were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Data normality was assessed with Kolmogorov-Smirnov testing. Between-group differences at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. Repeated measures ANOVA determined the effects of time and time-group interaction. All tests used a significance level of 0.05. 3 Results Fig. 1 show the participant selection process. Both exceeded the MCID value, representing clinically meaningful quality of life improvements for the app users over the study duration. 4 Discussion This study demonstrated that use of a smartphone-based asthma self-management app led to clininically meaningful improvements in asthma control and quality of life after 6 months. Specifically, the intervention group changes for both ACT and AQLQ-M scores exceeded the MCID thresholds, while the control group changes failed to achieve clinical significance by not reaching MCIDs. Surpassing these values signifies the mobile app yielded tangible patient benefits in areas like breathing, functioning, and quality of life impairment beyond standard treatment alone. In this study, use of the app resulted in a meaningful enhancement exceeding the 2.71 point MCID threshold for ACT scores. This degree of score increase translates to real-life improvements in symptom control that patients can feel in their daily lives [12,14,15,20,27–29]. However, a study using an older app found no differences in asthma control or self-efficacy between groups, suggesting app design and quality factors like usability and engagement may influence these outcomes [16]. Indeed, many asthma apps lack performance, appeal, and overall quality which could compromise usage and outcomes [30]. Additionally, a systematic review indicates multifunctional apps are more likely to enhance asthma control [31]. While adding to evidence for asthma apps' benefits, this study highlights the need to optimize app design to maximize effectiveness. The guideline-based asthma self-management app in this study incorporated multiple features that likely contributed to enhanced asthma control and quality of life in the intervention group. Key features enabling improved self-management include daily symptom and peak flow rate tracking with feedback messages, educational content about proper inhaler technique and controlling environmental asthma triggers, medication reminders and alerts for adherence, and ability to directly communicate with healthcare providers about changes in condition. This multifaceted approach addressed knowledge gaps, prompted adherence through reminders, and increased self-awareness of symptom changes - all valuable for daily monitoring and management. The app's capacity to augment traditional office-based asthma care suggests comprehensive telehealth apps providing education, monitoring tools, reminders, and communication access could meaningfully impact outcomes [31]. Thus, asthma apps hold promise not as substitutes for office-based treatment, but rather as adjunct tools bridging gaps through continuous personalized support. According to the findings, patients receiving the intervention exhibited improved quality of life at 6 months compared to controls and baseline, aligning with other research [14,15,17,32]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses [31,33–36] also demonstrate telehealth interventions significantly enhance asthma-related quality of life in children and adults. Asthma quality of life strongly associates with objective indicators like lung function and healthcare utilization, suggesting self-management gains translate into reduced exacerbations and urgent care needs [37,38]. The intervention's tracking features enabling daily peak flow and symptom monitoring likely contributed to such improvements. The 6-month study duration proved sufficient to yield positive effects, aligning with systematic review data showing ≥6 month telehealth interventions maximize asthma control and quality of life improvements versus shorter interventions [33]. Patient-centered asthma management programs can enhance care processes and outcomes over a 2-year period, according to National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) guidelines [39]. Considerable evidence shows long-term monitoring of asthma severity paired with self-management plans helps reduce exacerbations, improve medication adherence, and lower costs [18,40]. Since asthma is a chronic disease, these findings may reflect the time needed to observe health effects in these patients. Thus, further research building on current knowledge about optimal duration of smartphone-based interventions targeting asthma control and quality of life could strengthen this study's findings. Limitations of this study include the small sample size which may have limited ability to detect some between-group differences. The average patient age was 39.7 (±15.5) years, so findings may not fully generalize to other age groups. As clarified previously, all patients had existing asthma action plans and were on controller medications at baseline before app randomization. However, a limitation was the lack of data captured on medication adherence or environmental control changes during the 6-month follow-up that may have impacted outcomes. Additionally, the study did not collect objective lung function data like FEV1 or exacerbation rates, relying solely on subjective outcomes. Inclusion of these data may have further validated the patients' subjective symptom assessments on the ACT and AQLQ-M. Peak flow values were self-reported, though objective measures would have strengthened reliability. Finally, the unblinded nature of the intervention further poses the risk of psychological effects and reporting bias. App users may have consciously or unconsciously exaggerated responses, while absence of an app could have negatively impacted control patients' perceptions. Therefore, future efforts should incorporate objective outcomes to confirm clinical impacts beyond possible psychological effects or biased reporting. Overall, these limitations around sample size and age, treatment details, objective measures, exacerbation data, blinded protocols, and self-reported data restrict conclusions and indicate important avenues for methodology improvements in future mHealth app studies targeting asthma control. Still, demonstrating clinically important improvements on validated instruments supports mhealth apps as valuable supplementary interventions when thoughtfully designed, even if further objective impact validation remains needed. 5 Conclusion This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that use of a smartphone-based asthma self-management app improved asthma control and quality of life after 6 months compared to usual care alone. These findings provide evidence that comprehensive asthma apps addressing multiple self-management components like symptom tracking, medication reminders, and education can positively impact outcomes. Further research in larger, more diverse asthma populations, as well as other respiratory diseases like COPD, will help elucidate the full potential of these interventions. Overall, this adds to growing data indicating smartphone-based solutions can effectively supplement traditional asthma management when thoughtfully designed. These results can inform future efforts to employ mHealth technology to improve patient self-efficacy and burden for this prevalent respiratory disease.
