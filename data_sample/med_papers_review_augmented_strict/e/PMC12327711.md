# Nurse-led social media-based education in diabetic patients: Effects on COVID-19 fear and self-care - A 6-month follow-up trial.

## Abstract

Background: To evaluate the effect of nurse-led, social media-based patient education on COVID-19-associated fear and self-care in diabetic patients throughout a six-month follow-up period.

Materials and methods: A single-blind randomized controlled trial. A total of 113 diabetic patients were recruited using convenience sampling and randomly assigned to the intervention group ( n = 58) or control group ( n = 55). Data were collected using the Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCV-19S) and the Self-Care in COVID-19 Scale (SCOVID) at baseline, immediately after the intervention, and at two- and six-month follow-ups. The intervention group received a four-week COVID-19-related patient education program via social media, while the control group only received routine care. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).

Results: No significant differences in fear or self-care scores were observed between the groups at baseline ( P > 0.05). However, the intervention group exhibited significant improvements in both fear and self-care scores at all post-intervention time points compared to the control group ( P < 0.05). The reduction in fear scores was more pronounced between T1 (immediately after the intervention) and T2 (two months after the intervention), whereas it remained relatively stable between T2 and T3 (six months after the intervention). This indicated a sustained intervention effect over time.

Conclusion: Social media-based nurse-led education effectively reduces COVID-19-associated fear and improves self-care in diabetic patients, with benefits sustained for up to six months. This approach is recommended for accessible, scalable patient education during pandemics.

## Introduction

Introduction

In late December 2019, a novel disease with symptoms resembling those caused by coronaviruses emerged in Wuhan, China.[ 1 ] In February 2020, the causative agent of this disease was identified as a new coronavirus named SARS-CoV-2, and the World Health Organization (WHO) designated the disease as Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19). As COVID-19 rapidly spread across China and subsequently to other countries, WHO declared it a pandemic in March 2020.[ 2 ] By April 2024, over 775 million people worldwide have contracted this disease, with more than 7 million fatalities reported. Iran ranks as the 18 th most affected country, with over 7.5 million cases and 146,000 deaths.[ 3 ] The rapid spread and high transmissibility of COVID-19 have posed significant challenges, threatening both human societies and healthcare systems.[ 4 ] Among various population groups, individuals with underlying health conditions have faced a heightened risk of contracting COVID-19 and are more prone to developing psychological disorders as a result.[ 5 ]

Among individuals with underlying conditions, those with diabetes are particularly susceptible to COVID-19-related infections due to several pathological mechanisms. Coronaviruses bind to target cells via Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE), whose expression is elevated in diabetic patients due to certain medications. Consequently, individuals with diabetes are at an increased risk of severe COVID-19-related infections.[ 6 ] Given the high global prevalence of diabetes, understanding various aspects of COVID-19, particularly its psychological dimensions, is critical.[ 7 ] Cohort studies and systematic reviews have revealed that diabetic individuals constitute approximately 10% of COVID-19 patients hospitalized in general wards and 32% of those in Intensive Care Units (ICUs).[ 8 ] With their elevated vulnerability to severe COVID-19, diabetic patients are more likely to experience psychological challenges, including fear and distress.[ 9 ] Diabetic patients during the pandemic experienced anxiety, fear of infection, social isolation, and stress from disrupted medical care and uncertainty.[ 10 ] Moreover, this group of patients usually encounter significant financial and physical burdens, which in turn lead them to depression.[ 11 ] These psychological challenges compounded by the dual burden of managing a chronic illness and adhering to preventive measures against COVID-19 often required diabetic patients to make significant lifestyle adjustments.[ 12 ]

In addition to fear, the ability to engage in self-care may also be compromised during pandemics.[ 11 ] Self-care encompasses activities individuals undertake to maintain health, address emotional, psychological, and social needs, and prevent illness and injuries.[ 13 ] Effective pandemic control requires individuals to adopt preventive and self-care measures until the risk of disease transmission subsides.[ 14 ] Self-care is a vital component in preventing the spread of infectious diseases and maintaining public health under such conditions.[ 15 ] A high level of self-care is associated with improved quality of life and better treatment outcomes.[ 16 ] Diabetes-associated self-care involves a combination of behaviors, including regular blood glucose monitoring, medication adherence, maintaining a balanced diet, engaging in physical activity, and managing stress.[ 17 ] These practices not only help in achieving glycemic control and preventing complications but also improve mental health by reducing stress and anxiety, which are common among diabetic individuals.[ 18 ] Engaging in physical activity contributes to better mood regulation and enhances overall emotional well-being.[ 19 ] Furthermore, maintaining effective self-care can build psychological resilience, especially during periods of heightened stress, such as pandemics.[ 20 ] These activities are crucial to preventing acute complications like hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia and reducing the risk of severe outcomes from infections, such as COVID-19.[ 15 ] Enhancing self-care capabilities during pandemics is essential for diabetic patients to mitigate health risks and maintain their well-being.[ 21 ] By improving their self-care practices, diabetic patients can not only enhance their health status but also reduce the burden on healthcare systems.[ 22 ]

Nurses play a critical role in diabetes management by educating patients on self-care practices such as blood glucose monitoring, medication adherence, and lifestyle modifications, which are essential for glycemic control. Additionally, they provide ongoing support, monitor for complications, and empower patients to take an active role in managing their condition.[ 23 ] Nurse-led patient education and the provision of accurate and reliable information about COVID-19 prevention and management play a pivotal role in reducing stress among affected individuals and ultimately improving their quality of life. Therefore, providing accurate and accessible information for patients with underlying conditions and their families is both clear and necessary.[ 24 25 ] Nurse-led education via social media platforms, such as messaging applications, has proven to be an effective means of disseminating information, offering psychological support, and facilitating patient understanding.[ 26 ] Many patients currently rely on social media platforms to access information, education, and medical support. Online nurse-led education is an interactive, patient-centered approach that has shown advantages over conventional methods in providing valuable medical information.[ 27 ] Social media, defined as a set of internet-based software applications, enables users to create and share content. It also allows healthcare providers, particularly nurses, to enhance patient education, deliver timely information, and establish effective communication with patients.[ 28 ] This approach facilitates patient education and services regardless of time and location constraints.[ 29 ]

Given the importance of addressing fear and promoting self-care during pandemics in individuals with underlying conditions, existing studies have primarily explored general stress and anxiety management in diabetic patients or the role of healthcare providers in promoting adherence to treatment. However, there is a lack of detailed investigation into how targeted interventions, particularly nurse-led and technology-driven approaches, influence psychological outcomes and self-care behaviors in this population. To address this gap, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of nurse-led, social media-based patient education and follow-up on COVID-19-associated fear and self-care among diabetic patients. We hypothesized that this intervention would significantly reduce COVID-19-associated fear and improve self-care practices, with these effects persisting over a six-month follow-up period.

## Materials and Methods

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study aimed to evaluate the effect of nurse-led, social media-based patient education on COVID-19-associated fear and self-care over a 6-month follow-up period among diabetic patients. This single-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted from July 2021 to March 2022 using a pretest-posttest design.

Study participants and sampling

A total of 113 diabetic patients were enrolled using convenience sampling and randomly assigned to two groups: control ( n = 55) and intervention ( n = 58) [ Table 1 ]. To ensure balanced group sizes, block randomization was implemented utilizing six distinct blocks with sequences AABB, BBAA, ABBA, BAAB, ABAB, and BABA. In these sequences, 'A' denoted the intervention group, while 'B' represented the control group. The order of blocks was determined by rolling a dice, and participants were assigned based on the sequence of letters in the selected blocks. This method ensured an even allocation of participants while minimizing selection bias. The study and sampling were conducted at the Diabetes Clinics of Vali-e-Asr and Mousavi Hospitals, both affiliated with Zanjan University of Medical Sciences, Zanjan, Iran.

To ensure accurate results and anticipate potential obstacles, a pilot study was conducted with 20 diabetes patients. After evaluating the outcomes of various questionnaires, the Fear of COVID-19 Scale was determined to be the most suitable tool for calculating the sample size. Considering the “mean ± standard deviation” of fear, a confidence interval of 95%, and a test power of 80%, the minimum sample size was estimated to be 114 ( n = 57 for each group). To account for a potential 10% attrition rate, the final sample size was increased to 120 ( n = 60 per group).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria required participants to be aged 18–60 years, have a confirmed diagnosis of diabetes, possess literacy skills, have no diagnosed mental illness, and demonstrate the ability to operate a smartphone, tablet, or laptop. Exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of COVID-19 during the study period or voluntary withdrawal from the study. These criteria were designed to ensure the selection of participants who could actively engage with the intervention and complete all follow-up assessments, thereby maintaining the study’s methodological rigor [ Figure 1 ].

Data collection tools and technique

Data were collected using three instruments: a demographic questionnaire, the Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCV-19S), and the Self-Care in COVID-19 Scale (SCOVID).

Demographic questionnaire

The demographic survey was a self-reported questionnaire comprising questions on age, gender, marital status, education level, place of residence, income, insurance status, height, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), Fasting Blood Sugar (FBS), Regular Blood Sugar (RBS), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c).

Fear of COVID-19 scale (FCV-19S)

The FCV-19S is a 7-item tool developed by Ahorsu et al .[ 30 ] to measure COVID-19-associated fear. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 7 to 35. Higher scores indicate greater fear. The scale’s reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82) and concurrent validity have been established. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.813, which confirms good internal consistency.

Self-care in COVID-19 scale (SCOVID)

The SCOVID is a reliable and valid scale developed by De Maria et al . (2020)[ 22 ] to measure self-protection during the COVID-19 pandemic. The scale consists of 20 items divided into five domains: personal protective measures, social distancing, environmental disinfection, mental health, and healthy lifestyle. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Not at all = 1” to “Always = 5”. The total score ranges from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater levels of self-protection. The validity of the scale was established through construct and content validity, with a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 0.94. The reliability of the scale, assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, was reported as 0.88 for the entire scale. In this study, the main version of the scale was first culturally checked and translated into Persian using the forward-backward translation method. It should be noted that the translation process was conducted under the supervision of the scale developer, and the final Persian version of the SCOVID was approved by her. Then, the CVI and the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) for all 20 items were calculated to be 0.89 and 0.92, respectively. Additionally, we examined reliability using the internal consistency method with a sample of 30 diabetic patients who met the study eligibility criteria. Accordingly, Cronbach’s alpha for the entire scale was measured at 0.94, with domain-specific values ranging from 0.86 to 0.96.

Intervention

Before the intervention (T0), a pretest was conducted, and all participants completed the study questionnaires. The intervention group received patient education over two weeks via two social messaging applications, WhatsApp and Telegram. The initial session included a face-to-face orientation meeting featuring lectures, counseling, a question-and-answer segment about the study objectives, and the collection of written informed consent. Educational content was then delivered in a predetermined sequence using various formats, including text messages, audio files, photos, and videos. To ensure participants received and reviewed the materials, follow-up phone calls were conducted during the intervention period.

The educational content addressed a range of COVID-19-related topics, including disease characteristics, symptoms, modes of transmission, treatment options, vaccination, dietary recommendations, quarantine guidelines for patients and families, personal hygiene practices (e.g., handwashing), surface disinfection techniques, and identification of high-risk areas such as bathrooms and toilets. The materials were developed based on guidelines from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and protocols issued by Iran’s Ministry of Health, Treatment, and Medical Education. Participants in the control group received only routine diabetes-related care provided by the medical centers.

Immediately following the intervention (T1), the FCV-19S and SCOVID were administered to both groups via phone calls to assess COVID-19-associated fear and self-care levels. Follow-up assessments using the same instruments were conducted at two months (T2) and six months (T3) after the intervention to evaluate the medium- and long-term effects of the nurse-led, social media-based patient education on participants’ fear and self-care levels.

Participants were blinded to their group allocation throughout the study. To ensure ethical compliance, the control group was provided with the same educational content as the intervention group after the completion of the study.

Statistical analysis

Data were described using descriptive statistics, including percentage, mean, and standard deviation. The between-group and within-group comparisons were conducted using inferential statistics, including independent t -test, Chi-squared test, Mauchly’s test, and Greenhouse-Geisser correction. All processes of data analysis were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Moreover, the level of statistical significance was considered to be less than 0.05 ( P < .05).

## Results

Results

Demographic characteristics

The results of the independent-sample t -test indicated no statistically significant differences between the intervention and control groups in terms of quantitative demographic variables, including age, BMI, FBS, non-FBS, and HbA1c before the intervention ( P > 0.05). Similarly, the results of the Chi-squared test revealed no significant differences in qualitative demographic variables, such as gender, marital status, and education level, between the two groups ( P > 0.05). In other words, the two groups were homogenous in terms of both quantitative and qualitative variables [ Table 1 ].

Outcome variables

Fear

No statistically significant difference was observed in the mean fear scores between the intervention and control groups at T0 ( P = 0.066). However, the intervention group demonstrated a significant reduction in fear scores (19.00 ± 4.59) compared to the control group (17.46 ± 5.83) at T1 ( P = 0.003) [ Table 2 ].

Comparison of the Mean Fear Scores Between the Intervention and Control Groups Across the Measurement Timepoints

*Independent-samples t -test

The mean fear score had a further decrease in the intervention group at T2 (14.43 ± 4.86), while the control group’s mean fear score increased (19.34 ± 6.09) so that the difference was shown to be significant at this follow-up timepoint between the two groups ( P < 0.001). By T3, the mean score of fear had further reduced in the intervention group (13.55 ± 4.90) compared to that in the control group (18.80 ± 5.96), with the difference remaining statistically significant between the two groups ( P < 0.001). These results highlight the sustained effect of the intervention on reducing COVID-19-associated fear over time [ Table 2 ].

Mauchly’s test of sphericity indicated a violation of the sphericity assumption for fear ( P < 0.001); therefore, Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. The repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant time effect ( P < 0.001), based on which notable changes were indicated in fear mean scores over the study period. Additionally, a significant interaction effect between time and group highlighted that the trajectory of fear score changes differed considerably between the intervention and control groups ( P < 0.001) [ Tables 3 and 4 , Figure 2 ].

Self-care

At T0, there was no statistically significant difference in the mean self-care score between the intervention and control groups ( P = 0.085). At T1, the mean score of self-care in the intervention group increased significantly (78.47 ± 11.07) compared to that in the control group (73.13 ± 11.62), with the difference reaching statistical significance ( P = 0.014) [ Table 5 ].

Comparison of the Mean Self-Care Scores Between the Intervention and Control Groups Across the Measurement Timepoints

*Independent-samples t -test

A statistically significant difference was also found in the mean score of self-care between the two groups at T2 ( P < 0.001), where the mean score of self-care in the intervention group had risen further (81.00 ± 11.03) compared to the control group (74.01 ± 9.96). The difference continued to be statistically significant at T3 ( P < 0.001), where the intervention group achieved a higher significant mean self-care score (81.56 ± 11.14) compared to the control group (73.83 ± 10.83). These findings highlight the long-term effectiveness of the intervention in improving self-care levels over time [ Table 5 ].

Given the significance of Mauchly’s test, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. The results indicated that both the main effect of time and the interaction effect between time and group were statistically significant ( P < 0.001). This suggests that self-care levels changed over time and that these changes differed between the two groups. Furthermore, the mean difference in the mean score of self-care was also statistically significant between the intervention and control groups ( P = 0.045), which indicates that the intervention group experienced a significantly greater improvement in self-care levels over time compared to the control group [ Tables 6 and 7 , Figure 3 ].

## Discussion

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of a nurse-led, social media-based patient education program on COVID-19-associated fear and self-care among diabetic patients over a 6-month follow-up period. The findings indicated that participants in the intervention group, who received structured educational content via social media, experienced a greater reduction in fear and a more substantial improvement in self-care practices compared to the control group, which received standard care with no added intervention. These results underscore the potential benefits of integrating social media into patient education strategies, particularly during public health crises like the COVID-19 pandemic.

In terms of fear reduction, a significant decline was observed in the intervention group over time, whereas the control group either showed minimal improvements or an upward trend in fear scores. The final mean fear score in the intervention group was also notably lower compared to the baseline, emphasizing the sustainable effectiveness of social media-based education. This observation is supported by similar research demonstrating the value of digital health technologies in alleviating psychological distress. In this regrad, Prakasam et al . (2017) and Marker et al . (2020) reported that smartphone and video-based platforms reduced fear, stress, and distress among both patients and caregivers in diabetes management.[ 31 32 ] Moreover, Abbas et al . (2021) highlighted that accurate, credible social media content during the COVID-19 pandemic helps mitigate mental health problems, including fear, uncertainty, and stress.[ 33 ]

Some researchers came to different results from the ones we obtained in our study. Ahmad and Murad (2020) and Shehata and Abdeldaim (2022) conducted studies about the effect of social media on the spread of panic during the COVID-19 pandemic. They showed that getting data and news about COVID-19 can increase the level of panic among people so that the majority of their participants agreed that the excessive use of social media platforms for gaining information about the COVID-19 pandemic can worsen the spread of panic in the community.[ 34 35 ] Hammad and Alqarni (2021) also showed that there is a positive relationship between the excessive use of social media platforms during the COVID-19 pandemic and the level of fear and anxiety among people.[ 36 ]

Differences in study designs, the provision (or lack) of structured educational materials, and cultural factors likely explain these divergent outcomes. As such, it is essential that social media initiatives are carefully structured and contain reliable information. This approach may alleviate anxiety by dispelling misinformation and fostering greater patient trust.[ 37 38 ] Echoing this point, Talie Fenta et al . (2024)[ 39 ] showed that social media could be a double-edged sword—effective in sharing preventive measures but also prone to the spread of rumors and misinformation, thereby influencing fear and anxiety.

Similarly, the significant improvements in self-care among the intervention group reinforce the growing body of literature on the positive role of social media in promoting health behaviors. Consistent with Leong et al . (2022)[ 40 ] and Ghahramani et al . (2022),[ 41 ] our results demonstrated that educational videos and interactive content can enhance patients’ knowledge, attitudes, and overall engagement in self-care activities. These findings are particularly relevant in contexts where face-to-face education may be limited due to social distancing measures. As our results suggest, social media platforms offer a practical and scalable solution for reaching large patient populations with timely, evidence-based educational content.

The 6-month follow-up provided valuable insights into the long-term effectiveness of the intervention. Prolonged follow-up periods enable the observation of sustained behavioral changes and the emergence of any delayed effects. Studies by Raesi et al . (2021) and Houben-Wilke et al . (2022) similarly highlighted the importance of extended follow-ups for detecting persisting symptoms and evaluating the lasting impact of educational strategies on patients’ mental and physical well-being.[ 42 43 ] In this study, consistent improvements in self-care and sustained reductions in fear underscore the significance of continued engagement and follow-up for maintaining positive patient outcomes over time. Notably, while the greatest reduction in fear occurred between T1 and T2, the effect remained stable between T2 and T3, maintaining statistical significance. This suggests that the intervention’s benefits were sustained throughout the 6-month follow-up period without substantial decline.

Overall, our findings suggest that nurse-led education delivered through social media can serve as a feasible and effective strategy to manage fear and improve self-care among diabetic patients confronting the challenges posed by COVID-19. This approach not only provides real-time, accessible, and structured education but also offers an opportunity to curtail the spread of misinformation, thereby mitigating psychological distress while promoting beneficial health practices.

Limitations and recommendation

Considering the questionnaire-based method of data collection, the data gathered might not represent all the facts associated with the phenomena examined in the study. Moreover, the emotional state of the participants during the completion of the questionnaires may affect their answers. However, it was tried to address this limitation by presenting the questionnaires to the participants at the right time and place and asking them to accurately complete the questionnaires.

## Conclusion

Conclusion

This study’s findings indicate that a social media-based, nurse-led educational intervention effectively reduces COVID-19-related fear and enhances self-care behaviors among diabetic patients. By delivering accurate, structured content and maintaining sustained engagement over six months, this approach has proven both practical and scalable. The results underscore the potential of social media as a powerful platform for patient education, especially during crises that limit in-person interactions. To maximize its impact, health policymakers and healthcare professionals should integrate interactive, evidence-based social media strategies into routine diabetes care. Such strategies can address both the psychological and physical needs of patients, and ultimately ensure holistic care during and beyond the COVID-19 pandemic.

Practice implications

Healthcare policymakers and medical centers should capitalize on internet-based technologies, particularly social media platforms, to provide safer and more efficient patient education during pandemics. Training programs for nurses on effectively using these platforms can enhance the delivery of accurate, standardized information, thereby reducing misinformation and alleviating patient fears. By addressing these psychological and informational challenges, this approach can improve self-care practices among individuals with underlying conditions, particularly diabetes, and ultimately reduce the burden on healthcare systems while enhancing patient outcomes.
