# Autologous Platelet-Rich Gel for the Treatment of Diabetic Sinus Tract Wounds: A Clinical Study.

## Abstract

Background: The aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of autologous platelet-rich gel (APG) in the treatment of deep sinus tract wounds from diabetic ulcers.

Methods: Forty-eight patients with diabetic ulcers were randomly classified into two groups: an APG treatment group (25 patients) and a conventional wound dressing control group (23 patients). The sinus tract closure times, ulcer healing rates, hospitalization times, and hospitalization expenses of the two groups were compared.

Results: There were no significant differences in the basic data and wound conditions between the two groups. The cure (healed wound) rates were 96% and 87% for the APG group and control group, respectively. During the first 4 wk, the sinus tract closure rate for the APG group was significantly higher than that for the control group. However, there was no significant difference in the sinus tract healing between the two groups at the end of the 8th wk. For the APG group and the control group, the average hospital stays were 19.36 ± 7.239 d and 48.13 ± 11.721 d, respectively, and the total hospitalization expenses were 2.48 ± 0.45 ten thousand yuan and 5.63 ± 1.35 ten thousand yuan (P < 0.05), respectively. These differences were statistically significant.

Conclusions: When compared with conventional wound dressings, APG can accelerate the healing of deep sinus tract wounds associated with diabetic ulcers.

Open table in a new tab APG preparation The autologous platelet concentrates were prepared using a KYZ-2 medical centrifuge (Beijing Keyizhen Yanshan Medical Technology Co, Ltd, Beijing, China). The platelet concentrates were obtained from 20 mL of the patient's own peripheral blood, which was placed into a centrifuge tube containing potassium citrate. The sample was then centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 4 min to obtain the plasma (supernatant). Then, the plasma was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 6 min to collect the platelets from the pellet at the bottom of the centrifuge tube. The pellet diluted in 3 mL of plasma was considered to be platelet-rich plasma, whereas the remaining plasma was considered to be platelet-poor plasma. Next, 5000 U of thrombin was mixed with calcium chloride to create a mixture of 1000 U/mL. Finally, the previously prepared platelet-rich plasma and the thrombin mixture were combined at a ratio of 10:1 to obtain the APG (Fig. 2). Fig. 2 Preparation of APG. Group intervention The experimental group included 25 patients. Each of these patients' wounds were carefully disinfected, and the wounds were prepared (debrided) by removing the inactive and necrotic tissue. The debridement process was carried out carefully, moving from the shallow to the deep areas. Then, the patient's APG was placed into the wound tract and on the ulcer to cover the wound. Next, the wound was covered with a layer of Vaseline Petrolatum Gauze (Unilever, London, United Kingdom), and a pressure bandage was applied. This process was repeated weekly until the wound healed. The wound dressing frequency was once every 3 d, and the wound area and sinus tract depth were measured and recorded by the physician once a week. The control group included 23 patients, and the initial debridement was performed the same way as in the APG group (described previously). These patients were treated with dressings only, which involved cleaning the wound with normal saline and scratching the wound lightly until it bled. Then 0.1% rivanor (2-ethoxy-6, 9-aminoacridine lactate) gauze was applied, and the wound was covered with a sterile dressing. This process was repeated twice a week until the wound healed. During this time, the wound area and the sinus tract depth were measured once a week. The wound care procedures and APG preparations were performed by the same clinical team in one of the hospital's operating rooms, following the sterility protocols. Pictures were taken to record the wound changes over time, and the same camera was used for all the patients. During the course of the treatment, each of the patients' blood glucose levels was strictly monitored to rule out poor glycemic control as a wound healing factor. The wound changes, healing times, healing rates, and hospitalization costs of each patient were recorded. Histologic evaluation The granulation tissues from the patients’ wounds were collected at the end of the second and 4th wk (sample size of approximately 0.5 × 0.5 cm). These tissue samples were embedded in paraffin and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining. First, 4- to 8-μm thick slices were cut using a microtome. These were placed on glass slides and soaked in warm water (40°C) to fully stretch the tissue. Next, each slide was placed in xylene and fully immersed for 10 min; then, the xylene was replaced, and the immersion continued for 10 more minutes. The purpose was to dissolve the paraffin in the slice with xylene so that the dye solution could fully enter the tissue while it was being dyed. The xylene-soaked slices were then immersed in absolute ethanol to elute the xylene that was used in the dewaxing process so that water could enter the tissue. The slices were immersed in a series of ethanol concentrations (95%, 85%, and 70% for 5 min each) to achieve full hydration. The hydrated sections were washed in a phosphate-buffered saline solution for 5 min a total of three times. Then, a prepared hematoxylin and eosin staining solution was aspirated with a pipette, and 100 μL was dropped onto each tissue section. The sections were allowed to stain for 10 min before the staining solution was washed away with distilled water. For the differentiation, 1% ethanol was used, and then, the tissue sections were rinsed with double distilled water. A weakly alkaline blue-promoting solution was added to the tissue sections so that the nuclei would stain blue. The samples were then rinsed with double distilled water. Hematoxylin–eosin dye solution was added to the tissue samples, and they were allowed to stain for 3 min. Then, the tissue sections were immersed in a series of ethanol concentrations (80% and 95% and anhydrous ethanol for 5 s, 2 min, and 2 min, respectively) to achieve full dehydration. The dehydrated tissue samples were soaked twice in xylene for 4 min, and then, the tissue samples were allowed to dry and sealed with neutral resin. Finally, the slides were evaluated and photographed using a microscope. (Color version of figure is available online.) Discussion Wound healing consists of four distinct processes: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. However, the healing process in diabetic patients does not follow this timeline. Delayed wound healing in diabetic patients can be caused by chronic neuropathy, vascular diseases, deep infections, or a lack of cell growth factors.8,9 Moreover, the level of the infection and infiltration is deep, forming a sinus tract that is resistant to healing. Despite proper wound care and the corresponding treatment, these wounds still have huge impacts on the amputation rate and quality of life of these patients. In diabetic patients, the normal healing process is often interrupted, resulting in wounds that have stalled in one or more of the abovementioned healing phases.10 The ulcers can become chronic in nature because of the lack of growth factors and cytokines, which delays the healing process.11 The initial treatment approach for sinus tract wounds is conservative, historically involving gauze or gauze ribbon packing. However, this has been proven to contribute to prolonged treatment and sinus tract persistence. Better treatment results are needed, and most patients prefer limb salvage to amputation. Moreover, a noninvasive therapeutic process is better than an invasive one, especially when the same results are expected. In this clinical trial, APG was used as a noninvasive treatment method for chronic diabetic ulcers, and good results were obtained (Fig. 4). The wound healing time and sinus closure time of the APG group were significantly shorter than those of the control group. In addition, the hospitalization period of the patients in the APG group was significantly shortened, and the hospitalization cost was greatly reduced when compared with the control group. APG releases platelet-associated growth factors after activation by thrombin and calcium, including the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), PDGF, epidermal growth factor (EGF), transforming growth factor β, fibroblast growth factor, insulin-like growth factor 1, platelet thromboplastin, fibrinogen, and coagulation factors.12 These growth factors are closely related to wound healing. The addition of calcium gluconate promotes the gradual formation of native thrombin, and it mimics the physiological coagulation process, allowing for the sustained release of growth factors.13 These encourage the synthesis and deposition of the matrix, promote the formation of fibrous granulation tissue, improve collagen synthesis, and play important roles in posttraumatic epithelial regeneration, interstitial hyperplasia, and neovascularization.14 VEGF is a potent inducer of angiogenesis that affects the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells, which are the two major steps of angiogenesis. In addition, VEGF regulates angiogenic lesions, and the new blood vessels can bring other substances to promote wound healing. VEGF is also responsible for inducing other vascular genetic factors.15-18 PDGF promotes the rapid formation of granulation tissue by increasing cell proliferation, wound fibroblast and inflammatory cell infiltration, wound tissue collagen synthesis, and granulation tissue growth.19 EGF promotes cell mitosis and cell proliferation, which promote wound healing.20 Transforming growth factor β promotes epidermal cell migration, macrophage and fibroblast chemotaxis, and extracellular matrix synthesis and shaping, and it reduces scar formation. Fibroblast growth factor promotes mitosis in mesodermal and neuroectodermally derived cells, producing large amounts of collagen and fibroblasts, which constitute the extracellular matrix, whereas insulin-like growth factor and PDGF synergistically increase the regeneration of the epidermis and endothelium.21 The platelets, fibroblasts, and microvascular cells encourage tissue regrowth, which helps wounds heal.22 APG also contains dense particles that release large amounts of serotonin (5-HT), which may explain the pain relief after clinical injection of platelet leukocyte gel.23 As a local dressing, the role of APG is to provide the required growth factors for wound healing. It has been shown previously that diabetic ulcers are growth factor deficient.24 APG also exhibits an antibacterial effect, providing a good microenvironment for wound repair. The leukocytic inflammatory factors in APG, such as the interleukins, tumor necrosis factor, and myeloperoxidase, are involved in local anti-infective immune protection. As early as 1992, Yeaman et al.25 found that rabbit platelets could release small molecular weight antimicrobial proteins under the stimulation of a physiological concentration of thrombin, which was then purified to obtain this protein. It has also been proposed that platelets can inhibit tissue damage and microbial aggregation by releasing antibacterial proteins locally, suggesting that the host plays an important role in fighting infection. In 2002, a research team purified and isolated the human platelet supernatant from thrombin-stimulated peptides with antibacterial activity, confirming the antibacterial effect of platelets on Staphylococcus aureus.26 The use of APG, which is rich in white blood cells and platelets, to treat diabetic ulcers is a simple and inexpensive method that is widely used in many countries. However, it is rarely applied in the clinical setting in China. The APG preparation takes only 30-40 min, the process is simple, and it requires less technical equipment and fewer consumable substances. Moreover, the patient does not need to undergo a skin graft or flap transplantation. This reduces the patient's pain and trauma, and it does not increase the wound care burden or patient risk during treatment. In addition, the patient does not need to be hospitalized because this technique can be performed as an outpatient procedure, which may have certain medical and economic advantages while promoting wound healing. A physiological anatomical or “perfect” repair, which has been advocated in recent years, is the best embodiment of regenerative medicine available in the clinical setting. APG has certain advantages in the treatment of sinus wound. Although some sinus openings are relatively narrow, the injection of gelatinous APG can reach deep part of the wound. Regardless of the depth of the sinus, APG can be placed directly into the sinus cavity, fully covering the wound surface to maximize the cytokine effects. There are many reasons why APG is used less frequently in China. For instance, each time that the APG is prepared, the patient's own blood must be taken. At the same time, the collection conditions, preparation techniques, and specific environmental requirements create clinical application difficulties, and the patients and doctors are extremely resistant to these changes. In addition, because of the short preservation time and the specific storage conditions of conventionally preserved platelets, it is difficult to ensure a continuous supply of APG in clinical practice to meet the needs for timely treatments. In the future, we can explore how to prepare and store all the APG treatments at one time so that blood does not have to be taken from the patient during each treatment session. Alternatively, APG could be prepared as a lyophilized powder that can be activated by adding thrombin and calcium for each use. This may encourage its wider use in clinical practice. This study did have some limitations. For example, the number of patients was very small. Most of these patients undergo amputations because they seek medical care after it is too late for treatment. Moreover, the APG dose used by each patient did not have a fixed standard. Future randomized controlled trials need to be performed in larger populations to standardize the procedures, APG doses, and platelet concentrations for better results. Although APG therapy is not recommended as a first-line treatment, from our treatment experience, for patients with relatively small tissue defect caused by snus tract, or intolerant to surgical repair of wounds, compared with the routine dressing change, we believe that the use of APG gel is a more effective treatment. Conclusions The results of this study showed that, compared with routine dressing change, APG can accelerate the healing of diabetic ulcer sinus tract, shorten the healing time of clinical difficult wound, and shorten the hospital stay. APG can also accelerate the conversion of bacteria culture from positive to negative and has a certain antibacterial effect. In addition, APG is a safe, simple, and nonadverse treatment.
