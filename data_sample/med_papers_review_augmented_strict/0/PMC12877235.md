# Impact of diabetes on the association between serum urate levels and incident dementia: a cohort study in the UK biobank.

## Abstract

Objectives: Diabetes was associated with increased serum urate levels and a higher risk of dementia. However, current evidence regarding the association between serum urate and dementia is controversial.The research gap on how to effectively control urate levels in the population with diabetes still remains. We aim to examine the association of diabetes status and serum urate with dementia incidence, and the differences in this association among participants with different diabetes statuses.

Methods: A total of 321,896 participants was recruited from the UK Biobank and followed up until 2022. Diabetes status was classified into diabetes, prediabetes and normoglycaemia according to the American Diabetes Association 2023 guideline. Serum urate levels were stratified using gender-specific quartiles of concentrations. All-cause dementia, Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia were ascertained using the International Classification of Diseases-10th revision (ICD-10). Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to examine the association between serum urate, diabetes status, and dementia incidence.

Results: Of the 321,896 participants (mean age, 57 years old; 43.5% males), 7,087 (2.20%) individuals were diagnosed with dementia during the follow-up period. Diabetes was associated with a 70% 58%, and 134% increased risk for all-cause dementia, Alzheimer's disease, and vascular dementia respectively. Elevated serum urate levels were associated with a lower risk of all-cause and cause-specific dementia regardless of the status of diabetes. Each standard deviation increase in urate concentration was related to a 11% reduced risk for all-cause dementia (HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.86 to 0.91), 7% for Alzheimer's disease (HR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.88 to 0.98), and 12% for vascular dementia (HR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.81 to 0.95).

Conclusion: Appropriately higher urate levels within the threshold of hyperuricemia can reduce the adverse health effects of excessively high urate levels and better protect the cognitive health of people with varying diabetes status.

## Introduction

Introduction

The global prevalence of dementia among the elderly is continuously rising, with approximately 50 million people worldwide living with dementia in 2020 and is projected to increase to 152 million by 2050 [ 1 , 2 ]. As a main cause of disability among the elderly, it’s important to identify the risk and protective factors of dementia to prevent or delay the disease progression. Serum urate (uric acid) is a byproduct of purine metabolism within the human body. As a potent antioxidant in the body, it has become an increasingly researched topic in recent years with a focus on its impact on neurodegenerative diseases. We summarized the existing evidence on serum urate or gout and dementia or cognitive function in recent 15 years. However, owing to the sample size restrictions in previous studies, the evidence for the role of serum urate in all-cause and cause-specific dementia is still highly controversial [ [3] , [4] , [5] , [6] ].

Diabetes is a worldwide chronic disease, affecting approximately 529 million individuals of all ages by the year 2021. Prediabetes is a high-risk condition for developing diabetes but does not meet the standard diagnostic criteria, and projected to a 20% increasement over the next decade [ [7] , [8] , [9] ]. The 2020 Lancet Commission on Dementia highlights that roughly 40% of the global burden of dementia can be linked to 12 modifiable risk factors including diabetes, presenting a significant threat to the cognitive health of elderly individuals [ [10] , [11] , [12] ]. Due to insulin resistance and the decline in renal function, people with prediabetes and diabetes have significantly higher serum urate levels than normal individuals [ [13] , [14] , [15] , [16] ]. Elevated serum urate may be linked to numerous negative health effects; thus, accentuated attention is crucial in managing serum urate among the diabetic population. However, there is currently no research focusing on the relationship between serum urate levels and dementia in populations with diabetes and prediabetes. How to reasonably control the serum urate levels of diabetes and prediabetes populations in order to better protect their cognitive functions remains a knowledge gap. Further studies not only need to confirm the inconclusive correlation between serum urate levels and dementia, but also to further explore the potential variations in this correlation among populations with varying diabetes status.

In this large population-based cohort study from UK Biobank, we aimed to investigate the associations between serum urate levels and incident of all-cause and cause-specific dementia including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and vascular dementia (VaD), especially the discrepancies among individuals with and without diabetes.

## Research design and methods

Research design and methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a large population-based prospective study, which recruited over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 from 22 assessment centers of England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. The assessment visit included electronic signed consent, a self-completed touch-screen questionnaire, a brief computer-assisted interview, physical and functional measures, and the collection of blood, urine, and saliva, with the aim of investigating the genetic and non-genetic determinants of diseases in middle-aged and elderly populations [ 17 ].

In this study, we identified participants’ diabetes status with normoglycemia, prediabetes, or diabetes according to the standards of the 2023 American Diabetes Association guideline. After excluding individuals with missing data on serum urate levels, those with pre-existing dementia at baseline, and those subsequently withdrawn from the UK Biobank, a total of 321,896 participants were finally included as shown in Fig. 1 . UK Biobank has approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (MREC) as a Research Tissue Bank (RTB) approval, and all participants provided written informed consent. Assessment of serum urate

Participants in the UK Biobank had blood samples collected at baseline and stored at both −80 °C and in liquid nitrogen, in accordance with the sample handling and storage protocols at the time of sample collection. Serum urate levels were measured using the uricase Pedigree Analysis Package analysis on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 platform (Randox Biosciences, Crumlin, United Kingdom). Giving the consistent lower levels of serum urate concentrations in women both pre- and post-menopause compared to men, we used gender-specific quartiles of urate levels as grouping criteria in the entire study population and those with normoglycemia, prediabetes, and diabetes respectively, to mitigate potential gender-related confounding biases [ 6 , 18 ] ( Supplementary Table S17 ).

Definitions of prediabetes and diabetes

According to the standards by the 2023 American Diabetes Association guideline, participants were classified into diabetes, prediabetes, and normoglycemia. Due to non-fasting blood glucose measurements in the UK Biobank, only Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) concentrations were utilized for the classification process [ 19 ]. The definition of diabetes encompassed diagnosed diabetes by a physician during the touchscreen questionnaire or through hospital inpatient records diagnoses indicated by the International Classification of Diseases-10th revision (ICD-10) coding (E10, E11, E12, E13, and E14), or self-reported use of insulin or hypoglycemic medication ( Supplementary Table S2 ) during the touchscreen questionnaire or nurse interview, or the presence of an HbA1c level ≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol). Prediabetes was defined as having a HbA1c level of 5.7–6.5 % (39–48 mmol/mol) without a record of diabetes diagnosis or use of insulin and hypoglycemic medication. Normoglycemia was defined as having an HbA1c level <5.7% without a record of diabetes diagnosis or use of insulin and hypoglycemic medication [ [20] , [21] , [22] ].

Definition of outcome events

Outcome events were ascertained using hospital inpatient records containing data on admissions and diagnoses obtained from the Hospital Episode Statistics for England, the Scottish Morbidity Record data for Scotland, and the Patient Episode Database for Wales. In our study, the outcome event was the occurrence of dementia, and was defined using the following ICD-10 codes: all-cause dementia (F00, F01, F02, F03, F05.1, G30, G31.1, and G31.8), Alzheimer’s disease (F00 and G30) and vascular dementia (F01) [ 23 ].

Covariates

Based on previous studies, we selected the following potential confounding variables. Sociodemographic factors include age, sex, ethnicity (white and other ethnic group), educational attainment (high: college or university degree; intermediate: A/AS levels or equivalent, O levels/GCSEs or equivalent; and low: none of the aforementioned) [ 24 ], and the Townsend deprivation index. Lifestyle habits include smoking status (current, previous, or never), alcohol drinking status (current, previous, or never), and sleep duration. Physical measurements include BMI, forced expiratory volume (FEV 1 , litre), forced vital capacity (FVC, litre), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (DBP, mmHg). Past medical history includes whether has been diagnosed with cancer, angina, heart attack, or stroke (Yes or No). Additionally, blood biochemistry indicators include HbA1c (mmol/mol), apolipoprotein E (ApoE) ε4 carrier status (Yes or No), urate (μmol/L), high-density lipoprotein (HDL, mmol/L), low-density lipoprotein (LDL, mmol/L), and C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m 2 ). The eGFR was calculated using the 2021 CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) cystatin C/creatinine eGFR equation [ 25 ]. The highest missing proportion of variables was only around 15%, we conducted multiple imputations of missing data using the fully conditional specification method, using the predictive mean matching for numeric data, logistic regression imputation for binary data, and polytomous regression imputation for categorical data.

Statistical analyses

Firstly, we separately performed a statistical description of baseline information based on the presence of dementia diagnosis and gender. We used numbers (%) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. For continuous variables, between-group comparisons were conducted using t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, while for categorical variables, the chi-square test.

We utilized Cox regression with age as the timescale to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate the association of diabetes status (normoglycemia, prediabetes, diabetes, with normoglycemia as the reference) with all-cause and cause-specific dementia. Then, we investigated the relationship between serum urate levels (various percentiles with the 1st quartile as the reference, and changes per standard deviation) and dementia in all participants. And subgroup analyses were conducted based on renal function (eGFR <60 or ≥90) and ApoE ε4 haplotype (carrier or not). Subsequently, we explored the potential nonlinear associations and the possible gender differences between serum urate concentrations and the risks of all-cause dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, and vascular dementia using restricted cubic splines, with sex-specific first quartiles of serum urate concentration (305.9 μmol/L for male and 227.1 μmol/L for female) as the reference points. Finally, in individuals with diabetes, prediabetes, and normoglycemia, we separately investigated the association between serum urate levels and dementia to examine if any potential diabetes-related differences exist. To reduce model complexity and avoid overfitting, we used a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to select potential confounding variables in the association between urate and dementia, and obtained the minimum fully adjusted variable set including gender, ethnicity, alcohol drinking status, BMI, eGFR, and HbA1c ( Supplementary Fig. S1 ). The proportional hazard assumption was examined using Schoenfeld residuals and found no violation.

To ensure the reliability of the study results, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we identified the use of serum urate-lowering medications (including probenecid, allopurinol, colchicine, and sulfinpyrazone) in UKB participants, and further adjusted for that in our main analyses. Then we reclassified urate concentrations into low, normal, and high categories based on the respective cutoffs for males (3.5–7 mg/dL, equivalent to 208.18 and 416.36 μmol/L) and females (2.6–6 mg/dL, equivalent to 154.65 and 356.88 μmol/L), and reanalyzed the association between serum urate and dementia in all participants. We additional excluded a total of 1972 subjects who experienced outcome events or death within 2 years, and reanalyzed the association between diabetes and dementia, as well as the association between serum urate levels and dementia among all participants and participants with different diabetes status. Diabetes and incident dementia

A total of 44,863 (13.9%) participants were classified as having prediabetes, and 27,784 (8.6%) had diabetes at baseline. Individuals with diabetes or prediabetes exhibited higher incidence rates of all-cause dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, and vascular dementia compared to those with normoglycemia. After controlling for potential confounding variables, the presence of diabetes was significantly associated with a 70% increased risk of all-cause dementia (HR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.55–1.85), a 58% increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease (HR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.33–1.87), and notably, a 134% increased risk of vascular dementia (HR, 2.34; 95% CI, 1.94–2.81). However, our study revealed no significant association between prediabetes and dementia as referenced in Supplementary Table S4 .

Serum urate and incident dementia

We found that elevated serum urate was associated with a reduced risk of dementia when compared to the first quartile in general participants. For each standard deviation increment in urate concentrations, the multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio for all-cause dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, and vascular dementia were 0.89 (95% CI, 0.86 to 0.91) and 0.93 (95% CI, 0.88 to 0.98), and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.81 to 0.95), respectively ( Table 2 ). In the subgroups of renal function and ApoE ε4 haplotype, higher levels of serum urate showed a similar protective effect against all-cause dementia and vascular dementia to the main analyses, but the protective effect against Alzheimer’s disease became less pronounced. Only the third quartile of serum urate were observed to have a significant protective effect against dementia in the subgroups of Non-ApoE s4 carrier (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.68 to 0.98) and eGFR ≥90 (HR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.70–0.98) ( Supplementary Fig. S3 ). ## Discussion

Discussion

In this large-scale and nationally representative study, we observed that diabetes was associated with an increased risk of all-cause dementia, Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia, yet such association was not observed in participants with prediabetes; an appropriate increase in serum urate was linked to a decreased risk of all-cause and cause-specific dementia, a with no significant gender differences. Furthermore, this protective effect was also evident in participants across varying diabetes statuses and different genetic susceptibility.

The existing body of research investigating the risk for dementia in individuals with diabetes is quite wide-ranging. Diabetes is acknowledged as a prominent risk factor for dementia, potentially increasing the risk of all-cause dementia by 40%–90% [ 10 ]. However, we did not find that prediabetes was associated with a higher risk of dementia, which seemed to be inconsistent with some current studies. Nevertheless, Hu et al. conducted a prospective cohort study with 15,792 participants aged 45–64 years from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC), revealing that the link between prediabetes and dementia could be explained by the subsequent development of diabetes. Preventing the progression from prediabetes to diabetes could alleviate the burden of dementia [ 31 ]. Furthermore, in a prospective study based on 7 international observational studies from the STROKOG (Stroke and Cognition) consortium, Lo J. W et al. found that diabetes mellitus, but not prediabetes, is associated with poorer cognitive performance in patients 3–6 months after stroke [ 32 ]. Therefore, we speculate that the association between prediabetes and dementia may largely depend on the progression of prediabetes. If prediabetes does not progress to clinical diabetes in the future, it may not emerge as an independent risk factor for dementia. In our study, we found that higher serum urate level was associated with a lower risk of developing dementia, regardless of the participants’ diabetes status and genetic susceptibility. A similar outcome was noticed by Lu et al., where a longitudinal investigation into 59,224 patients suffering from gout and 238,805 matched non-gout individuals in The Health Improvement Network (THIN) of UK, revealed a 24% reduction in the risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease in gout patients [ 5 ]. Scheepers LEJM et al. conducted a prospective study on 1462 middle-aged and elderly women from the Prospective Population Study of Women in Gothenburg, and found that per SD increase in serum urate concentrations were associated with approximately 20% decreased risk for all-cause dementia and Alzheimer’s disease, and 34% decreased risk for vascular dementia [ 4 ]. The results of the above study are consistent with our study. Nevertheless, Latourte A et al.'s analysis of 1,598 elderly individuals from the Three-City Dijon Study (3C-Dijon) in France, where gender-dependent thresholds for uric acid in the serum were also used, associated the highest quartile of uric acid with a 50% increased risk of both Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia which contradicts our research findings [ 6 ]. The speculated reasons might be attributed to varying ethnicity, sample size and the significant temporal difference between these two cohorts. The mean age of subjects in Latourte A’s study was higher than that in our study (72.4 vs. 57.5), which could lead to older adults with high uric acid facing more health issues and indirectly increase the risk of dementia. Latourte A also found that there was no clear association between high serum uric acid levels and extensive white matter hyperintensities (a biomarker of cerebral small vessel disease). Similarly, our study identified hemorrhagic/ischemic stroke as one major component endpoints of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and found that CVD did not significantly mediate the association between serum urate and dementia. Mirroring our findings, Chen X et al.'s research on genetically manipulated mice and early Parkinson’s disease patients revealed that risk of increased blood pressure is negligible with therapeutic measures to raise uric acid levels [ 33 ]. This results align with our findings, suggesting that the cardiovascular risks associated with elevated uric acid levels may be far less important compared to its neuroprotective effects [ 34 ].

The physiological mechanisms explaining the high incidence of cognitive dysfunction in diabetic patients include diabetes-induced deposition of toxic lipids and proteins on blood vessel walls, damaging endothelial cell function and impairing vasodilatory substances, leading to reduced cerebral blood flow. This condition can upregulate inflammatory mediators and damage the blood-brain barrier, causing abnormal neuronal activity [ [35] , [36] , [37] , [38] ]. We found that higher serum urate levels are protective against the onset of dementia, which may be due to its antioxidant function. Urate has previously been demonstrated to possess antioxidant properties, capable of effectively scavenging substances such as hydroxyl free radicals and reducing oxidative stress response [ 34 , 39 ]. Therefore, urate exerts neuroprotective effects by inhibiting the accumulation of oxygen free radicals and protecting mitochondrial function, inhibiting the cytotoxic activity of lactoperoxidases, and repairing free radical-induced DNA damage [ 40 , 41 ], demonstrating a protective effect against dementia incidence.

Upon performing a subgroup analysis and stratification on diabetes status, it appeared that the protective contribution of elevated serum urate levels to Alzheimer’s disease was less conspicuous compared to the overall population. In addition to suspecting a decrease in statistical power due to reduced sample size, there could also be an imbalanced distribution of unmeasured confounders between diabetic and non-diabetic participants that may also weaken the potential protective effects observed. However, greater protective effects for vascular dementia were still noticeable even though there were fewer cases than Alzheimer’s disease in different subgroups. We speculate that the antioxidant function of serum urate can better protect the function of cerebral small vessel and play a protective role against vascular dementia [ 42 , 43 ].

The concentration of urate at the 75th percentile for those with diabetes and prediabetes is approaching the threshold for hyperuricemia, while in individuals with normoglycemia, the 75th percentile concentration remains within the normal range. Nevertheless, excessive serum urate may lead to oxidative stress that exceeds its antioxidant capacity, increasing the risk of cerebrovascular risk factors [ 4 ]. Therefore, reasonably increasing serum urate concentration but below the threshold for hyperuricemia may not only help reduce the adverse health effects of hyperuricemia but also play its potentially cognitive protective role. In particular, for individuals with diabetes and prediabetes who face higher serum urate concentrations than the normal, this may provide some reference value for the clinical management of urate levels and cognitive protection in this people.

The strengths of our research lie in the large sample size and a follow-up period for over 10 years, ensuring a sufficient number of dementia cases. Based on previous studies, we additional assessed the association between serum urate and dementia among different diabetes status and potential gender differences. This may provide reliable evidence for the current research field. However, it must also be pointed out some limitations. First, we cannot exclude the influence of other unmeasured confounding factors, but we have identified and adjusted for potential confounders and changed the classification method for urate concentrations in sensitivity analysis. Second, we used only baseline measurements of serum urate and did not consider the impact of changes during the follow-up period on the onset of dementia.
