# Mass spectrometry and serum pattern profiling for analyzing the individual risk for endometriosis: promising insights?

## Abstract

Objective: To evaluate whether distinct patterns of serum proteins in symptomatic women are of value to predict endometriosis before laparoscopy.

Design: Prospective exploratory cohort study.

Setting: Tertiary care center.

Patient(s): A total of 91 consecutive symptomatic patients suffering from dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, chronic pelvic pain, or unexplained infertility.

Intervention(s): Collection of serum samples and a standardized protocol for patients' history before laparoscopic diagnosis.

Main outcome measure(s): Protein expression was analyzed by mass spectrometric analysis according to surface-enhanced laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (SELDI-TOF MS) standards. The analysis of data was performed using a genetic algorithm (ClinProTools 2.0 software) and a rule-based decision-tree algorithm (XLminer software).

Result(s): A total of 90 out of 91 samples were eligible for analysis. At laparoscopy, 51 of 90 patients (56.7%) exhibited endometriosis and 39 of 90 (43.3%) were disease free. Analyzing the serum samples, the software revealed a unique selection of mass peaks between 2,000 and 20,000 Da, which allowed for discrimination between patients suffering from endometriosis and control subjects. Overall recognition capacity was 70.8%, exhibiting a sensitivity of 81.3% (95% confidence interval [CI] 66.5-92.5) and a specificity of 60.3% (95% CI 46.1-74.2]) using the genetic algorithm, and a sensitivity of 78.4% and a specificity of 59.0% using the rule-based decision-tree algorithm.

Conclusion(s): These findings provide direct evidence that screening for serum protein patterns using SELDI-TOF MS before laparoscopy might be of discriminative value in the prediction of disease and partly confirms recently published data. However, in this prospective setting, we found both low sensitivity and low specificity, which disqualifies the screening for serum protein patterns by SELDI-TOF MS as a "quick fix" diagnostic test.

Key Words Endometriosis diagnosis serum protein pattern profiling proteomics SELDI-TOF mass spectrometry Endometriosis is one of the most common benign diseases among young women, affecting 10%–22% of all women of the reproductive age group (1–3). However, endometriosis is still an underdiagnosed disease and the interval between onset of symptoms and the diagnosis of disease is estimated to be between 3 and 7 years depending on which symptoms are predominant and the age of onset of those symptoms (4). A definitive diagnosis of endometriosis is based on direct visualization of peritoneal and ovarian implants at laparoscopy or laparotomy (5) and might be combined with histologic confirmation of lesion biopsies. Up to now, several screening markers from patient's serum, endometrium, and peritoneal fluid have been introduced to facilitate a decrease in the diagnostic delay and to preselect patients with a high risk for endometriosis for diagnostic laparoscopy and consecutive therapy (6). As a diagnostic tool, serum markers are of special interest, because serum is easily accessible for a screening test. Endometriosis is regarded as a polygenically inherited disease of complex multifactorial etiology (7). Among all theories, transplantation of endometrial tissue via retrograde menstruation is the most widely accepted hypothesis (8). Besides a massive exposure to menstrual efflux, a defect of clearance from pelvic peritoneum suggests that other factors, such as aberrant expression of certain cytokines and tissue matrix metalloproteinases as well as aberrant expression of aromatase may contribute to the development of the disease (9). In patients with endometriosis, C-reactive protein and several cytokines, including serum amyloid A (SAA), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), macrophage chemotactic protein 1 (MCP-1), interleukin (IL)-6 and -8, and cognate chemokine receptor 1 (CCR-1), are elevated in both peritoneal fluid and peripheral blood (10). This might indicate that endometriosis can be viewed as local disease with a systemic subclinical manifestation. Moreover, changes on the proteomic level might precede the clinical manifestation of disease, demonstrating differences between the healthy and diseased at an early stage. Therefore, the analysis of serum protein expression is of great interest to learn more about the pathogenesis of endometriosis, to gain further insights in the mechanisms involved and to explore the diagnostic feasibility of this screening procedure. To date, proteomic techniques such as surface-enhanced laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (SELDI-TOF MS) have successfully been used to differentiate between healthy and diseased individuals using serum samples of patients with ovarian cancer (11). A series of clinical applications of this new diagnostic tool followed for malignant diseases such as prostate cancer (12) and hepatocellular carcinoma (13). Recently, Liu et al. introduced a model for the detection of endometriosis using plasma protein profiling by SELDI-TOF MS (14). For the purpose of setting up a classification and regression tree as a diagnostic model, they used a training set and then analyzed 30 samples according to those model criteria. They found a sensitivity of 87.5% and a specificity of 85.7%. However, the setting of the study was not prospective-explorative, and the sample size was limited to 30 plasma samples. In the present prospective-explorative study, we aimed to evaluate whether distinct patterns of proteins in serum samples of symptomatic women can be used to predict endometriosis before laparoscopy. Such analysis would allow for minimal invasive diagnosis of disease. Materials and methods Patients, Tissue Sampling and Processing This study was designed as a prospective-explorative cohort trial and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (project EK 137/04). All participating patients signed an informed consent form before enrollment in this trial. A total of 91 women aged 22 to 47 years (mean 32.3 years) presenting to this tertiary care center for diagnosis and/or treatment of dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, chronic pelvic pain, or unexplained infertility were selected. Before enrollment, estrogen-dependent diseases (EDD) other than endometriosis were excluded clinically by gynecologic examination and transvaginal ultrasound. None of the patients were diagnosed with endometriosis or other EDD or received endocrine therapy such as GnRH analogues or danazol before inclusion in the study. Women using oral contraceptives or nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs were included in this trial, but were analyzed separately owing to potentially altered endocrine regulation and protein activity in the serum. Menstrual characteristics as well as other demographic data were collected in a standardized interview, and all participants completed a questionnaire including visual analog scales to assess the level of dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, and (chronic) pelvic pain irrespective of menstrual symptoms and sexual intercourse. Blood samples were obtained before laparoscopy by venipuncture and processed within 30 min to minimize autolytic effects (15). In brief, after the blood samples were centrifuged, serum aliquots were frozen at −80°C. Repeated freeze/thaw cycles during further proceedings were avoided to protect protein integrity. Endometriosis was diagnosed by laparoscopic visualization (5), the current gold standard, followed by histopathologic assessment of putative lesions. The stage of endometriosis was assigned during laparoscopy according to the revised American Fertility Society classification (rAFS) standards (16). SELDI-TOF Sample preparation We used Q10 (anionic exchange surface) ProteinChips (Ciphergen, Freemont, CA) for this study using a protocol optimized for serum. The chips were pretreated (5 min 10 mmol/L HCl, 5 min 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate, 0.1% Triton X100). Five microliters of serum was diluted with 45 μL of 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 0.1% Triton X100 (binding puffer, pH 7.0), and 2 μL of this dilution was incubated onto the chip surfaces (20 min, room temperature, humid atmosphere, vigorous shaking) using the bioprocessor device (Ciphergen). The pH value of the binding puffer solution was 7.0 to impart an overall net positive charge on the proteins, resulting in less binding and therefore higher specificity of the binding (according to the manufacturer's recommendations). The unbound proteins were removed by washing steps (3 × phosphate-buffered saline, 3 × aqua bidest.). After air drying, two applications of 0.8 μL matrix solution (saturated solution of 4-hydroxy-3,5-dimethoxicinnamic acid in 50% acetonitrile with 0.5% trifluoroacetic acid) finished the preparation. Special care was taken to standardize every detail of the protocol to avoid artificial results due to biased sampling. Open table in a new tab Data Analysis Mass spectra obtained were sorted into disease-related classes and analyzed by ClinProTools 2.0 software (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA) using the genetic algorithm method. According to standardized procedures, each spectrum underwent multiple preprocessing steps during the loading procedure. One of these is normalization to the total ion count (TIC). For each spectrum, the intensity values were accumulated. If the TIC was positive, each intensity value of the spectrum was scaled by the TIC value to obtain a normalized intensity value for that spectrum. A second normalization step was done after the peak-picking procedure. For each peak, its values are standardized such that the values of the peak were N(0,1) distributed. This means that the peak values have a mean of 0 and a variance of 1. This is common practice and makes peaks with large value differences more comparable. For analysis the spectra obtained by SELDI-TOF MS were exported as CSV files. Peak significance between groups was evaluated by Mann-Whitney U test. Open table in a new tab In addition, we applied the rule-based DTA for the analysis of data and found that out of the 51 endometriosis patients, 40 were aligned correctly to the classification “diseased,” which expresses a rate of 11 out of 51 (21.6%) false negative classifications. A total of 23 out of the 39 healthy control subjects were correctly classified as “healthy” and 16 (41.0%) were false positive. Thus, by means of the DTA a sensitivity of 78.4% (95% CI 69.5%–91.2%), a specificity of 59.0% (95% CI 48.5%–71.2%), and a positive predictive value (PPV) of 71.4% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 67.6% were revealed in the same data set. Subgroup analyses of the data set were performed regarding stage of the disease. Owing to the limited sample size of 51 patients with endometriosis, we grouped minimal and mild endometriosis (rAFS stages I and II) and moderate and severe endometriosis (rAFS stages III and IV) to allow for sufficient sample sizes within the subgroups. For rAFS stages I and II (n = 19) versus control, we found an overall recognition capability of 78.2%, exhibiting a sensitivity of 89.7% (95% CI 75.8%–97.1%) and a specificity of 66.7% (95% CI 43.4%–87.4%). The five selected differentially expressed peaks found between groups were statistically significant (P .05). These proteins were then compared with proteins associated with an elevated risk for endometriosis, such as IL-1, IL-8, IL-10, regulated on activation, normal T expressed and secreted (RANTES), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), SAA, TNF-α, and MCP-1. None of the identified peaks, however, corresponded to any of these proteins. Whether fragments or subunits of proteins were present in the serum was not analyzed in detail. Moreover, to affirm our findings about proteomic techniques for diagnosis of endometriosis, we analyzed the data set of a recently published study with the permission of Liu et al. (14). We re-evaluated the data set of that study by using the GA and DTA that we applied to our data. We found a sensitivity of 55.6% and a specificity of 64.9% using the GA and a sensitivity of 92.7% and a specificity of 62.8% using the DTA. Owing to the very small sample size, no subgroup analyses were performed. Discussion These findings provide direct evidence that prospective screening for protein patterns in serum samples of symptomatic patients using SELDI-TOF MS before laparoscopy might be of discriminative value in the prediction of disease. However, according to the present data set, low sensitivity and specificity, as well as low PPVs and NPVs, disqualifed the screening for serum protein patterns by SELDI-TOF MS as a “quick fix” diagnostic test. In the present study, a unique selection of protein peaks was identified in the serum samples. However, we were unable to identify a specific protein peak tightly associated with the presence or absence of pelvic endometriosis, such as SAA, which has been identified by SELDI-TOF MS to be a potential biomarker for monitoring the severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (19). Several protein peaks found to be associated with endometriosis were analyzed and compared with proteins reportedly associated with an elevated risk for endometriosis, such as IL-1, IL-8, IL-10, TNF-α (20), RANTES (21), VEGF (22), SAA (23), and MCP-1 (24). However, none of the peaks identified in the present study corresponded to any of these proteins. Biomarkers associated with endometriosis, such as IL-6, cancer antigen (CA) 125, CA 19.9 (25), and CCR-1 (26), are of a molecular weight much higher than 20,000 Da and could therefore not be identified by SELDI-TOF MS. Fragments or subunits of these proteins, however, might still have been present in the spectra. Therefore, to identify these proteins, sequencing would have been indicated, but that was not conducted in the present study. The principal approach of clinical proteomics is different: The presence of one protein which might serve as a biomarker for a certain disease is not the main focus, but rather the serum-based proteome pattern is subjected to an artificial intelligence–based algorithm to find hidden patterns of combinations of markers that might discriminate between healthy and diseased (27). In the present study, we therefore used a GA as well as a rule-based DTA. We demonstrated that this technique might be of discriminative value for the prediction of disease. However, by means of the GA, low sensitivity (81.3%) and specificity (60.3%) preclude a recommendation for clinical application of this screening method. Interestingly, we found significantly differential protein patterns in minimal and mild endometriosis that showed increased sensitivity (89.%) and specificity (66.7%) compared with the analysis of all samples. These findings may indicate that the changes on the proteomic level in patientś serum reflect the systemic subclinical manifestation and high activity of endometriotic lesions which are often found in early stages of disease (28). Moreover, by means of the rule-based DTA a sensitivity of 78.4%, a specificity of 59.0%, a PPV of 71.4%, and a NPV of 67.6% also suggest as promising method, but it is of limited value for clinical application as a diagnostic screening tool. Recently, Liu et al. introduced a model for the detection of endometriosis using plasma protein profiling by SELDI-TOF MS (14). For the purpose of setting up a classification and regression tree as a diagnostic model, they used a training set and then analyzed 30 samples. According to that model, they found a sensitivity of 87.5% and a specificity of 85.7%. Owing to the very small sample size, that preliminary report is of limited value to be introduced as a minimal invasive diagnostic tool in the screening for endometriosis. Moreover, in that study, patients with confirmed endometriosis were compared with healthy control subjects free of complaints, which does not reflect the clinical setting for which a diagnostic test is principally required. However, when we analyzed the data set of Liu et al.'s study using the GA and rule-based DTA that we applied to our data, we found a sensitivity of 55.6% and a specificity of 64.9% using the GA and a sensitivity of 92.7% and a specificity of 62.8% using the DTA. Therefore, when applying the algorithms used in the present study, our data explicitly confirm the limited value of this proteomic technique as a screening method for endometriosis. The diagnosis of endometriosis remains difficult. The heterogeneous clinical appearance, diverse localization of endometriotic implants, and enormous variety and severity of complaints reflect the complexity of the pathogenesis of endometriosis and suggest that the disease probably implicates more than one entity (29). Moreover, among symptomatic patients within a prospective setting, several occult or microscopic endometriosis lesions might lead to the misdiagnosis of “no endometriosis” (30). In addition, some patients might suffer from a very early stage of endometriosis and are about to express these diagnostic features in, e.g., half a year (31). Summing up, the current gold standard diagnoses them as “healthy” or at least “disease-free.” On the molecular level, however, the disease could already have started, leading, e.g., to the patients' symptoms. Add to that, a difference between the “disease-free” patients and truly healthy patients occurs, which in turn influences their protein expression. Using a high-end and very sensitive tool for analyzing protein expression, such as mass spectrometry, these differences on the molecular level can be detected. Keeping this in mind, a broadening of the protein pattern of the “healthy” group occurs, which in turn overlaps with the protein pattern of the “diseased” group. In terms of mathematics, this truly hampers sensitivity and, most of all, specificity of a screening method, including serum pattern profiling. Because endometriosis is still an underdiagnosed disease, and the interval between the onset of symptoms and the final diagnosis/causal therapy of this disease has not decrease in the recent years, there is still no patient screening tool developed. The present study uses the proteomic approach toward this objective and puts first reports suggesting proteomic tools for screening of endometriosis patients into perspective. In his recent review, Poon (17) pleaded that second validation studies—ideally in a prospective setting—are needed to determine the genuine clinical values of diagnostic proteomic patterns identified by SELDI-TOF MS. In conclusion, we can state that these findings provide direct evidence that screening for protein patterns in serum samples of symptomatic patients using SELDI-TOF MS before laparoscopy might be of discriminative value in the prediction of disease. However, because endometriosis is a local disease of the female pelvis with systemic subclinical manifestation, and serum protein expression is highly susceptible to interference, the proteomic analysis of other compounds such as the eutopic endometrium might be of further interest to learn more about endometriosis. Therefore, further studies might focus on changes on the proteomic level in eutopic endometrium as a primary source of ectopic endometrial implants playing a key role in the pathogenesis of endometriosis and might serve as diagnostic target for minimal invasive techniques of diagnosis, i.e., microcurettage using Pipelle.
