# Development of a health equity tool in resuscitation sciences and application to current research in extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation for cardiac arrest.

## Abstract

Background: Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) is increasingly used for adults with cardiac arrest (CA) refractory to Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS). Concerns exist that adding ECPR could worsen health inequities, defined as differences in health outcomes that are unfair or unjust. Current guidelines do not explicitly address this issue. This study narratively reviews the latest evidence on ECPR, focusing on its implications for health equity and derives a health equity tool that may serve as a basis of comparison for resuscitation sciences.

Methods: We searched the American Heart Association (AHA) and International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) websites for the latest ACLS guidelines and scientific summaries on ECPR for CA and identified randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies. We identified population and individual characteristics associated with inequities based on the literature and expert opinion. These characteristics were used as a health equity tool to assess: differences in baseline risk, population exclusion and trial representation in studies, outcome analyses, and implementation barriers. We used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) Evidence to Decision (EtD) framework to evaluate ECPR's impact on health equity.

Results: Four RCTs involving 435 patients were conducted in the (2/4) USA, (1/4) Czech Republic, and (1/4) Netherlands. We identified thirteen characteristics associated with health inequities. All trials took place in urban, high-resourced hospitals and excluded older adults (60-75+ years). Across all RCTs, women were under-represented, and in the two USA-based trials, Black individuals were under-represented. There was no difference in baseline rate of survival with minimal or no neurologic impairment between sexes, but an observed trend favoring younger patients (<65). One trial's subgroup analysis showed no significant differences in ECPR effectiveness by sex or age. We noted that implementing ECPR for out-of-hospital CA faces challenges due to demographic variability, differences in emergency services, access to existing ECPR programs, and limited implementation outside urban areas.

Conclusions: A health equity tool based on axes of health inequities for resuscitation identified that health equity is reduced with the use of ECPR for CA. Mitigation strategies should involve evaluating demographics, health equity measures, outcomes and ensuring equitable access to ECPR across catchment areas before and after implementation.

## Introduction

Introduction

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) uses veno-arterial (VA) extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) to support patients without sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) 1 , 2 . Its complexities require specialized teams, equipment, and multidisciplinary support, limiting availability to well-equipped healthcare systems.

Current guidelines 3 – 5 recommend ECPR for refractory cardiac arrest (CA) in adults based on the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) Consensus on Science With Treatment Recommendations (CoSTR) and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) Evidence to Decision (EtD) framework 6 , 7 . ILCOR found no studies on ECPR’s impact on health equity 8 . However, they noted that resource-limited areas likely cannot afford ECPR, potentially worsening existing health inequities.

Health equity aims to eliminate unfair or unjust health differences 9 . Recently, attention has grown around resuscitation health equity, highlighting several populations experiencing barriers to timely and appropriate care 10 . In the US, marginalized groups reported by race often receive fewer recommended interventions and experience worse health outcomes 11 . In one recent study reviewing over 110,000 witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrests across the United States, non-White individuals received bystander CPR less frequently during CA than White individuals 12 . Structural healthcare inequities exacerbate these disparities by limiting access to timely care for marginalized communities 13 . Additional factors such as disparities in CPR training, language barriers, and historical distrust of the healthcare system, contribute to delays and suboptimal care for marginalized groups 12 , 14 .

Guideline recommendations are important in advancing health equity by explicitly considering the impact of individual decisions made by patients, clinicians, and policymakers 15 . When health equity is overlooked, guidelines may unintentionally create or worsen disparities by promoting harmful practice standards 15 , 16 . Recently, Dewidar et al., 17 has developed a seven-step approach to completing the health equity criterion of the GRADE EtD framework. The objective of this study is to use that approach 17 to develop a health equity tool for resuscitation sciences, and demonstrate its application to the evidence on ECPR use for CA.

## Methods

Methods

The protocol for the study has been published on Open Science Framework (OSF) 18 . The reporting of this review is guided by the PRISMA-Equity and SWiM 19 reporting guidelines ( Supplementary Appendix Table S1 ). No ethics approval was needed for this study.

Identifying evidence informing ECPR recommendations for adults with CA

We searched the AHA website 20 for the latest guideline recommendations on ECPR use in adults after CA. We reviewed the supporting texts for evidence including evidence syntheses and primary studies (randomized controlled trials (RCT)s and observational studies). We also searched the ILCOR website ( https://ilcor.org/ ) for the most recent evidence synthesis on adult ACLS and screened the included primary studies for eligibility.

Eligibility criteria

We included all primary studies (RCTs and observational studies) involving adults with CA in any setting (out-of-hospital or in-hospital) who received ECPR, including extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or cardiopulmonary bypass, compared to those who received manual or mechanical CPR, and reported changes in any clinical outcomes.

Data extraction

The lead author collected all the data, and the senior author (LJM) reviewed it. We collected key study characteristics reported for the intervention and control arms, eligibility criteria, participant numbers, funding sources, publication year, trial locations, metrics for defining vulnerable populations (e.g., CDC social vulnerability index 21 ). We also collected survival and bystander CPR rates in the control arm.

We utilized the seven-step approach by Dewidar et al. 17 (described in Supplementary Appendix Table S2 ) to extract health equity-relevant information. The PROGRESS-Plus framework (detailed in Supplementary Appendix S3 ) was used as the starting point to identify factors contributing to health inequities in resuscitation and refined them through review of published literature 13 , 22 – 24 and discussions within the project group (OD, LJM, ALB, MDR, CS).

We gathered information on differences in baseline risk of adverse events/prevalence of outcome of interest, analyses across prespecified populations, and implementation considerations noted by the authors. Additionally, we collected participant demographic characteristics to assess the representation of prespecified subpopulations.

Data synthesis

We narratively summarized the findings according to Dewidar et al.’s framework 17 and reported the results from RCTs and observational studies separately.

We assessed representation of prespecified subpopulations in the included trials using the Participant Prevalence Ratio (PPR) developed by Poon et al. 25 Calculating the PPR illustrates the rate of enrollment of subpopulations in RCTs relative to their proportion in the disease subpopulation. To calculate the numerator, we determined the percentage of each subpopulation as a proportion of the total participants included in each trial. Incidence data from published literature was used to estimate the percentage of subpopulations affected by CA. Given that CA is an acute condition, incidence data was considered appropriate instead of prevalence data. National-level aggregate data incidence data from published literature, including the ROC Epistry 26 dataset, EuReCa TWO 27 project and prospective ARREST registry (Amsterdam Resuscitation Studies) 28 , provided sex-disaggregated data (general population, witnessed status, shockable rhythm and witnessed shockable), while the CARES 29 registry offered race and ethnicity data. We calculated the PPR using general CA population data and adjusted for trial eligibility criteria. We calculated the median of the PPR’s grouped by subpopulations and described their spread using Interquartile Range (IQR).

A subpopulation (e.g., women recruited in trials) would be considered underrepresented or overrepresented when the PPR was 1.2, respectively, relative to their proportion in the disease population (e.g., women with CA in real-world setting that are eligible for ECPR).

## Results

Results

Study identification and characteristics

We identified one systematic review on ECPR for CA 30 from our search of the ILCOR ACLS CoSTR and 2023 AHA ACLS guideline recommendations ( Figure 1 ). The review included 39 studies: 3 published RCTs, 3 ongoing RCTs, 27 observational studies (adult and pediatric), and 6 cost-effectiveness studies. We excluded the cost-effectiveness studies and pediatric studies (4 observational studies). Of the 3 ongoing trials, one has been published. Thus, in our assessment, we included 4 RCTs 31 – 34 and 23 35 – 57 observational studies.

Eligibility criteria across the RCTs varied ( Table 2 ). Upper age limits ranged from 65 31 to 75 years 34 . All RCTs required witnessed CA for eligibility. EROCA 34 required shockable rhythm if not witnessed, while INCEPTION 32 and ARREST trials 33 required both witnessed and shockable rhythm.

Transfer time to the emergency department (ED) was <30 minutes in two RCTs 33 , 34 , and time to the catheterization lab was <60 minutes in the other two 31 , 32 . Ventricular fibrillation (VF) as the initial cardiac rhythm in the control arms ranged from 64% 31 to 100% 33 , 34 . Bystander CPR rates in the control arms were over 80% in all RCTs. Survival to hospital discharge in the control arms ranged from 7% 33 to 30% 34 .

Excluding populations experiencing inequities

All the RCTs implemented varying age cut-offs for excluding older adults. PRAGE OHCA 31 trial had the lowest age cutoff, excluding individuals 65 and older while the ARREST trial 33 excluded 75 years and above. EROCA 34 and ARREST 33 trials excluded prisoners, and ARREST 33 trial excluded nursing home residents.

Representation of populations experiencing inequities

Sex was reported in all the included trials. Only the two US-based trials 33 , 34 reported on race or ethnicity, limiting the evaluation of representation of different subpopulations, the calculation of a PPR, and the generalizability of trial results ( Table 4 ; Supplementary Appendix Table S6 ). Using the general CA population data, women were well-represented in the EROCA trial 34 , with a PPR of 0.91, closely aligning with their incidence in the general population affected by CA. In the Prague OHCA trial 31 , women were under-represented with a PPR of 0.49, less than half of what would be expected based on epidemiological CA incidence data. Similarly, in the ARREST trial 33 , women had low PPRs of 0.47. The INCEPTION 32 trial also showed significant under-representation of women, with a PPR of 0.29, mirroring the disparities found in the other trials. These disparities persisted even after adjusting for trial eligibility criteria ( Table 4 ).

Black individuals were notably under-represented in both the EROCA 34 and ARREST 33 trials with a PPR of 0.26, indicating inadequate representation relative to their demographic proportions in general CA population data. No data was available to calculate PPR while accounting for trial eligibility ( Table 4 ).

Differences in baseline rate of survival in the control group

Only the PRAGUE OHCA trial 31 reported the baseline rate of survival with minimal or no neurologic impairment at 180 days across different age groups and sex. Among individuals aged 65 and older, the baseline rate of survival of this outcome was notably lower compared to those under 65 years (14.3% versus 24.7%, respectively). No difference was observed between men and women, with both groups showing similar baseline rates of survival (21.8% for men and 22.7% for women).

Differences in intervention effects

Only the PRAGUE OHCA trial 31 was able to conduct post-hoc subgroup analyses for survival with minimal or no neurologic impairment at 180 days across age and sex. They reported absolute and relative differences between groups.

No significant differences were found in the relative effects of ECPR between those aged ≥65 and <65 (Odds Ratio (OR), 95% Confidence Intervals (C.I.); 2.40 (0.72–7.95) vs OR, 95% C.I.; 1.47, 0.78–2.79), suggesting similar effectiveness of ECPR across age groups. The p-value for interaction was 0.48, indicating no statistically significant interaction effect by age.

In terms of absolute differences, individuals aged ≥65 (Difference %, 95% C.I.; 14.3%, −4.6 to 33.2) had greater benefit from ECPR compared to those <65 (Difference %, 95% C.I.; 7.8%, −5.1 to 20.82). However, the difference was not statistically significant.

Similarly, no significant differences in the relative effects of ECPR were observed between men (OR, 95% C.I.; 1.79, 0.97–3.30) and women (OR, 95% C.I.; 1.00, 0.24–4.10). The p-value for interaction was 0.46, indicating no statistically significant interaction effect by sex. Absolute differences suggested a trend where men (Difference %, 95% C.I.; 11.5%, −0.5 to 23.5) may benefit more from ECPR compared to women (Difference %, 95% C.I.; 0.0%, −24.8 to 24.8), although these findings did not reach statistical significance.

Equity barriers to implementation

Trials conducted at single sites, like the EROCA trial 34 , may not reflect the varied population densities, median ages, and traffic patterns typical of other settings where ECPR is used for OHCA. Outside large metropolitan areas, obtaining comprehensive experience with extracorporeal CPR is hindered by logistical difficulties, as evidenced by incomplete screening logs in some participating centers in two trials 32 , 33 . Variations in emergency response infrastructure, including the availability of centralized care and access to highly trained teams capable of rapid and expert response CA create barriers to the equitable implementation of ECPR 31 .

Health equity-relevant evidence in observational studies on ECPR outcomes for CA

The characteristics of the observational studies are described in Supplementary Appendix Table S7 . All the studies described the age and sex of the study participants, two 40 , 51 described ethnicity or race and one study indicated whether the participants lived in rural or urban settings 57 .

The median age for patients receiving ECPR was 57 years (IQR, 54–61; n = 18), while for those receiving CCPR, it was 65 years (IQR, 59–71). Men were 3.53 times more likely to receive ECPR (IQR, 2.48–5.07; n = 19) and 2.86 times more likely to receive CCPR (IQR, 1.71–3.50) than women. Observational studies on the impact of age on ECPR survival have yielded mixed results. Some studies 38 , 58 indicated a negative association between increasing age and survival rates, while others 39 , 48 , 59 found no significant age-based difference between ECPR and CCPR groups. Three studies 38 , 48 , 59 suggested that women may benefit more from ECPR than CCPR, with better survival rates for women who received ECPR.

## Discussion

Discussion

We have identified a health equity tool for resuscitation based on axes of health inequities. Using this tool on the current evidence base, we have highlighted critical inequities in the recruitment for trials evaluating the efficacy of ECPR for CA. Women were under-represented across all the trials and Black individuals were notably under-represented in the two USA-based trials. All trials occurred in high-resource urban settings, limiting broader applicability. Despite no evidence for sex differences in outcomes, men are three times more likely to receive ECPR. These findings suggest that the current recommendation implementation practices of ECPR for CA reduces health equity.

Other than sex, race and ethnicity, we have very little information on how other characteristics in table 1 contribute to health inequities. For instance, health equity-relevant information regarding the impact of geographical vicinity to the ECPR center, proxied by time to cannulation - a key prognostic factor for ECPR effectiveness 60 – was absent. In practice, the time to ECPR significantly varies and its optimal threshold remains uncertain 61 . The time-sensitive nature of ECPR creates health equity concerns as individuals with intersecting marginalized characteristics (such as lower socioeconomic status, remote employment, limited social or political influence) are less likely to access these facilities. Thus, targeted strategies are needed to ensure equitable access.

Trial enrollment may be affected by underlying health equity issues affecting the chain of survival. The low PPR observed for women that persisted despite adjustments for eligibility criteria suggests that broader societal and systemic factors may drive these disparities rather than epidemiological factors. Trial data also suggests that sex is not associated with ECPR effectiveness, further supporting this interpretation and broader cardiovascular literature where women are repeatedly under-recognized, understudied, underdiagnosed and undertreated 62 . In the context of ECPR, our health equity tool will help address this issue and improve the generation and subsequent implementation of equitable evidence-based care 62 .

Although trial design and execution strategies are the responsibility of trialists and current recommendations have been published to support equitable trial design, to wider societal and policy developments are needed to make them effective 63 . Thus, sex and gender disparities should be addressed through public education on bystander CPR, addressing disparities in emergency response systems and refining healthcare delivery to address systemic barriers to timely interventions for women. Similar issues are most likely affecting all the characteristics identified in the health equity tool. To improve representation in ECPR trials, it is important to address local barriers to implementation for each target population, including optimizing the survival rate across sex, ethnicity and neighborhood such that all have an equitable chance for survival before implementing ECPR as a trial arm. This highlights the need for a dual approach: addressing societal inequities that limit equitable access to care while refining trial designs and reporting to improve and demonstrate representativeness.

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings confirm the known inequities in access to resources and optimized care, leading to disparities in resuscitation outcomes across published ECPR trials 64 – 66 . ILCOR suggested that resource-poor areas may lack centers capable of providing ECPR. Aside from sex, age and ethnicity (in US trials), health equity analyses were rarely reported in RCTs, aligning with findings from other reviews 67 . While observational studies more often explore social determinants of health 68 , this was not consistently reported in ECPR research 69 , 70 . This suggests a broader issue within ECPR and resuscitation science regarding the collection and reporting of these critical characteristics.

Implications for research

We propose Table 1 as a health equity tool for resuscitation sciences including characteristics both relevant to both the individual and the community. Although we developed this health equity tool for the purpose of reporting resuscitation science and developing guidelines, we encourage authors of primary studies to present disaggregated data (e.g., participant characteristics) based on Table 1 and conduct carefully planned and well-formulated hypothesis-driven subgroup analyses 71 , 72 where possible. We acknowledge the challenges associated with collecting many of these variables. Karran et al. 73 reviewed data collection approaches for axes of health inequities within the PROGRESS-Plus framework, however, tools are needed to capture these social determinants, especially in the context of routinely collected data 74 . Nonetheless, adherence of primary study authors to consensus health equity reporting standards would facilitate analyses like those presented in this review 75 , 76 .

Enhancing the design of resuscitation RCTs to prioritize health equity from the outset is crucial for addressing health inequities. Current studies of ECPR lack representation of populations experiencing inequities as they less often manifest the typical clinical profile of those who may benefit from ECPR. The INCLUDE framework from Trial Forge encourages trialists to consider key questions about participant diversity, such as which groups will benefit from the trial and how processes can be adapted to meet their needs 77 . Further research is needed to explore how to improve participant diversity in the context of ECPR 78 , such as using physiological surrogates for eligibility other than witnessed CA, optimizing bystander CPR and emergency service outcomes before ECPR implementation, and adopting flexible protocols like mobile units or hubs and spoke implementation strategies based on proximity to ECPR facilities.

Implications for guideline developers

Many guideline developers primarily use systematic reviews to evaluate benefits and harms but may overlook collected information on other elements, such as social determinants of health. Conducting a separate, health equity-focused review may be impractical due to time constraints and resource demands. We recommend the seven step approach applied in this review (described elsewhere 17 and supplementary appendix S2 ) to better develop health equity considerations and minimize reliance on opinion-based judgments. Table 1 serves as health equity tool for the design and development of resuscitation science clinical trials and guidelines.

Although observational studies are susceptible to biases in assessing intervention effects, they offer insights into real-world ECPR use 79 . Men are three times more likely to receive ECPR than women, likely due to implicit biases and the misconception that women are less prone to CA 80 , 81 , leading to delays in detection and resuscitation 82 . Women are also less likely to receive bystander CPR 83 , 84 , longer transit times and delays to ECPR facilities, and higher comorbidity rates like chronic kidney disease 85 and undiagnosed diabetes 86 , which can confer an adverse prognosis and make them ineligible for ECPR. The lack of standardized ECPR protocols allows gender biases to influence clinical decision-making.

Limitations

The limitation of our approach includes its reliance solely on the health equity-related information reported in the published manuscripts identified during the reviews. As a result, we were unable to conduct analyses for most of the axes of inequities listed in table 1 . For the calculation of the PPR, we used available sex disaggregated data for general CA population, as well as those with shockable rhythm and witnessed CA. However, we recognize that the estimates maybe inaccurate given the variation in eligibility criteria among trials with three of them requiring both for eligibility.

## Conclusion

Conclusion

A health equity tool based on axes of health inequities for resuscitation was derived and used to identify that health equity is reduced with the use of ECPR for CA. Based on the health equity tool, mitigation may include evaluating demographics, health equity measures and outcomes, and ensuring equitable access to ECPR in catchment areas before and after implementing ECPR.
