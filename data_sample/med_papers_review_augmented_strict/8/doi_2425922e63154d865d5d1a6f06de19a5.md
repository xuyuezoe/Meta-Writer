# Inclusion of renal vascular lesions in the 2003 ISN/RPS system for classifying lupus nephritis improves renal outcome predictions.

## Abstract

The 2003 International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) pathological classification system of lupus nephritis specified the importance of vascular damage and indicated this should be included in the diagnostic summary. Few pathological studies of lupus nephritis, however, focus on the patterns of renal vascular involvement. Here we assessed renal vascular lesions in lupus nephritis based on the 2003 ISN/RPS classification system and evaluated their association with clinical and pathological data in a large cohort from a single center in China. Among 341 patients with lupus nephritis, 279 were diagnosed with single or multiple renal vascular lesions that included 253 with vascular immune complex deposits, 82 with atherosclerosis, 60 with thrombotic microangiopathy, 13 with noninflammatory necrotizing vasculopathy, and 2 with true renal vasculitis. Patients with thrombotic microangiopathy had the poorest renal outcome. In multivariate Cox hazard analysis after inclusion of renal vascular lesions, the new chronicity index score became a significantly better independent risk factor for renal outcome (hazard ratio 2.32). Thus, renal vascular lesions are common in lupus nephritis and closely correlate with clinical disease activity and renal outcome. Inclusion of a detailed description of renal vascular lesions in the ISN/RPS classification of lupus nephritis may strengthen its predictive value for renal outcome.

Keywords lupus nephritis renal vascular lesions SLE The kidney is one of the most commonly involved organs in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Renal histopathological lesions were closely associated with different clinical characteristics, therapeutic responses, and outcomes in patients with lupus nephritis.1,2 In addition to glomerulonephritis, renal vascular lesions should also be investigated in detail because their presence can adversely affect the prognosis of the renal disease.3,4 However, the World Health Organization classification5-7 and National Institutes of Health (NIH) system of activity indices (AIs) and chronicity indices (CIs)8 were exclusively based on glomerular lesions. The 2003 International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) in its pathological classification9 of lupus nephritis specified the importance of vascular damage and indicated that it should be included in the summary diagnostic line: ‘Indicate and grade (mild, moderate, severe) tubular atrophy, interstitial inflammation and fibrosis, severity of arteriosclerosis or other vascular lesions.’ However, few pathological studies on lupus nephritis have focused on the patterns of renal vascular involvement since then. In this study, we evaluated various renal vascular lesions in patients with lupus nephritis on the basis of the 2003 ISN/RPS classification system and further assessed their associations with clinical and pathological data, using a large cohort of patients with lupus nephritis. Open table in a new tab According to the 2003 ISN/RPS classification system for lupus nephritis, 20 patients were classified as class II (5.9%), 70 as class III (20.5%, including 29 as class III+V), 188 as class IV (55.1%, 24 as class IV-segmental (IV-S) and 164 as class IV-global (IV-G), including 35 as class IV+V), 60 as class V (17.6%), and 3 as class VI (0.9%). No patient was classified under class I in this study. All of the patients received oral prednisone therapy. The majority of patients completed treatment with oral cyclophosphamide (68/341) or monthly intravenous cyclophosphamide (600–800mg/month) (176/341). The other patients received mycophenolate mofetil (23/341), leflunomide (39/341), and azathioprine (15/341). Twenty patients received prednisone alone. Most of the patients achieved clinical remission, 199 with complete remission and 102 with partial remission. Forty patients presented with treatment failure. The average follow-up time was 56.4 months (range 6–360 months). During the follow-up period, four patients died because of severe infection (3/341) or heart failure (1/341). With regard to long-term renal outcome, 40 patients reached the secondary end point (end-stage renal disease or doubling of serum creatinine): two with doubling of serum creatinine and 38 with end-stage renal disease. The distribution of renal vascular lesions in lupus nephritis Among the 341 patients with lupus nephritis, 279 patients (81.8%) with renal vascular lesions were identified and classified as follows: 253 with vascular immune complex deposits (74.2%), 82 with atherosclerosis (24.0%), 60 with thrombotic microangiopathy (17.6%), 13 with noninflammatory necrotizing vasculopathy (3.8%), and only two with true renal vasculitis (0.6%). A total of 105 patients (37.6%) presented with more than two types of vascular lesions: 82 with two types of vascular lesions, including 37 with immune complex deposits and thrombotic microangiopathy, 41 with immune complex deposits and atherosclerosis, 2 with immune complex deposits and noninflammatory necrotizing vasculopathy, 1 with immune complex deposits and true renal vasculitis, and 1 with thrombotic microangiopathy and atherosclerosis; 20 with three types of vascular lesions, including 12 with immune complex deposits, thrombotic microangiopathy, and atherosclerosis, 5 with immune complex deposits, noninflammatory necrotizing vasculopathy, and thrombotic microangiopathy, 3 with immune complex deposits, noninflammatory necrotizing vasculopathy, and atherosclerosis; and 3 with four types of vascular lesions, including 2 with immune complex deposits, noninflammatory necrotizing vasculopathy, thrombotic microangiopathy, and atherosclerosis and 1 with immune complex deposits, noninflammatory necrotizing vasculopathy, thrombotic microangiopathy, and true renal vasculitis. Among the 253 patients with vascular immune complex deposits, C1q was the most commonly seen one (221/253, 87.4%), followed by C4d (149/253, 58.9%), IgG (112/253, 44.3%), C3c (108/253, 42.7%), IgM (99/253, 39.1%), and IgA (36/253, 14.2%). Among the 60 patients with thrombotic microangiopathy, 35 had microthrombi in the glomeruli, 17 had microthrombi in small arterioles and/or arteries, and 35 had mucoid changes and/or onion-skin lesions in arterioles and/or arteries. The prevalence of thrombotic microangiopathy was significantly higher in patients with C4d deposition on arteries and/or arterioles compared with those without C4d deposition (37/112 (24.8%) vs. 23/169 (11.9%); P=0.002). Clinical evaluation The criteria for system involvement were consistent with the 1997 American College of Rheumatology revised criteria for SLE.34 The response to therapy included complete remission, partial remission, and treatment failure detailed in previous works.37-40 A relapse was defined as nephritic relapse and proteinuric relapse.41 The patients were followed up in outpatient lupus clinics. The primary end point was defined as death, and the secondary end point was defined as end-stage renal disease or doubling of serum creatinine. Informed consent was obtained for renal biopsy from each patient. The research was in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The design of this work was approved by the local ethical committees. Laboratory assessment Serum antinuclear antibodies were detected using the indirect immunofluorescence assay (EUROIMMUN, Lübeck, Germany), and anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies were detected using the Crithidia luciliae indirect immunofluorescence test (EUROIMMUN). Anti-extractable nuclear antigen antibodies, including anti-Sm, anti-Sjögren’s syndrome A antigen, anti-Sjögren’s syndrome B antigen, and anti-ribonucleoprotein antibodies, were detected using the immunodotting assay (EUROIMMUN). Anti-cardiolipin antibodies were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (EUROIMMUN). Serum C3 was determined using the rate nephelometry assay (IMMAGE; Beckman-Coulter, IMMAGE, Fullerton, CA; normal range >0.85g/l). Statistical analysis Statistical software SPSS 13.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL) was used for statistical analysis. Quantitative data were expressed as mean±s.d., and as median with range (minimum and maximum). Differences in semiquantitative data were tested with the Kruskal–Wallis test and the Mann–Whitney U-test. Bonferroni’s correction was used for multiple comparisons. The Spearman’s rank-order correlation test was performed between various lesions. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to analyze patients’ prognosis. Univariate survival analysis was carried out using the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis of patient survival was performed using the Cox regression model. Variables that did not affect survival significantly were removed by a stepwise procedure according to a likelihood ratio. Results were expressed as hazard ratio with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was considered at P<0.05.
