# Effects of traditional Chinese exercises on brain-derived neurotrophic factor in middle-aged and older adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.

## Abstract

Background: Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) may help middle-aged and older adults resist age-related neurodegenerative conditions and psychiatric disorders. Recent studies suggested that Traditional Chinese exercises (TCEs) may be a promising strategy to improve the BDNF levels of these populations, while the effectiveness has yet to be definitively confirmed due to the variances in the study designs and observations. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to examine the effects of TCEs intervention on BDNF in middle-aged and older adults.

Methods: The search was conducted in November 2024 in seven Chinese and English databases. Two reviewers independently reviewed the search results, extracted the data, and assessed the risk of bias and certainty of evidence. Meta-analyses and meta-regressions were performed to determine the overall effect size and the impact of potential moderators.

Results: Ten publications consisting of 543 participants were included. The overall effect size of TCEs on BDNF was large and significant [Hedges'g = 0.82, 95 % CI (0.55, 1.09), p < 0.01]. Subgroup analysis revealed that the effect size was non-significant for participants with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (p = 0.08), while significant for participants with normal cognitive function (p < 0.01). In the meta-regression, moderators such as the mean age, sex, and baseline BDNF levels of participants, as well as total TCEs time were not associated with outcome variables. The certainty of the evidence was assessed as moderate.

Conclusions: This systematic review and meta-analysis indicates that TCEs intervention could increase the levels of BDNF in middle-aged and older adults with normal cognitive function.

Systematic review registration: www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/, identifier: CRD42023484121.

## Introduction

Introduction

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is considered a pivotal protein, ubiquitously located within the hippocampus, the cerebral cortex, and the peripheral nervous system. 1 , 2 , 3 BDNF plays an essential role in promoting neuronal survival and growth via multifaceted mechanisms including the protection of neuronal cells and DNA from oxidative stress-related damage, the regulation of neurogenesis, the promotion of axonal and dendritic proliferation, and the modulation of synaptic plasticity. 4 , 5 , 6 For example, BDNF promotes long-term potentiation in glutamatergic neurons of the hippocampus, facilitating the storage of long-term memories. 7 Simultaneously, it facilitates dendritic spine growth and reorganization in response to dynamic neuronal activity, thereby helping enhance the synaptic connectivity. 8 , 9

Studies have shown that the concentration and expression of BDNF decline significantly in middle-aged and older adults. 10 , 11 , 12 This decrease in BDNF is highly linked to age-related neurodegenerative conditions and cognitive impairments, as well as a heightened risk of psychiatric disorders such as depression. 13 , 14 The decline of BDNF levels contributes to the pathogenesis of these conditions by rendering neurons more vulnerable to insults, such as oxidative stress and neurotoxicity, particularly from amyloid-beta (Aβ) accumulation, which is prevalent in neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Alzheimer's Disease). 15 , 16 , 17 Therefore, numerous efforts have been put in to develop effective strategies to increase the BDNF levels of middle-aged and older adults, such as intracranial BDNF infusion or BDNF mimetics intake. 18 , 19 However, these strategies may be associated with potential adverse effects (e.g., seizures and pain). More appropriate safe strategies are thus needed to restore/increase BDNF in the middle-aged and older population. 20

Recent literature increasingly corroborates that exercise is an efficacious and relatively safe non-pharmacological strategy to increase BDNF levels. 21 , 22 Exercise enhances BDNF levels primarily through several central and peripheral pathways, including the release of myokines (e.g., irisin) from skeletal muscle, increases in cerebral blood flow, and improvements in metabolic environment. 23 , 24 , 25 Among different types of exercise, Traditional Chinese exercises (TCEs) are one unique exercise form that is considered suitable and beneficial for middle-aged and older adults due to their low physical load and diverse elements (i.e., aerobic, postural balance control, social, and meditation elements). 26 TCEs are a kind of multimodal mind-body aerobic exercises derived from traditional Chinese medicine, and the specific types include Tai Chi, Baduanjin Qigong, Wuqinxi Qigong, Liuzijue Qigong, and Yijinjing Qigong. 27 , 28 TCEs primarily consist of continuous, rhythmic movement integrating with breath control and meditation that can improve both physical and mental well-being. 29 Uniquely, the meditation elements in TCEs may foster an environment conducive to BDNF expression by enhancing vagal nerve activation, inhibiting the stress response of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and reducing levels of pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α and IL-6). 30 , 31 , 32 Recent studies have suggested that TCEs may be one effective strategy to induce significant improvement in BDNF levels in middle-aged and older adults. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 However, variance is presented in the study observation and study design (e.g., different types of TCEs intervention, study protocol, and participant characteristics). For example, one study showed that 10 weeks of TCEs (i.e., Tai Chi) could significantly improve BDNF levels in older adults, 35 but another study reported no significant effects as induced by 12 weeks of Baduanjin Qigong on BDNF compared to the control group. 39

Although several systematic reviews have examined the effects of exercise interventions on BDNF levels and included studies involving TCEs, they either perform subgroup analyses of TCEs within broader categories of exercise or overlook the unique characteristics of TCEs by aggregating them with other exercise modalities (e.g., aerobic exercise or physical activity). 40 , 41 To date, no comprehensive review has yet specifically examined the effects of TCEs on BDNF levels, nor have potential moderating factors (e.g., characteristics of intervention and participants) been investigated within the context of TCEs. Therefore, to highlight the recent study findings and to explicitly and comprehensively examine the effects of TCEs on BDNF in middle-aged and older adults, and the potential contributors to such effects, we here completed a systematic review and meta-analysis based upon the up-to-date peer-reviewed publications. Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included in this work, and several subgroup analyses (e.g., intervention types) were performed. This work will ultimately provide critical knowledge to inform the appropriate intervention design in future research and rehabilitative practice for the maintenance of BDNF in mid-aged and older adult populations.

## Material and methods

Material and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines 42 and registered with PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD42023484121), an international prospective registry for systematic reviews.

Data sources and search strategies

Two authors (JG and JH) independently searched PubMed, Web of Science, MEDLINE, SPORT-Discus, PsycINFO, Cochrane Library, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) from inception to November 20, 2024, using a comprehensive search strategy ( Table S1 ). A secondary search strategy was also used which involved a manual search in the reference lists of eligible studies. Searches were limited to publications in English and Chinese.

Selection criteria

All included studies must be published articles. The inclusion criteria were carried out according to the PICOS principle: (1) Population: all participants included in this study were at least of mean age ≥45 years; (2) Interventions: the interventions used were TCEs only or TCEs combined with other interventions; (3) Comparisons: groups were characterized by active (e.g., cognitive training) or inactive comparators (e.g., health education, non-intervention, placebo, or usual care (i.e., routine treatment for participants’ diseases)); (4) Outcome: the study outcome was BDNF; (5) Study design: studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Articles were excluded if: (1) no specific data for study outcomes were provided; (2) review papers and conference abstracts and articles; (3) repeated publications.

Data extraction

The process of data extraction was conducted independently by two authors (JG and JH) according to the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook. 43 The extracted information of the publications included: study (authors, year), participants (age, sex, physical condition), grouping and sample size, interventions (type, frequency, number of sessions, duration of each session, duration of intervention), intervention environment (e.g., group-coached and/or home practices), and outcome measures. Any outcome measures on which the two authors disagreed were discussed with the other two authors (KZ and DB) until a consensus was achieved.

For each study, the mean and the standard deviation (SD) of pre-post change in outcomes were extracted. For studies that did not report the pre-post change in outcomes or the outcomes were presented as “Mean ± SE/SEM (standard error/standard error of mean)”, calculations were conducted using the following formulas 44 : M e a n ( c h a n g e ) = M e a n ( p o s t ) − M e a n ( p r e ) S D ( c h a n g e ) = S D ( p r e ) 2 + S D ( p o s t ) 2 − 2 S D ( p r e ) 2 × S D ( p o s t ) 2 × R S D ( p r e ) = S E ( p r e ) × n ; S D ( p o s t ) = S E ( p o s t ) × n R = 0.4 / 0.5

If the full-text article data were presented only in a figure format, WebPlotDigitizer (Ankit Rohatgi, 2019, V.4.2; WebPlotDigitizer, Pacifica, CA, USA) was used to extract the data from the figures. If any relevant data were missing, the corresponding author or authors were contacted by the first researcher via email.

Quality assessment

The quality of included studies was assessed independently by two authors (JG and JH) based on the principles of the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro). The PEDro scale was specifically designed to assess the methodological quality of randomized controlled trials in physical interventions, making it highly suitable for evaluating studies in this work. 45 , 46 This scale evaluates key factors such as randomization, blinding, and allocation concealment, which are crucial for ensuring the internal validity of the included studies in this systematic review and meta-analysis. 46 Specifically, the PEDro scale consists of 11 items, where we were required to fill out “no” or “yes”. For each “no” or “yes” response, we assigned a value of 0 or 1, respectively. The total score for each study ranged from 0 to 11. As blinding (especially participants and investigators) was not easily implemented in exercise intervention trials, 45 , 47 , 48 , 49 the methodological quality classification of each article was adjusted with eligibility criteria considered as previously described [sum scores: ≥6 (“high quality, low risk of bias”); scores: 4–5 (“acceptable quality, moderate risk of bias”), and scores: ≤3 (“low quality, high risk of bias”)]. 47

The quality of the evidence was also assessed independently by two authors (JG and JH) based on the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria, 50 which characterizes the evidence on the study limitations, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. 51 , 52 Any score on which the two authors disagreed was discussed with a third author (KZ or DB) until a consensus was achieved.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed in Stata/MP 17.0 (STATA Corp., College Station, TX) and R 4.2.0 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Since the units of BDNF measured across studies were different (e.g., ng/mL or pg/mL for BDNF levels 34 , 39 ), a random-effects model was used to calculate Hedges'g and 95 % confidence interval (CI) as the measure of effect size for the difference in pre-post change between the intervention and control conditions. 53 Effect sizes were classified as trivial (Hedges'g 50 %), subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the impact of study characteristics on the result (e.g., intervention type or participant age). Sensitivity analyses were subsequently performed by sequentially excluding each study (i.e., “leave-one-out” method) to assess the stability of the pooled estimates and to determine whether any study influenced the overall effect size. 55 In addition, publication bias was assessed by generating funnel plots and conducting Egger's test. If a significant asymmetry was detected, we used Trim and Fill method to adjust for publication bias. 57

To examine characteristics that may influence the effects of TCEs intervention, subgroup analyses were performed to compare the effects on the observations between different protocol characteristics of the TCEs intervention (e.g., the different types of intervention or control). Additionally, we performed meta-regression to explore the dose-response relationship of TCEs on BDNF levels, and the total TCEs time (i.e., session number ∗ session duration) was used as an effect moderator. Secondary meta-regressions were performed to examine the impact of potential moderators on overall effects, including participants’ mean age, sex (i.e., sex ratio (men/women)), and baseline levels of BDNF. The slope coefficient and p-value for each covariate were determined about the desired outcome. The p-value indicates the significance of any potential association, while the slope coefficient evaluates the strength of the association, showing how much the outcome varies with each unit change in the covariate. All the statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

## Discussion

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to summarize the study findings on the effects of TCEs on the BDNF in middle-aged and older people. Ten studies were included and the overall score was assessed as “high quality”, indicating no risk of bias. The results suggested that TCEs are a promising strategy to increase the levels of BDNF with moderate quality of evidence. Meanwhile, our analysis did not reveal a dose-dependent relationship, indicating that the benefits of TCEs on BDNF may not necessarily correlate with longer durations or higher frequencies of the intervention. This has practical implications for designing accessible intervention programs that do not impose excessive burdens on participants.

As mind-body exercises, TCEs might improve the BDNF levels of middle-aged and older adults through both physical-related and mental-related mechanisms. The physical elements (i.e., aerobics and postural balance control) in TCEs might stimulate the release of myokines (e.g., irisin), thereby activating neuronal αV/β5 integrins and promoting BDNF synthesis. 61 , 62 Furthermore, TCEs might elevate calcium levels in neurons, activating calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), which enhances BDNF transcription. 63 , 64 , 65 Increased blood flow and metabolic changes during exercise might also create a favorable environment for BDNF signaling, supporting neuronal survival and hippocampal neurogenesis. 38 , 66 On the other hand, the mental component (i.e., meditation) in TCEs might activate frontal cortex pathways pertaining to attention and cognitive processing, directly promoting neuroplasticity and upregulating BDNF. 30 , 31 It might also enhance vagal activation, facilitating BDNF release via parasympathetic pathways and stimulating the BDNF receptor (i.e., Tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB)). 32 , 67 Meanwhile, meditation might attenuate the stress-induced activation of the HPA axis, reducing glucocorticoid secretion that inhibits BDNF synthesis. 68 Previous studies have found that exercises with intensity similar to TCEs (i.e., low-to-moderate intensity) did not significantly improve BDNF levels. 69 , 70 , 71 This may suggest that the meditation component of TCEs plays a critical role in enhancing BDNF levels. However, studies are needed to directly examine and compare the contributions of meditation and physical components to such benefits of BDNF, which will provide critical knowledge to the appropriate protocol to maximize the benefits of interventions using TCEs for BDNF.

It is observed in the subgroup analysis that all three types of TCEs (i.e., Tai Chi, Yijinjing Qigong, and Baduanjin Qigong) can induce significant benefits for BDNF of middle-aged and older adults, while the effect was more pronounced in older adults than middle-aged participants. The difference may be attributed to age-related variations in neuroplasticity. 10 , 11 , 12 Older adults, with lower baseline BDNF levels and more pronounced neurodegenerative changes, exhibit a greater relative increase after TCEs, reflecting a ‘floor effect’. In contrast, middle-aged adults may experience a more limited response due to their already higher baseline BDNF levels (i.e., ‘ceiling effect’). However, meta-regression analysis revealed that there was no significant moderation effect of age or baseline BDNF levels on the overall efficacy of TCEs. This may potentially be attributed to the fact that the middle-aged participants included in the study were all in late midlife (mean age >55), and such a narrow range of age from middle to older adult cohorts may result in less pronounced differences. 72 Further investigation is needed to examine the differential effects of TCEs on BDNF across various age groups.

Additionally, the subgroup analysis showed that TCEs can increase BDNF significantly only in those with intact cognitive function. One possible explanation might be that MCI is oftentimes associated with neuron loss, mitochondrial dysfunction, and increased inflammation and oxidative stress, all severely interfering with the potential effects of TCEs on the regulatory elements pertaining to BDNF. 37 , 73 , 74 Another potential reason is that it might be challenging for people with MCI to correctly perform TCEs, especially the meditation elements in TCEs that require high cognitive demand. 75 , 76 Therefore, it is critical in future studies to provide adequate familiarization and practice following the guidance of TCEs to participants and closely monitor and track the compliance of each intervention session. Meanwhile, the number of studies focusing on the MCI cohort, as well as the sample size of each study, is relatively small, more studies with larger sample sizes are thus needed to examine and confirm the effects of TCEs on BDNF in people suffering from MCI.

It is also observed that TCEs induce a significantly greater increase in BDNF levels compared to active controls, which highlights the advantages of using TCEs for BDNF in middle-aged and older adults. However, the number of included studies in this subgroup analysis is small (n = 3), and the active controls were all non-exercise interventions. 37 , 39 , 59 Therefore, the direct evidence to compare the effects of TCEs on BDNF to other types of exercise is still highly demanded.

Limitations

The results of this work still need to be taken with caution. First, the number of included studies was relatively small (n = 10), and six of them were completed in China, indicating the main focus on the benefits of TCEs for BDNF was limited within one area. More studies consisting of larger sample size of participants with different cultural backgrounds from more countries are thus highly demanded. Additionally, due to the limited number of studies, subgroup analyses to characterize the influences of protocol settings of TCEs (e.g., appropriate number of sessions, the frequency of intervention) on BDNF cannot be completed. Moreover, the existing studies primarily focus on interventions such as Tai Chi, Baduanjin Qigong, and Yijinjing Qigong, and further investigation is needed to verify the effects of other TCEs types. Lastly, the current understanding of how TCEs affect BDNF levels largely stems from studies on animal research or human research of other exercises. Studies with more rigorous design are thus needed to explicitly explore these critical insights of the effects of TCEs on BDNF. Nevertheless, the knowledge obtained from this work will help inform the appropriate design of intervention protocols of TCEs for BDNF in the near future.

## Conclusions

Conclusions

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that TCEs have promise to increase the levels of BDNF in middle-aged and older adults with normal cognitive function. Future RCTs with rigorous designs are needed to help obtain more definitive conclusions on the effects of TCEs on BDNF in middle-aged and older adults.
