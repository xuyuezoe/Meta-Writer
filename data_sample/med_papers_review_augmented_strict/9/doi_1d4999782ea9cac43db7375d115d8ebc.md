# Hair follicle immune privilege and its collapse in alopecia areata.

## Abstract

Anagen stage hair follicles (HFs) exhibit "immune privilege (IP)" from the level of the bulge downwards to the bulb. Both passive and active IP mechanisms protect HFs from physiologically undesired immune responses and limit immune surveillance. IP is relative, not absolute, and is primarily based on absent, or greatly reduced, intra-follicular antigen presentation via MHC class I and II molecules, along with prominent expression of "no danger" signals like CD200 and the creation of an immunoinhibitory signalling milieu generated by the secretory activities of HFs. Perifollicular mast cells, Tregs and other immunocytes may also contribute to HF IP maintenance in healthy human skin. Collapse of anagen hair bulb IP is an essential prerequisite for the development of alopecia areata (AA). In AA, lesional HFs are rapidly infiltrated by NKG2D + T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, while perifollicular mast cells acquire a profoundly pro-inflammatory phenotype and interact with autoreactive CD8+ T cells. Using animal models, significant functional evidence has accumulated that demonstrates the dominance of the immune system in AA pathogenesis. Purified CD8+T-cell and NK cell populations alone, which secrete fγ, suffice to induce the AA phenotype, while CD4+T-cells aggravate it, and Tregs and iNKT cells may provide relative protection against AA development. While IP collapse may be induced by exogenous agents, inherent IP deficiencies might confer increased susceptibility to AA for some individuals. Thus, a key goal for effective AA management is the re-establishment of a functional HF IP, which will also provide superior protection from disease relapse.

Abbreviations AA alopecia areata APC antigen-presenting cell HF hair follicle IP immune privilege MHC major histocompatibility complex NK natural killer 1 INTRODUCTION One of the most striking features of healthy anagen stage hair follicles (HFs) is the relative immune privilege (IP) that their epithelium exhibits from the bulge region, the niche for HF stem cells, downwards to the hair bulb.1 Several passive and active mechanisms of IP are present in HFs, the nature and potency of which vary over time and in response to changes in the local environment. The net effect of IP conferring factors, set against pro-inflammatory IP collapse inducing actions, can determine the development of HF focal inflammation. Functionality and failure of HF IP has become recognized as a chief element in the pathobiology of several inflammatory hair loss disorders, such as primary cicatricial alopecias,2, 3 and alopecia areata.4-6 The current review defines the primary concept of IP and why it may be expressed in HFs. It examines key features of the anagen HF bulb's physiological IP, including HF cell–expressed IP-modulating receptors and ligands, the role of perifollicular immune cells and cell populations elsewhere in the body that can also support HF IP. The review then explores the central role that HF bulb IP collapse plays in alopecia areata (AA). Non-specific inflammatory mechanisms that could lead to IP failure and AA development are explored, in addition to the classic autoimmune cascade currently recognized in AA disease pathogenesis. The review closes with discussion of current and future therapeutic strategies for AA that involve restoring some aspect of HF IP. We argue that restoration of HF IP is an effective approach for the treatment of AA and for preventing AA relapse, which is typically seen after discontinuation of treatment with glucocorticosteroids or JAK inhibitors.7, 8 2 BASIC IMMUNE PRIVILEGE CONCEPTS IP is the phenomenon whereby organs or tissues are protected from challenge by the host immune system. It occurs in corneal tissue,9, 10 testes,11-13 placenta,14 liver,15 gut,16 brain17 and possibly also in mucosal tissue.18, 19 Nobel laureate immunologist Peter Medawar first developed the concept of IP in the late 1940s20 on the basis of transplantation experiments.21, 22 However, the original concepts, that IP only permits the escape of allotransplants from immune elimination and that it is an organ-specific phenomenon, have become outdated.23 Today, IP is understood as a dynamic, functional characteristic of anatomically defined tissue compartments within selected organs. It represents a relative state, rather than an absolute state and may actually be much more widespread than previously realized. Under certain inflammatory or therapeutic circumstances, a tissue that normally does not exhibit relative IP can develop it or can be manipulated to develop it.1, 24-27 Consequently, IP can be acquired locally in many different tissues, either constitutively or in response to inflammation (see Ref. Compelling functional evidence for the existence of HF IP has not only been provided long ago by Billingham21, 22 but also in mutant mice overexpressing non-HF antigens (e.g. 39) as well as by the observation that AA does not occur without HF IP collapse.4, 6, 33, 43, 44 3 THE BIOLOGICAL FUNCTION OF HAIR FOLLICLE IMMUNE PRIVILEGE Originally, we introduced the concept that the main biological function of HF IP is to sequester highly immunogenic autoantigens from immune recognition, such as melanogenesis-related self-peptides and other autoantigens generated during anagen.43 Accordingly, IP may protect the hair bulb from autoaggressive inflammation during its immunologically vulnerable phase of pigmentary activity and hair shaft production.1, 43 Melanocyte- and melanogenesis-associated epitopes can operate as autoantigens for the T cell–mediated transfer of AA lesions to human scalp skin transplanted onto immunocompromised mice45; successful immunotherapy against melanoma antigens in mice induces AA-like alopecic lesions46; the melanocytes of the human HF pigmentary unit actively downregulate MHC class I expression (Paus & Sinclair, unpublished data); and AA can be associated with vitiligo.4, 47, 48 Recently, autoreactive T cells were identified in mice and humans with AA with specific responses to melanogenesis-related antigens, and also to keratinocyte derived antigen epitopes, including trichohyalin.49, 50 Also of note, copy number variant analysis indicates duplications in melanin-concentrating hormone receptor 2 (MCHR2) that are present in some patients with AA.51 All this supports the concept that one of the key functions of IP is to shield intra-follicularly expressed (auto-)antigens generated during anagen from immune recognition, and to induce a state of peripheral tolerance against them whenever they may become exposed to the immune system.1, 52 Beyond immediate HF protection from immune attack, HF IP might even promote peripheral self-tolerance.52 For example, mouse skin with all HFs in mid-late anagen exhibits a profoundly immunoinhibitory milieu,53, 54 most likely due to the secretion of immunoinhibitory IP guardians by HFs. Further, the number, phenotype, apoptosis-modulating activities and protein expression patterns of perifollicular macrophages significantly fluctuate during the human anagen-catagen switch,55 similar to observations in mice. 56 While it is as yet unclear what exactly this means for HF IP, it suggests that HF cycling can alter the local immune status of skin. We can only speculate on the selection advantage that the development of IP in HFs may have provided during evolution. In mammalian skin, relative IP has so far been identified in the HF and also in the proximal nail matrix of humans57 and mice (Meyer/Paus, unpublished observation). The immunobiology of the feather follicle is largely unknown,58 but conspicuously, chickens can develop spontaneous autoimmune feather loss,59 which renders it likely that feather follicles also have some form of relative IP. Claws, teeth, horns and other skin appendages are vital for the survival of species. Auto-inflammation of appendages could occur after trauma or infection, for example in predators,57 and any significant loss would likely lead to death for the affected animal. This suggests that the principle of protecting skin appendages from inflammation and autoimmune destruction had already been established long before the appearance of HFs during vertebrate evolution. Hair coat maintenance is a crucial survival factor for many mammalian species. Besides key roles for hair shafts in seasonal and climate adaptation, physical protection and camouflage, they are also of elementary importance in inter-sex and social hierarchy communication, as well as in procreation.60 Consequently, it is plausible that the appearance of pigmented hair shafts necessitated the creation of a special immunological milieu to protect these crucial skin appendages from the risk of autoimmune elimination entailed by the constitutive, cyclic production of potentially immunostimulatory autoantigens.1 Maintenance of a functional fur coat is certainly critical for the survival of most mammalian species in the wild, and those that developed effective HF IP may well have enjoyed a considerable selection advantage during evolution. 4 DIFFERENCES IN HF IP BETWEEN RODENTS AND HUMANS Throughout the current review, evidence in support of IP and its collapse in AA is sourced from both human and rodent studies. Unfortunately, there are limited comparative data on HF IP between the two species. The central mechanisms of immune tolerance control that affect skin and HFs are likely comparable between rodents and humans. However, peripheral regulatory mechanisms could differ due to species variation in the homeostatic distribution of immune cells,61 and as a consequence of hair density affecting the nature and number of immune cells in the skin.62 This may affect the ability to respond to, and the nature of the immune response to HF-derived (auto)antigens. One might also anticipate differences in HF IP linked to HF cycling.63 Major hair cycle-dependent changes in the number and location of various immunocyte populations can be seen in murine skin during synchronized cycling.31, 56, 64-66 With age-associated changes in anagen wave distribution,67 and consequent fluctuation in the anagen:telogen ratio, rodent HF IP status may vary significantly over time; at least in terms of intensity. The initial synchronized growth phase may produce a relatively higher level of anagen HF-associated (auto)antigen release in rodents. This may be a desirable event given that exposure to foreign antigens when the rodent (and chicken) immune system is immature typically leads to immune tolerance promotion.68-70 In humans, the anagen:telogen ratio also changes with age, though the fluctuations are less extreme as compared to rodents given the mosaic pattern of hair cycling.71, 72 The first hair growth phase in humans typically occurs in utero comprised of lanugo hair,73 though in principle, this may also have an impact on the developing immune system and tolerance promotion.74 Unfortunately, very little is known with regards to differences between rodents and humans in the molecular regulation of IP at the level of individual HFs. The two currently recognized inducers of HF IP collapse, IFNγ and Substance P, are operative in both mice32, 75 and humans.76, 77 However, the key-secreted IP guardians in human HF IP, αMSH, TGFβ1/2, IL-10 and IGF-176 failed to protect murine anagen HFs from IFNγ-induced IP collapse in vivo.32 This suggests that there may be important differences in the controls of human versus rodent HF IP that await systematic dissection. In the absence of comprehensive comparative data, we present mouse and human data as a melange in support of HF IP and its collapse in AA. 5 MECHANISMS OF HF IP It is very likely that multiple mechanisms, similar to those employed by other IP tissues (see Ref. [1, 78-80]), are intimately involved in maintaining HF IP. Evidence from both the murine and the human system has established that HFs show at least two distinct areas of relative IP; the anagen hair bulb,29, 31, 32, 81 and the bulge region (Figure 1).2, 30, 81-83 These two distinct compartments of the HF both show a prominent reduction of expression for MHC class I and β2-microglobulin and expression of other IP markers (Table 2).30 However, there are some differences in the expression of specific IP conferring mechanisms between the bulge and bulb. Only the bulge is characterized by the prominent expression of the “no danger” signal, CD200, while MHC class I and II expression is lower in the hair bulb as compared to the bulge.30, 81 It has also been speculated that the special extracellular matrix composition of the HF mesenchyme, and specifically the expression of select glycosaminoglycans in the lower CTS, may contribute to HF bulb IP by impeding T cell trafficking in and out of the HF epithelium.1, 84 Further investigation is needed to fully understand the mechanisms of IP in different regions of the HF structure. [36] Vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP) + ↓ [98] 5.1 The role of MHC molecules in hair bulb IP MHC class I molecules are typically found on the cell surface of all nucleated cells. Physiologically, their role is to present peptide fragments from within the cell to CD8+ lymphocytes.85 If the antigen epitopes presented are recognized by lymphocytes with the relevant TCR specificity, there is a rapid activation of the cells leading to production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and apoptosis promoting factors including granzymes and perforin.86 Natural killer (NK) cells are primed to recognize and attack MHC class I–negative cells.87 In immunologically privileged tissues and malignant cells, this mechanism tends to be circumvented in part by very low or absent expression of ligands, such as MICA, and ULBP3/6, that stimulate the NK cell-activating receptor, NKG2D.88-93 In contrast, MHC class II molecules are physiologically only found on professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells, macrophages, and Langerhans cells. APCs use MHC class II to present phagocytosed and processed antigen epitopes to CD4+ lymphocytes.94 If epitopes are recognized by CD4+ T cells with cognate TCRs, activation ensues with rapid increase in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines that classically support the activities of CD8+ T cells and/or B cells.94, 95 However, MHC class II expression can also be induced on other cells,96 including keratinocytes, by IFNγ.97-99 Keratinocytes expressing MHC class II can successfully present antigen epitopes to CD4+ T cells,100, 101 although it is unclear at what level of efficacy this actually occurs in human skin in vivo. Of great significance for understanding AA susceptibility, subtle changes to MHC structure, encoded by specific gene alleles, can determine increased or reduced presentation of specific antigen epitopes.102 Consequently, specific alleles for MHC class II, and to a lesser extent MHC class I, can be strongly correlated to various autoimmune diseases, and AA is no exception.35, 91, 103-106 While conflicting evidence has been published on the HLA associations of AA in different populations, large-scale genome wide association studies implicate HLA-DR as a key aetiological driver of AA,103 consistent with an important supporting role for MHC class II mediated antigen presentation to CD4+ T cells. Polymorphisms in HLA-A,104 HLA-B (along with MICA)107 and HLA-C105 are also linked to AA risk. While it is as yet unclear what exactly these HLA associations mean for the functionality of HF IP, it is reasonable to assume that the corresponding variations in MHC structure significantly influence the strength at which (auto-)antigens are presented by HF keratinocytes via MHC class I to autoreactive CD8+ T cells, or by HF-associated APCs via MHC class II molecules to autoreactive CD4+ T cells, that have escaped (HF-dependent?) peripheral tolerance induction.52 Subtle, genetically coded changes in MHC structure and function could thus greatly increase the risk of an anti-HF autoimmune attack in the event of HF IP collapse (see below). Specific HLA-DR polymorphisms may also confer decreased risk of AA.108 For example, it has been proposed that HLA-Dw52a may confer relative AA protection.109 Given the key roles of MHC class I and II in activation of the adaptive immune system, a very effective method of conferring IP in any tissue is to avoid MHC presentation of antigen epitopes. Not surprisingly, malignant cells exploit this immune escape principle by actively downregulating the presence of MHC on their cell surface.110 Several IP tissues exhibit a relative lack of MHC expression.12, 111-113 Similarly, normal human and rodent anagen VI HF bulbs are relatively deficient in both MHC class I and II as well as β2-microglobulin (Figure 1).37 This lack of MHC expression breaks down during the development of AA and both MHC I and II become strongly and ectopically expressed by the lower HF epithelium.46, 84, 114 In contrast, non-classical MHC molecules have been shown to be expressed by normal HFs in mice or human tissues in a hair cycle-dependent manner (Table 2).31, 32, 36 Some non-classical MHC molecules, such as HLA-E and HLA-G, can downregulate immune responses.115 Whether these MHC immune regulators are functionally involved in HF IP is currently unknown. Occasional intraepithelial MHC class II+ Langerhans cell can be spotted in close proximity to the bulge or isthmus in murine and human HF epithelium. However, the very few CD1a+ or ultrastructurally detectable Langerhans cells that are present in human anagen hair bulbs do not express MHC class II.30, 36, 43, 81 This suggests that they are unable to present antigens to cognate CD4+ T cells and thus cannot function as professional APCs in anagen HFs. Moreover, in conjunction with the immunoinhibitory mechanisms described below, this renders it likely that autoantigen epitopes which are presented via any residual low-level MHC expression in anagen hair bulbs would induce peripheral tolerance.1, 31 5.2 Cell expressed molecules as “IP guardians” Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is expressed in several tissues and its activity is associated with IP status.116-118 PD-L1 delivers an inhibitory signal through PD-1 present on T cells to induce cell anergy.119, 120 High expression of PD-L1 in placenta contributes to fetomaternal tolerance while PD-L1 deficiency reduces foetus survival.117, 121, 122 PD-L1 is present in proximal HFs on keratinocytes, connective tissue sheath (CTS) and dermal papilla (DP) cells (Figure 1)37. Cultured CTS and DP cells strongly expressed PD-L1, which was involved in suppression of immune responses in co-culture assays; knockdown of PD-L1 or use of blocking antibodies partly removed the immune-suppression and allowed allogeneic peripheral blood mononuclear cells to respond to CTS and DP cells.37 Therapy with PD-L1 has been demonstrated to be effective in experimental psoriasis and vitiligo.123 In contrast, PD-1 or PD-L1 blocking antibody treatment in clinical trials for cancer can trigger vitiligo,124 and AA-like hair loss.125, 126 The cytosolic enzyme, indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), is expressed by HF bulge cells,30 and may also be expressed at a lower level elsewhere in the HF.34, 127 IDO catabolizes tryptophan which is critical for T-cell activity.30 IDO expression in HFs is expected to impede any local autoaggressive T-cell activities, thus mirroring the situation in eye and fetomaternal IP.78, 80 In addition, IDO-expressing cells can increase the number of Treg cells since co-culturing immune cells with IDO-expressing fibroblasts significantly increases the number of CD25+ FOXP3+ Tregs.128 Protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 22 (PTPN22) inhibits T-cell activation by restricting signalling downstream of T-cell receptor (TCR) activation.129 Specific PTPN22 polymorphisms are associated with several autoimmune diseases including rheumatoid arthritis, type I diabetes and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).130, 131 Variants of PTPN22 have been identified as a general risk factor in AA with a particularly strong effect observed for patients with more severe AA presentations.132, 133 However, nothing is known in terms of the mechanistic significance of PTPN22 in HF IP and AA pathogenesis. 5.3 The role of death receptors and ligands in hair bulb IP HFs may also have the ability to block lymphocyte activity through the Fas-Fas ligand (FasL) system, as identified in eye graft models.134-136 In fact, mice with mutations in Fas or FasL are largely resistant to the development of AA: When skin from mice with non-functional Fas or FasL are grafted to AA-affected mice, the grafted skin remains resistant to AA and hair continues to grow from the grafted skin.136 Also of note, inhibition of Fas/FasL-mediated apoptotic signalling by the main component of Astragalus membranaceus, a medicinal plant with anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, anti-viral, and anti-apoptotic properties, improved hair growth in a mouse model of non-inflammatory hair loss.137 This raises the as yet unanswered question whether Fas-FasL signalling plays a role in the pathogenesis of human AA. Fas-FasL interaction in AA could be important for regulation of pro-inflammatory cells and/or protection of HF cells from apoptosis. TNFα can promote apoptosis in receptor (TNFR1)–expressing cells.138 TNFα appears to be required for normal catagen development,139 the apoptosis-driven HF involution stage of the hair cycle.140 Classically, TNFα is produced by lymphocytes and dendritic cells and this promotes apoptosis in non-immune system cells expressing TNFR1 and also increases MHC expression.141, 142 AA has been treated successfully with TNFα antagonist adalimumab.143 However, while TNFα stimulation promotes keratinocyte apoptosis in the murine anagen hair bulb,144 it does not induce HF IP collapse, in striking contrast to IFNγ.32, 76 Further, there are several case reports of TNFα-antagonist drugs inducing AA.145-147 The question arises, therefore, whether TNFα promotes AA or might confer relative immune resistance to AA? While TNFα can drive cell apoptosis, and induce a dysregulated type I interferon response,148 it can also promote select immunoregulatory activities. For example, TNFα can induce the expression of IDO in corneal tissue and has a synergistic effect on IDO promotion when combined with IFNγ.149 In the presence of TGFβ, TNFα can promote a tolerogenic state in APCs,150, 151 possibly by suppressing IL-12,152, 153 which in turn promotes Treg cells.154 TNFα can induce programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression on mast cells.155 In an experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mouse model, TNFα deficiency exacerbates the disease,156 and there are case reports of TNFα antagonist induced demyelination and exacerbation of multiple sclerosis.157, 158 Whether a pro-apoptotic or anti-inflammatory role is dominant in AA is likely determined by the status of other pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, the expression of Fas-FasL, and/or the expression of TNFR1 versus TNFR2.138, 141, 142, 159 Other TNF family death receptors and ligands, such as TRAIL-TRAILR1/2, TWEAK-Fn14 and death receptor decoys TRAIL-R3 and TRAIL-R4,138 have not been investigated in AA in any detail. Given these receptor-ligand interactions are all part of the same TNF superfamily, pleiotropic function might also be anticipated.138, 141, 142, 159 Of particular note, TRAIL may play a significant role in maintaining tolerance to eye antigens.160 Recently, increased expression of TWEAK was identified in AA patients, but whether it confers resistance or susceptibility to AA is unknown.161, 162 5.4 The role of secreted factors as “HF IP guardians” The first line of HF protection from undesired and potentially deleterious immune responses likely involves secreted factors that create an immunoinhibitory cocoon around every healthy HF, beginning at the level of the bulge (Figure 1). Several potent immune suppressors, such as αMSH, TGFβ1, TGFβ2, cortisol and IL-10, are generated by the epithelium of healthy HFs.76, 84, 163-165 Together, under physiological conditions, these factors may allow HFs to regulate immune cells in the surrounding vicinity of the HFs and dampen their pro-inflammatory activities well in advance of any contact with HF tissues. The functional importance of some of these secreted HF IP guardians as protectors against and as restorers of IFNγ-induced HF IP collapse, such as αMSH, TGFβ and IL-10 and IGF-1, has been documented in organ-cultured human HFs and skin.76, 166 The IP guardians TGFβ1167 and TGFβ230, 168 are expressed in the HF outer root sheath (Figure 1). The expression of these factors may prevent the attack of NK and CD8+ T cells in tissue characterized by downregulated MHC class I.168, 169 Further, TGFβ2 can help convert effector T cells into Treg cells.170 An apparent deficiency in cytokine Red/IK is present in human HFs in advance of overt hair loss.34 Red/IK downregulates the expression of MHC molecules and interferes with immune cell activation.171, 172 Macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) is produced by HFs and may contribute to IP.88, 173 It is known to inhibit innate immune cell migration, and it is a potent inhibitor of NK cell function.30, 88, 173-175 Further, thrombospondin 1 (TSP1), a secreted glycoprotein that can mediate immunoregulation,176 may also be expressed by HFs.177, 178 There are likely other immunoinhibitory factors secreted by HFs that remain as yet unidentified, but together with those mentioned above, the immunoinhibitory functions of perifollicular mast cells (see below),179 and perhaps also tolerogenic dendritic cells as seen in mouse studies,180 they create an immunosuppressive, tolerance-promoting signalling milieu in and immediately around the lower HF. It is as yet very poorly understood though, which genes control this special immune environment and regulate its restoration whenever exposure to an abundance of pro-inflammatory signals/stressors threatens its collapse. 5.5 AIRE function in HF IP One of these as yet unknown regulatory, HF IP-protective genes could be the tolerance-promoting autoimmune regulator gene (AIRE), which may be defective in some AA patients. AIRE controls the expression of tissue specific antigens in the thymus.181 This helps to eliminate autoreactive T cells, induces regulatory T-cell (Treg) production and may even encourage some autoreactive T cells to directly differentiate into Treg cells.182 AIRE expression by dendritic cells in the periphery likely promotes tolerization of autoreactive cells.181, 183 AIRE is also expressed in HF keratinocytes and closely associates with keratin 17.184 While its significance remains unclear, AIRE in keratinocytes may regulate keratin 17–dependent pro-inflammatory gene expression.185 A loss-of-function mutation in AIRE interferes with autoreactive lymphocyte deletion and impairs the regulatory function of Treg cells.186 AIRE mutations also skew CD8+ T cells towards an effector cytotoxic phenotype.187 Mutations of the AIRE gene cause the monogenic autoimmune disease, polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy (APECED),188 and notably, there is an unusually high frequency of AA in APECED patients.189 Specific AIRE polymorphisms have been identified in association with vitiligo190 and AA,189, 191, 192 though results are conflicting.193 Identifying a significant role for AIRE has rekindled interest in the role that Tregs play in the maintenance of physiological HF IP, as opposed to defective Treg functions in AA.194-198 Owing to this progress in research, the original HF IP scenario, and how its failure predisposes and can lead to AA, has become much more complex (see Figure 2) (for discussion, see4 incl. supplement). Human HF organ culture-based evidence suggests that this includes calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP), a key immunoinhibitory neuropeptide that is often co-expressed with pro-inflammatory substance P in sensory nerve fibres of murine and human skin.200, 201 CGRP significantly reduces IFNγ-induced degranulation of perifollicular mast cells.99 That CGRP is indeed a HF IP guardian has been confirmed by independent investigators.202 Also of note, CGRP suppresses nitric oxide (NO) activity in eye IP.203 More recent evidence suggests that vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), a central IP guardian in the anterior eye chamber,78, 79 also contributes to human HF IP. VIP is expressed in, and released from, perifollicular sensory nerve fibres98 that insert into the richly innervated, VIP receptor-positive HF epithelium200, 204 in human skin, but does not appear to be expressed by the HF epithelium itself. Further, previous research demonstrated that the neurohormone somatostatin was strongly expressed in human HF epithelium and had a significant immunosuppressive effect using allogeneic co-culture cell assays.36 Somatostatin suppresses T cell activity,205-207 inhibits expression of activation markers and proliferation of T cells208 and decreases IFNγ release by leucocytes.209, 210 Together with the previous demonstration that substance P is the second-strongest HF IP collapse inducer after IFNγ reported in human HFs so far,77 it is conceivable that sensory skin nerve–derived neuropeptides have a profound impact on HF IP, primarily in the bulge region, but also to a lesser extent in the innervated HF bulb. It has already been shown that the stress-associated neuropeptide, substance P, plays a key role in the induction of perifollicular inflammatory cell infiltrates and murine HF IP breakdown under conditions of neurogenic skin inflammation induced by perceived stress.211, 212 Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) functions as the up-stream regulator of neuroendocrine stress responses.213 It controls adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and cortisol production in human scalp HFs,163, 164 which display a fully functional peripheral equivalent of the central hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.163 As discussed in-depth elsewhere,164 even though many details are still obscure, the regulated neuroendocrine activity of the HPA axis equivalent in human HFs affects HF IP on several levels. Specifically, CRH, ACTH and β-endorphin activate mast cells present in the CTS, while the potent immunoinhibitors, αMSH and cortisol, can act as HF IP guardians.164 During anagen, the epithelium of human scalp HFs strongly expresses ACTH and stimulates intra-follicular cortisol generation and subsequent immunosuppressive activity.163 αMSH, derived from the cleavage of pro-opiomelanocortin polypeptide,30, 214, 215 is strongly expressed in HFs.76, 165, 216, 217 Expression of αMSH can suppress the activities of IL-1 and TNFα, and blocks endotoxin activation via toll like receptor-4.218, 219 5.7 Support of hair follicle immune privilege by CD4+ regulatory T cells Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are a subpopulation of lymphocytes which skew immune responses towards peripheral tolerance10, 24-26, 220 and are involved in preventing autoimmunity.221, 222 Tregs come in several forms with the most well-understood being those that are CD4+, CD25+ and Foxp3+ (natural Tregs).223 Tregs manifest their regulatory function through several mechanisms including cell-cell contact regulation via TCR, and other molecules such as cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), GITR and LAG-3.224 They also promote immunosuppression in their vicinity via secretion of immunomodulatory soluble factors such as IL-9, IL-10, TGFβ and IL-35.225-228 Mouse studies demonstrate that Treg suppression mediated via cytokines is of particular importance at environmental interfaces, such as the gut, lungs and skin.225, 229, 230 In autoimmune diseases, a lower frequency of Tregs is often observed in the active disease state.231, 232 Unsurprisingly, a genetic deficiency in Treg production significantly increases the risk of autoimmune disease development.233 Treg cells require maintenance signals from their local environment. Among these signals, TGFβ appears to have a crucial role in Foxp3 maintenance of Treg cells and the ability of Treg cells to induce non-Tregs to develop regulatory properties, a phenomenon known as infectious tolerance.234, 235 HFs likely promote Treg cells in their immediate vicinity. Foxp3 transcription can be induced by several pathways: first by TGFβ signalling through induction of SMAD3 and suppression of extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs); second by IL-2, IL-7 and/or IL-15 expression (which maintains Foxp3 expression when in the presence of TGFβ), signalling through the intracellular signalling molecule, signal transducers and activators of transcription TCR T cell receptor (STAT) 5, which binds the Foxp3 promoter; and third through the TCR and nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT).236-238 IL-7, IL-15 and TGFβ are all expressed by HF epithelium.30, 167, 168, 179, 239 Overall, Tregs have a significant role to play in HF IP maintenance within the skin in close proximity to HFs and also may confer protection to HFs in other body locations where antigen presentation to T cells is active. 5.8 Perifollicular mast cells may exert immunoinhibitory functions The potential contribution of perifollicular mast cells to HF IP remains to be fully dissected. Mast cells have been shown to exert hair growth regulatory activities in mice.1, 56, 65 However, immunohistological evidence suggests that perifollicular mast cells are a key innate immunocyte population in the HF mesenchyme and may help to maintain HF IP under physiological circumstances. In the connective tissue sheath of healthy human scalp HFs, these perifollicular mast cells display an immunoinhibitory phenotype characterized by high expression levels of TGFβ1, IL-10 and PD-L1, which switches to a profoundly pro-inflammatory phenotype when perifollicular mast cells are associated with lesional AA HFs.179 In recent years, mast cells have emerged as important contributors in the induction of tolerance or immunosuppression. They have been shown to promote tolerance through induction of Treg cells and the production of IL-9 in a mouse model of skin allografts.241-244 In an in vitro co-culture system, murine mast cells have been shown to increase the percentage of CD4+, CD25+ and FoxP3+ regulatory T cells in a TGF-β1-dependent manner.244 In the skin, mast cells limit inflammatory reactions by the production of IL-10.245 In naïve bone-marrow derived mast cells, we could show that the expression of PD-L1 was high and constitutive,246 further supporting that in homeostatic conditions mast cells preferentially display an immunoinhibitory phenotype. Interestingly, an inhibitory function of mast cells has also been observed in cancer. They increase with tumor progression and negatively correlate with patient survival, in part because tumor-infiltrating mast cells may suppress normal T-cell immunity through the upregulation of PD-L1.155 5.9 Other cells with a potential role in HF IP It is expected that further dissection of HF IP will reveal that, besides Tregs, other perifollicular immunocyte subpopulations will turn out to contribute to HF IP maintenance such as inhibitory NK cells,247 immunoregulatory CD8+ T cells248 and tolerogenic dendritic cells.180 Throughout the ongoing exploration of HF IP in rodents and humans, prior progress in understanding fetomaternal and ocular IP has provided key pointers and theoretical concepts (reviewed in Ref. [25, 78, 111, 249, 250]). Recently, however, we have argued that these classical IP models, particularly ocular IP, may also profit greatly from investigating human HF IP, since the latter is so much more accessible and tractable than the human eye.79 This exploratory journey continues today, and we may still have only scratched the surface of what is biologically fascinating and clinically important about HF IP. 6 COLLAPSE OF HF IMMUNE PRIVILEGE Billingham's visionary HF IP concept22 did not gain much traction among immunologists or hair researchers, even after Westgate and colleagues re-emphasized this concept.84 This motivated us and others to systematically characterize the immunology of mouse and human HFs, which—until then—had largely been terra incognita on the map of cutaneous immunobiology and immunodermatology (reviewed in Ref. [1]). Yet, Westgate's hypothesis that IP collapse is somehow important for catagen development84 remains unsupported and may be misleading. Currently, there is no evidence that catagen development requires HF IP collapse, and the postulated switch of the previously MHC class I-negative HF epithelium to an MHC I-positive state during catagen does not really occur: instead, a pseudo-switch in MHC Ia expression patterns is mimicked by the rapid disappearance of MHC I-negative HF keratinocytes during catagen31 due to massive keratinocyte apoptosis.140 However, Westgate's timely resuscitation of Billingham's HF IP concept encouraged us to formulate the hypothesis that the collapse of HF IP lies at the heart of AA pathogenesis (Table 2).43 Antigenic challenge or activated lymphocyte cell transfer can induce autoimmune disease against most IP sites including the testes,251, 252 cornea253 and pregnant uterus.254 HF IP may also be broken by activating the immune system against HF autoantigens through active immunization, as suggested by some induction models of AA,45, 255-257 or transgenic promotion of HF antigen-specific TCRs.39 More significantly, passive transfer of lymphocytes or plasmacytoid dendritic cells from a mouse with AA to a naïve recipient lead to onset of localized AA, later leading to a more extensive hair loss.258, 259 These observations indicate that passive sequestration of antigens behind basement membrane barriers and absence of MHC class I and II is not enough to fully explain the manifestations of HF IP. Secreted immunoregulatory factors that actively suppress the skin immune system are likely also significant in conferring IP. If so, then deficiencies in one or more of these secretory factors may leave an individual more susceptible to the onset of HF autoimmunity (Table 2). This concept has now become widely accepted as an important, if not critical element in the pathobiology of AA.4, 33-37, 42, 75, 260, 261 7 ALOPECIA AREATA Through its long history, a link between AA and inflammation has been well recognized.262 It presents with a wide array of clinical and aetiological variations,263, 264 but in all instances it is a non-scarring alopecia.265, 266 Histopathological examination reveals anagen stage HFs affected by a peri- and intra-follicular inflammatory cell infiltrates primarily comprised of CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells,267 as well as antigen-presenting cells (APCs),259, 268 mast cells,179 NK cells88 and sometimes eosinophils.269 While CD8+ T cells readily penetrate follicular regions, CD4+ T cells primarily reside in perifollicular locations in AA.267, 270, 271 Paus and colleagues have recently postulated that AA does not necessarily represent a single disease entity, but rather it encompasses a stereotypic HF response pattern.6, 272 When confronted with a defined, IFNγ-dependent pro-inflammatory signalling milieu, even perfectly healthy anagen HFs in genetically non-predisposed individuals are at risk to undergo HF IP collapse and subsequent HF dystrophy as well as premature catagen development, which can progress to the AA phenotype. According to this new paradigm, “autoimmune AA [AAA]” only occurs in a subset of AA patients on the basis of a genuine, HF autoantigen-dependent autoimmune response pattern involving autoreactive CD8+ T cells (see Table 3, 1a-c).6 No matter how this stereotypic HF response pattern is initiated, it cannot progress to the pathognomonic clinical features we diagnose as AA (ie focal or widespread hair loss in clinically healthy-appearing skin, cadaver and exclamation mark hairs, with regrowth of white hair shafts),4 without several events co-occurring (Table 3). On the other hand, Tregs are characterized by their plasticity and functional instability under inflammatory conditions.314 It has been observed that in response to inflammatory conditions, Tregs can be converted to pro-inflammatory-helper cells and thus may serve as essential contributors to long-lasting inflammatory diseases.315 Decreased stability of Foxp3 expression and increased proportions of Th1-like IFNγ-expressing cells have been shown to promote inflammation in autoimmune diseases, such as type 1 diabetes, juvenile arthritis and multiple sclerosis.316, 317 In addition, studies demonstrate that the pathological conversion of Tregs contributes to the loss of corneal IP.318 A possible role for unstable Tregs in AA pathogenesis has not been studied. However, the apparent plasticity of Tregs does not undermine their importance in the maintenance of IP, which is widely reported by the literature.10 It should be emphasized that Treg cells are not the only cells to regulate immune responses.319 Previously, our group described regulatory NK and NKT functions in AA mouse models.247, 277 A role for these cells has also been described in various autoimmune diseases,320-322 including rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis.323 The literature reveals that there are different NK and NKT subsets including regulatory ones.324, 325 Collectively, these studies indicate that multiple cell types may have roles in AA aetiology by breaking the delicate balance between scalp skin-resident memory T cells versus immune cells with regulatory effects.44 9 THERAPEUTIC PERSPECTIVES Since failure of IP mechanisms enables AA development, it is logical to anticipate that restoring and/or supplementing IP signalling is a highly effective way to treat AA and prevent future relapse. Exogenous factors could modify the nature and intensity of IP and its breakdown in AA including the HF and gut microbiomes,326 the bioavailability of energy for immune cell activity327 and atopic responses to environmental allergens,264, 328 for example. As such, targeting these external factors with treatment may facilitate IP regeneration in AA-affected HFs. There is also some evidence that current therapies indirectly facilitate HF IP restoration. For example, the potent IP guardian, αMSH, increases when AA is treated with UVA-1 phototherapy.329 Diphencyprone (DCP) treatment normalizes MHC expression in HFs and promotes IL-10 and TGFβ1 expression.330-332 However, more direct approaches would likely achieve greater improvements in IP to prevent and reverse AA. Several neuropharmacological drugs333 and other treatments are in development or currently available that hold potential for regenerating HF IP (Table 4), though these drugs are largely untested for use in treating AA. For example, the immunosuppressive drug FK506 (tacrolimus)334, 335 downregulates MHC class I expression and has been shown to protect HFs from IFNγ-induced IP collapse in ex vivo studies.76 Despite some success in rodent models,336, 337 clinical trials with topical FK506 have been unsuccessful, probably due to poor skin penetration.338-340 However, in a case report systemic FK506 produced a hair growth response.341 Special formulations may be needed to improve transcutaneous drug penetration, or perhaps intradermal injection can be used, as for corticosteroids,342, 343 or microneedling.344 Beyond repurposing and reformulating drugs developed for other diseases, the process of HF IP collapse presents multiple key events that could be targeted with more specific therapeutic interventions (Table 5). Much more systematic research and development is needed to provide clinicians with better, practically useful therapeutic options in AA management beyond corticosteroids, DCP and JAK inhibitors. Rather, AA may represent a stereotypic HF response pattern to a number of different immunopathological events that any normal HF, irrespective of its “genetic make-up,” can develop. According to this new hypothesis, distinct immunopathological starting events coalesce in the collapse of HF IP and result in the clinical AA phenotype (Figure 3), if certain conditions are met (Table 3). The basic tenets of the original HF IP collapse theory and its role in AA pathogenesis43 remain largely unchanged, and this pathogenesis scenario has proven to be more plausible and better-supported by published evidence than any other (for debate, see Ref. [33]). The role of MHC molecules, immunoregulatory cytokines, co-stimulatory molecules and Tregs in HF IP maintenance, collapse and restoration receives ever-increasing attention.33, 35, 42, 194, 197, 198, 294, 295, 297 Furthermore, perifollicular cells, particularly mast cells, are finally recognized as potentially important players in HF IP in health and inflammatory HF diseases.38, 179 Looking back at the history of HF IP research, the latter has become inseparable from AA research, and AA has turned out to be a uniquely instructive model disease in which the clinical consequences of a defective IP of the anagen HF bulb can be interrogated. New principles of how HF IP is generated and maintained and how it interacts with the surrounding skin immune system have been, and continue to be, characterized. New discoveries in IP derived from other tissues continue to instruct AA research and understanding, but equally, research on IP collapse in AA can inform other inflammatory diseases. According to the IP collapse concept of AA pathobiology, whenever hair shafts regrow spontaneously HF IP must have been successfully restituted.
